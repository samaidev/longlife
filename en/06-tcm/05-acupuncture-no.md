# Acupuncture, Acupoints, and NO: Mechanisms and Evidence for Acupuncture’s Regulation of the Nitric Oxide Pathway

> Acupuncture treats illness by stimulating specific points on the body’s surface (acupoints) with fine needles and has a clinical history of more than two millennia. Modern research reveals that many of its effects—local vasodilation, anti-inflammatory action, analgesia, and blood-pressure reduction—are closely coupled to nitric oxide (NO) signaling. This chapter reviews how needling activates nitric-oxide synthases through somatosensory and humoral circuits, how electroacupuncture (EA) drives vagal/sympathetic anti-inflammatory reflexes, and the evidence grades and controversies around the “acupuncture–NO–anti-inflammation/aging” chain. Acupuncture may be viewed as a non-pharmacological, quantifiable “whole-body NO-bioavailability modulator,” alongside exercise and dietary nitrate as one of three major non-drug NO interventions (for NO biology, see 01-foundations/03-no-basics.md).

---

## Core concepts

Acupuncture is a surface-stimulation therapy guided by TCM theories of meridians and acupoints. Meridians are channels through which qi and blood circulate; acupoints are sites where “meridian qi” enters and leaves the body. Fine-needle lifting/thrusting and twirling stimulate “de qi”—the sensations of soreness, numbness, distension, and heaviness—or a device can deliver quantified electrical stimulation (electroacupuncture, EA). Modern mechanistic research reframes acupuncture as an activator of “somato-visceral reflexes”: needling is a controllable somatosensory stimulus that activates Aβ, Aδ, and C afferent fibers, ascends through the dorsal horn to autonomic nuclei in the brainstem, and then modulates visceral function through vagal and sympathetic efferents. At the same time, mechanical stretch and local micro-injury from needling directly activate eNOS/nNOS in skin and muscle, releasing NO and dilating local microvessels. For aging research, acupuncture is of interest because it can physically activate both the “vascular NO axis” and the “vagal anti-inflammatory axis,” corresponding to two longevity-network nodes: maintenance of vascular endothelial function and suppression of chronic inflammation (inflammaging; see the 12 hallmarks in 01-foundations/01-aging-hallmarks.md).

## Acupuncture basics: meridians, acupoints, and needling techniques

**Meridian theory:** TCM holds that the body has twelve regular meridians and eight extraordinary vessels that form a network for qi and blood circulation. The *Lingshu: Meridians* systematically describes their courses and indications. No definitive anatomical counterpart for meridians has been established, but studies suggest spatial overlap with fascial connective-tissue planes, peripheral neurovascular bundles, and lymphatic routes (Langevin 2002). A modern interpretation tends to regard a meridian as a “functionally integrated band of several tissues (nerve, vessel, fascia, lymphatics) along a fixed trajectory,” rather than a separate anatomical structure. Because of its diffusibility and vascular coupling, NO has been proposed as one candidate molecular mediator of “propagated sensation along the channel.”

**Acupoints:** Acupoints are sites where meridian qi enters, exits, or gathers. They include standard meridian points, extra points, and Ashi points (tender points). Common points have functional locations: Zusanli (ST36, stomach meridian) is associated with gastrointestinal function and general strengthening and is called a “longevity point”; Neiguan (PC6, pericardium meridian) is used for chest/heart symptoms and nausea/vomiting; Hegu (LI4, large-intestine meridian) for the head/face and analgesia; Sanyinjiao (SP6) is a major gynecological point. Anatomically, many acupoints lie near nerve trunks/branches, perforating vessels, and intermuscular spaces. For example, the deep region of ST36 lies near a branch of the deep peroneal nerve, while PC6 lies between the median nerve and palmaris longus tendon. This supports the morphological idea that “acupoints are sites of high neurovascular density” and may explain why needling there readily induces neural reflexes and NO release.

**Needling techniques:** The central goal of fine-needle technique is “de qi,” a sensation of soreness, numbness, distension, or heaviness under the needle, sometimes spreading locally or distally. Traditional practitioners consider “qi arriving” necessary for efficacy. Standard methods are **lifting and thrusting** (moving the needle vertically) and **twirling** (rotating it). Modern studies indicate that twirling creates the strongest connective-tissue winding and mechanical traction, making it effective at triggering local mechanochemical signaling (fibroblast calcium waves and adenosine release). **Electroacupuncture (EA)** substitutes pulsed current for manual manipulation, with quantifiable parameters (frequency 2–100 Hz, intensity 0.5–5 mA, continuous/dense-sparse/intermittent waveform), helping research overcome the difficulty of reproducing manual technique. Most modern mechanistic studies use EA because its “dose” can be programmed. **Auricular acupuncture** and **acupressure/embedding (intradermal needles)** are lower-intensity variants. Stimulation of the concha, where the auricular branch of the vagus nerve (ABVN) is distributed, is viewed as a natural platform for non-invasive transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation (taVNS), directly linking to neuroimmune-axis regulation (see 03-immunity/06-neuro-immune-axis.md).

## The molecular mechanism chain

### 1. The physical–neural–chemical cascade: from needle tip to molecule

**Mechanical signaling is the first level.** Langevin’s work (Langevin & Yandow 2002, *Anat Rec*) showed that acupoints are not anatomically independent structures but strongly overlap with connective-tissue planes. During needle rotation, the needle pulls collagen bundles, “winding” local connective tissue and transmitting mechanical deformation to fibroblasts several centimeters away. Fibroblasts respond to traction with calcium waves and release ATP and adenosine. Goldman 2010 (*Nat Neurosci*) directly measured increased adenosine in local microdialysate after needling; an adenosine A1-receptor agonist mimicked, and adenosine deaminase reversed, acupuncture’s mechanical analgesia—among the strongest lines of evidence for “local acupuncture chemistry.” Mechanical traction also affects microvascular endothelium: shear-like deformation around the needle activates PI3K-Akt through the PECAM-1/VE-cadherin/VEGFR2 complex and phosphorylates the activating eNOS site Ser1177. This is homologous to the molecular mechanism by which exercise-induced blood-flow shear stress activates eNOS (see the shear-stress section in 01-foundations/03-no-basics.md).

**Neural signaling is the second level.** Needling—especially twirling, lifting/thrusting, and EA—activates three classes of afferent fibers in skin and muscle: Aβ (touch), Aδ (fast pain/mechanical), and C fibers (polymodal nociceptors). Somatic afferents enter the dorsal horn through dorsal-root ganglia and undergo three levels of processing: (1) segmental gating—signals inhibit nociceptive ascending transmission through the substantia gelatinosa (gate-control theory of Melzack and Wall); (2) projections to the brainstem—activation of the periaqueductal gray (PAG)–rostral ventromedial medulla (RVM) descending inhibitory system, releasing serotonin (5-HT) and norepinephrine (NE) to suppress pain in the dorsal horn; and (3) projections to autonomic nuclei—the nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS), dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus (DMV), and intermediolateral cell column (IML), providing an anatomical basis for acupuncture’s effects on visceral function. The neurophysiological correlate of de qi is activation of deep-muscle afferents dominated by Aδ fibers; fMRI studies show characteristic activation of regions including the insula and anterior cingulate during de qi.

### 2. Local acupuncture→NO pathway (the core chain in acupuncture–NO research)

```
Needle twirling/lifting-thrusting/electroacupuncture
   │
   ├─ (1) Mechanical traction on connective tissue → fibroblast calcium waves → ATP/adenosine release
   ├─ (2) Microvascular endothelial shear deformation → PI3K/Akt → eNOS Ser1177 phosphorylation
   ├─ (3) Activation of sensory terminals (nNOS) and endothelium (eNOS)
   │
   ▼
Local NO release (local concentration in the μM range)
   │
   ├─ sGC/cGMP/PKG → vascular smooth-muscle relaxation → local blood flow ↑
   │     (laser Doppler flowmetry (LDF): local acupoint blood flow rises
   │      30–60% after needling and may remain elevated for 30–60 minutes)
   ├─ Inhibits platelet aggregation and leukocyte adhesion → improved microcirculation
   └─ Reacts with superoxide to form ONOO− (a double-edged risk at excessive concentrations)
```

**The series of studies by Ma Shu-Xiang** established the hypothesis of “high-NO zones at acupoints and meridians.” Ma 2003 (*J Altern Complement Med*) detected band-like high expression of NOS protein and NO metabolites in acupoint regions of rat skin and subcutaneous tissue, spatially overlapping with TCM meridian trajectories. Ma 2007 (*Nitric Oxide*) used NO-selective microelectrodes at the human skin surface and found significantly increased NO release after needling; baseline NO on meridian lines was higher than at non-meridian comparison sites. These findings suggest that some of the “high conductivity” attributed to meridians may involve NO-mediated synchronization of vascular and neural activity, although sample size and reproducibility remain disputed.

**Tsuchiya 2007 (*Anesth Analg*)** is a representative human acupuncture–NO study. After fine-needle acupuncture in healthy volunteers, chemiluminescence showed a significant increase in plasma NO metabolites (NOx), while laser Doppler flowmetry detected increased skin blood flow at the needled site. The temporal synchrony suggests that acupuncture-induced systemic NO release accompanies local vasodilation. Other human studies report increased nailfold microvascular flow velocity and fingertip temperature after needling LI4 or ST36. In animal studies, these effects are partially blocked by the non-selective NOS inhibitor L-NAME, supporting a mediator role for NO.

**Bidirectional regulation of eNOS/iNOS:** Acute acupuncture mainly activates constitutive eNOS/nNOS (Ca²⁺-dependent, transient, low-concentration, physiological NO for vasodilation). In chronic inflammation or tissue injury, however, local pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IFN-γ) can induce iNOS transcription through NF-κB/STAT1, generating sustained, high-concentration NO. Animal studies show that EA at ST36 increases eNOS expression/phosphorylation in infarcted myocardium or ischemic limbs (promoting protective angiogenesis), while suppressing excessive iNOS induction in sepsis models (avoiding ONOO− injury). Thus acupuncture may balance NOS isoforms by “increasing eNOS and curbing excessive iNOS,” molecularly resembling the TCM method of “tonifying qi and invigorating blood” (see other chapters in 06-tcm).

### 3. Frequency specificity of electroacupuncture: Han’s endorphin map

Classic work from the laboratory of Ji-Sheng Han (Han 2003, *Trends Neurosci*) established frequency-specific neuropeptide release: **low-frequency EA (2 Hz)** activates brain β-endorphin and enkephalin, acting at μ/δ opioid receptors for slower onset and longer-lasting effects; **high-frequency EA (100 Hz)** releases dynorphin, acting at κ opioid receptors for faster onset and shorter duration; dense-sparse 2/100 Hz stimulation recruits both peptide classes. This advanced acupuncture from an “empirical therapy” to “programmable neuropharmacology”: choosing frequency means choosing transmitters. NO has a dual role in this circuit. At low concentrations, spinal dorsal-horn NO via sGC/cGMP can enhance descending analgesia; at high concentrations in peripheral inflammation it sensitizes nociceptors (increasing CGRP/substance-P release and peripheral sensitization). The complete analgesic chain is needling→release of multiple transmitters (endorphins, 5-HT, NE, NO)→suppression of nociceptive transmission in the dorsal horn→closing the pain gate.

### 4. EA anti-inflammatory neural circuits: somatic stimulation→vagus/sympathetic nerves→spleen

This is among the most active areas of acupuncture-mechanism research over the past decade and directly connects to this knowledge base’s neuroimmune-axis chapter (see 03-immunity/06-neuro-immune-axis.md):

```
EA at ST36 (hindlimb) ── somatic Aβ/Aδ afferents ──▶ spinal cord ──▶ autonomic nuclei in brainstem
   │
   ├─▶ Vagal efferents (Torres-Rosas 2014, Nat Med)
   │     EA activates vagal output → spleen/adrenal gland → dopamine → D1 receptors on splenic macrophages
   │     → suppresses TNF-α synthesis (significantly improves survival in a sepsis-shock model)
   │
   └─▶ Sympathetic preganglionic neurons (Liu S 2020, Neuron)
         Hindlimb acupoint (ST36) → NPY⁺ sympathetic preganglionic neurons → adrenal gland → catecholamines → anti-inflammatory
         Abdominal acupoint (ST25) → NPY⁻ sympathetic preganglionic neurons → spleen → catecholamines → pro-inflammatory
         (Somatotopic specificity: stimulation site determines direction of immune regulation)
```

**Torres-Rosas 2014 (*Nat Med*):** In a mouse sepsis model, low-intensity, low-frequency EA at ST36 markedly lowered serum TNF-α and improved survival; the effect disappeared after vagotomy or splenectomy. The study proposed a spleen–dopamine–D1-receptor pathway, challenging the classic “splenic sympathetic–NE–β2AR” model (Rosas-Ballina 2011, *Science*; see the neuroimmune-axis chapter). **Liu 2020 (*Neuron*)** used viral tracing to map somatic–sympathetic topology: hindlimb (ST36) and abdominal (ST25) acupoints drove molecularly distinct sympathetic preganglionic neuronal populations (NPY⁺ promoting adrenal anti-inflammation vs NPY⁻ promoting splenic pro-inflammation). This suggests “acupoint selection is pathway-switch selection.” Somatotopic organization is a neuroanatomical basis for acupoint specificity and one of the most notable original findings in current acupuncture-mechanism research.

In humans, dozens of acupuncture/EA clinical trials report lower circulating TNF-α, IL-6, or CRP, though most are small and heterogeneous. Mechanistically they point to a reflex arc of “somatic stimulation→autonomic nerves→spleen/immune organs.” Another NO-related anti-inflammatory pathway is that vagal efferent activation may increase eNOS/NO in the spleen and inflamed tissues (the NO component of the vagal anti-inflammatory pathway; see “Coupling between NO and vagal anti-inflammatory signaling” in the neuroimmune-axis chapter), forming a third proposed link: “acupuncture→vagus→NO→NF-κB inhibition.”

### 5. Acupuncture for blood-pressure reduction: dual NO-mediated endothelial and central mechanisms

A series of studies from Longhurst’s laboratory (Zhou W, Tjen-A-Looi SC, Li P) show that EA at PC6 and ST36 activates somatic afferents to GABAergic/opioidergic pathways in the rostral/caudal ventrolateral medulla (rVLM/cVLM), suppressing sympathetic output and lowering peripheral resistance. At the same time, acupuncture increases endothelial eNOS/NO, directly dilating resistance vessels. The combined pathways form a “central sympathetic inhibition + peripheral NO vasodilation” two-arm structure. This partly overlaps with mechanisms of exercise and nitrate-mediated blood-pressure reduction and may explain why acupuncture’s effect is modest (roughly 4–6 mmHg systolic) but has been reproduced in several RCTs.

## Details of clinical and experimental evidence

**Chronic pain: true acupuncture vs sham—the dividing line in evidence strength.** Vickers 2012 (*Arch Intern Med*) pooled individual data from 29 RCTs, totaling 17,922 people with chronic pain (back/neck pain, osteoarthritis, chronic headache). Real acupuncture improved pain more than sham (needling at non-acupoints or superficial needling), with an effect of about 0.15–0.30 standard deviations (SD), persisting at 12-month follow-up. Sham acupuncture itself was substantially better than no treatment (about 0.4–0.5 SD). The 2018 update (*J Pain*, 39 trials, 20,827 participants) reached a similar conclusion: the advantage of real acupuncture over sham was only a fraction of an SD—“acupuncture works, but the specific component accounts for only a small part of the benefit; much comes from placebo/expectation/natural history.” Hinman 2014 (*JAMA*, n=282, knee osteoarthritis) found no difference between real acupuncture and superficial non-acupoint sham in pain or function. Divergent conclusions among large trials are central to the debate over acupuncture specificity.

**Migraine and headache: sham also performs well in large RCTs.** Linde 2005 (*JAMA*, German GERAC trial, n=302 with migraine) and a 2005 tension-type-headache trial found that both real and sham acupuncture substantially improved headache frequency compared with usual care, but did not differ significantly from each other. Thus acupuncture—real or sham—outperformed no acupuncture, while acupoint specificity was not established for headache indications. The 2016 Cochrane review of headache by the Linde team reached a similar conclusion: acupuncture can be a preventive option, but evidence of superiority over conventional preventive drugs is limited.

**Postoperative/chemotherapy-related nausea and vomiting: PC6 is the best-established point.** Ezzo 2006 (Cochrane) included 40 RCTs and found that PC6 electrostimulation/acupuncture significantly reduced acute chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting (relative risk reduced by about 25–30%), making this one of acupuncture’s best-supported indications. Meta-analyses of postoperative nausea/vomiting (Lee 1999, *Anesth Analg*) support PC6 stimulation as an adjunct to antiemetics. The WHO’s 2003 report *Acupuncture: Review and Analysis of Reports on Controlled Clinical Trials* listed nausea/vomiting as “proven effective” and included more than twenty other conditions with supporting evidence, including back pain, migraine, and knee osteoarthritis.

**Acupuncture for hypertension: one of the few “mechanism-to-clinic” loops.** Flachskampf 2007 (*Circulation*, German RCT, n=160 people with hypertension) randomized participants to true acupuncture (including PC6, ST36) or sham. After six weeks, 24-hour ambulatory systolic pressure fell by about 5.4 mmHg and diastolic pressure by about 3.4 mmHg in the true-acupuncture group, compared with only 0.5–2 mmHg in the sham group; the between-group difference was significant, and part of the effect persisted at 12 weeks. Although smaller than typical first-line antihypertensive effects (usually 10–15 mmHg), this suggests acupuncture may be an adjunct. Mechanistically, the Longhurst group showed in hypertensive animal models that EA-related pressure reduction depends on medullary opioid receptors and GABA pathways, with NO participating in peripheral resistance regulation. **A 5-mmHg reduction in systolic pressure is associated with about a 10% reduction in stroke risk**, so even a modest effect may matter at population scale.

**Human evidence for acupuncture’s anti-inflammatory effects:** Several small RCTs report lower serum TNF-α, IL-6, or CRP in rheumatoid- and knee-osteoarthritis patients after acupuncture/EA (mostly 2–8 weeks, samples of 20–100, moderate but highly heterogeneous effects). In light of Liu’s 2020 somatotopic mechanism, evidence-based optimization of “acupoint and dose” for anti-inflammatory acupuncture (low frequency, hindlimb acupoints, low intensity) is emerging.

**Safety:** White 2001 (*BMJ*) prospectively surveyed 34,000 acupuncture consultations in the UK and reported no serious adverse events (pneumothorax, infection, nerve injury); minor events (bruising, faintness, transient pain) occurred in about 6–7%. Pneumothorax is the most dangerous complication, usually from deep needling of chest/back points; infection risk depends on use of single-use sterile needles. People with bleeding disorders or taking anticoagulants should use caution; pregnant patients should avoid blood-moving points such as LI4 and SP6. Overall, when performed by a qualified practitioner, acupuncture is safer than most drug interventions.

## Links to NO, NAD+, and longevity networks

- **Acupuncture as a programmable “NO-bioavailability enhancer”:** Alongside exercise (shear stress→eNOS) and dietary nitrate (nitrate–nitrite–NO pathway), acupuncture is a third non-drug NO intervention, with three adjustable dimensions—acupoint, frequency, and intensity. All three share the endpoint of restoring eNOS function and maintaining NO homeostasis, corresponding to the NO-basics chapter’s central proposition: maintaining NO homeostasis is maintaining a youthful state.
- **Acupuncture’s anti-inflammatory action directly counters inflammaging:** EA suppresses core SASP (senescence-associated secretory phenotype) cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6 via vagal/sympathetic reflexes. Mechanistically, this resembles a “physical anti-inflammatory drug.” Chronic inflammation is a hub among the 12 hallmarks of aging, linking vascular, metabolic, immune, and neurodegenerative processes (see 01-foundations/01-aging-hallmarks.md). By acting on both vascular (NO) and inflammatory (vagal) axes, acupuncture may be a rare “dual-axis intervention.”
- **Microcirculation and organ perfusion:** Improved microcirculation (increased LDF flow and nailfold flow velocity) implies improved tissue oxygen supply and metabolic-waste clearance, supporting the hypothesis that better microcirculation may delay organ aging. NO-dependent vasodilation also helps maintain cerebral blood flow and blood–brain-barrier integrity (eNOS↓→cerebral perfusion↓→neuroinflammation↑; see the neuroimmune-axis chapter). Acupuncture’s effects on cerebral perfusion, suggested by fMRI/transcranial-Doppler studies, warrant further testing in cognitive-aging research.
- **NAD+/SIRT1 intersection:** Alongside eNOS activation, acupuncture may increase SIRT1 (a known intersection between NO and NAD+-dependent enzymes; see the NO-basics chapter). SIRT1 suppresses NF-κB acetylation/activation and promotes anti-inflammatory activity, converging with acupuncture’s anti-inflammatory effects. “Acupuncture→NO→SIRT1→NF-κB↓” may theoretically connect acupuncture to NAD+-based longevity networks, but direct human evidence remains weak.

## Intervention directions and actionable suggestions

1. **Evidence-informed acupoints and frequencies:** For anti-inflammatory/immune modulation, low-frequency (2-Hz) EA plus hindlimb/distal points (ST36, with the strongest animal mechanistic evidence); for analgesia, use Han’s frequency map (2 Hz or dense-sparse 2/100 Hz for chronic pain); for nausea/vomiting, PC6; for blood-pressure reduction, PC6 + ST36 (Flachskampf protocol).
2. **Dose parameters:** Human RCTs commonly use 2–3 sessions per week, 20–30 minutes per session, with manual twirling or low-frequency EA during needle retention (2 Hz; intensity sufficient for slight local muscle twitching), and assess after 6–12 weeks. There is preliminary evidence linking de qi (soreness, numbness, distension, heaviness) with response; practitioners should aim to elicit it.
3. **Adjunct, not replacement:** Effects on blood pressure (~−5 mmHg), pain (~0.2–0.3 SD), and inflammation are moderate to small. Position acupuncture as supportive/synergistic, combined with exercise (the primary NO pathway), a Mediterranean-style diet, and smoking/alcohol reduction—not as a stand-alone anti-aging intervention.
4. **Safety red lines:** Use a licensed TCM practitioner and confirm single-use sterile needles. Avoid deep needling of chest/back points (pneumothorax risk). People taking anticoagulants, pregnant patients, and sites with skin infection require avoidance or caution; faint-prone people should be treated lying down.
5. **Monitoring:** For vascular/metabolic indications, periodically recheck blood pressure, FMD (endothelium-dependent dilation), and CRP/IL-6 to track objective change.

## Open questions and controversies

- **Debate over acupoint specificity:** Vickers’ IPD meta-analysis (real acupuncture better than sham, small effect) and Hinman 2014 (real = sham) coexist; in the GERAC migraine trial, sham equaled real acupuncture. Is acupoint specificity a true biological property (as the somatotopy in Liu 2020 suggests), or is it diluted by placebo effects in large studies? Current evidence neither supports the strong claim that “an acupoint is a precise target” nor the claim that acupuncture is “completely ineffective.”
- **The human “vagus–dopamine–spleen” pathway has not been replicated:** Torres-Rosas’s mechanism has no direct human equivalent evidence, and its “dopamine replacing NE” conclusion conflicts with Rosas-Ballina’s 2011 classic model. The precise neural circuit for acupuncture’s human anti-inflammatory effects remains unknown.
- **Limits of direct human NO measurement:** Plasma NOx has a short half-life and is strongly affected by dietary nitrate; skin-surface microelectrode studies have small samples and uncertain reproducibility. Quantitative evidence that acupuncture increases human NO is weaker than animal evidence.
- **Publication bias and low-quality trials:** Small positive studies are overrepresented in acupuncture research. Sham-control design is itself controversial (is superficial needling truly inert?).
- **Almost no evidence for anti-aging indications:** Animal studies of acupuncture’s effects on SASP and epigenetic aging are only beginning; human aging endpoints (telomeres, epigenetic clocks) have scarcely been studied. “Acupuncture is anti-aging” is currently a plausible hypothesis, not an evidence-based conclusion.
