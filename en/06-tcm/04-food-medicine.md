# Food as Medicine and Dietary Approaches to Healthy Aging: A Modern Nutrition and NO-Pathway Annotation of the TCM “Food and Medicine as One” Concept

> **Core thesis:** “Food and medicine from the same source” (food as medicine) is not merely a wellness slogan, but a system of everyday interventions institutionalized through national catalogues and partially validated by modern nutrition science. TCM classifies foods by four natures, five flavors, and five colors corresponding to the organs. Behind some of these categories, molecular nutrition identifies testable pathways: polysaccharides and saponins in qi-tonifying foods activate AMPK/eNOS; allicin, quercetin, and catechins in blood-activating foods directly or indirectly increase NO bioavailability; nitrate-containing vegetables provide an oxygen-independent NO bypass that does not rely on eNOS; and anthocyanins and lignans in dark-colored foods provide antioxidant and epigenetic effects. The distinctive value of dietary approaches to aging is that they are low-dose but sustainable, accessible, and naturally integrated with lifestyle: they are “daily interventions targeting the NO/NAD+ pathways” (see 01-foundations/03-no-basics.md).

## I. Food as medicine: from the *Shennong Bencao Jing* to national catalogues

### 1.1 The three grades in the *Shennong Bencao Jing*: an early “safety classification”

The concept of food and medicine from the same source can be traced to the *Shennong Bencao Jing* (compiled around the Eastern Han dynasty). It lists 365 medicinal substances and classifies them by toxicity and safety with prolonged use into **upper, middle, and lower grades**:

| Grade | Number | Definition | Modern interpretation |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Upper | 120 | “Primarily nourish life in accordance with Heaven; non-toxic; taking much or for a long time does not harm people; for those wishing to become light, benefit qi, remain youthful, and extend life” | Substances suitable for long-term dietary use—highly overlapping with today’s food-medicine catalogue |
| Middle | 120 | “Primarily nourish the constitution in accordance with humanity; may be non-toxic or toxic, and should be used as appropriate” | Dual-use food/medicinal substances requiring pattern-based and need-based use |
| Lower | 125 | “Primarily treat disease in accordance with Earth; many are toxic and cannot be taken for long” | Therapeutic medicines, not to be treated as foods |

Upper-grade substances such as ginseng, goji, yam, jujube, Poria, honey, black sesame, and coix seed are still almost all included in national food-medicine catalogues. The management logic of “upper-grade substances may be taken long term” is aligned in its aim with modern **long-term exposure safety assessment** (chronic, genetic, and reproductive toxicology): substances used as foods must withstand scrutiny for “daily intake and lifetime limits.”

### 1.2 Modern evolution of the food-medicine catalogue

The current “Catalogue of Substances Traditionally Used as Both Food and Chinese Medicinal Materials” (commonly called the food-medicine catalogue) is managed dynamically by China’s National Health Commission (formerly the Ministry of Health):

- **2002:** The Ministry of Health’s notice on further standardizing the management of health-food ingredients published an initial list of **87 substances** (including yam, hawthorn, goji, jujube, Poria, mulberry, raspberry, cassia seed, chrysanthemum, honeysuckle, black sesame, ginger, and honey).
- **2014:** More than ten substances were added, including Angelica, turmeric, rose, ginseng, *Lonicera confusa*, saffron, and tsaoko.
- **2018:** Nine substances—Codonopsis, Astragalus, cistanche, *Dendrobium officinale*, American ginseng, reishi, Gastrodia, Cornus, and Eucommia leaf—entered a pilot program.
- **2023:** These nine substances moved into the formal catalogue, with **recommended intake amounts** and restrictions on eligible populations issued at the same time. Codonopsis and Astragalus thus obtained formal “food status,” but with dose limits.

The catalogue’s logic is “safe for long-term consumption at normal amounts in traditional food-use forms.” It distinguishes three regulatory categories: health foods, medicines, and ordinary foods. Catalogue substances may be used as ordinary food ingredients; non-catalogue Chinese medicinal materials may not be added to ordinary foods. This regulatory framework itself expresses the distinction “dietary therapy is not drug therapy”: the catalogue regulates **safety**, not **efficacy**.

### 1.3 TCM dietary therapy and modern nutrition: two vocabularies, overlapping targets

| Dimension | TCM dietary therapy | Modern nutrition |
|---|---|---|
| Basis for classification | Four natures, five flavors, meridians, ascending/descending/floating/sinking (based on experience and Five-Phase models) | Macro-/micronutrients, phytochemicals, energy density (based on molecules and epidemiology) |
| Unit of intervention | Food combinations and medicinal cuisine (chief/deputy/assistant/envoy) | Individual nutrients, foods, or dietary patterns (Mediterranean diet, DASH) |
| Individualization | Food selected by pattern, constitution, season, and organ state | Genetic polymorphisms, gut microbiome, metabolic phenotype |
| Evidence forms | Millennia of clinical experience and case records | Cohorts, RCTs, meta-analyses |
| Intersection | Foods described as “tonifying qi” or “invigorating blood” | Nitrate, polyphenols, polysaccharides, saponins→NO/AMPK/SIRT1 pathways |

The convergence is not accidental: the list of foods used to “tonify qi and invigorate blood” (see 06-tcm/01-yiqi-huoxue.md) nearly item by item corresponds to prominent molecules in contemporary cardiovascular nutrition. The value of dietary therapy is to return the “molecule” to “a plate of food,” allowing long-term intervention in ordinary meals—consistent with the repeatedly supported nutrition-epidemiology principle that **dietary patterns are superior to isolated supplements**.

## II. The four natures, five flavors, and five colors: a modern annotation of the framework

### 2.1 Four natures (cold, cool, warm, hot, neutral)

The four natures are qualitative descriptions of the direction in which a food adjusts the body’s energy state. In modern terms, they may partially map onto **thermogenesis, perfusion, and inflammatory state**:

| Nature | Representative foods | Modern molecular annotation |
|---|---|---|
| Cold/cool | Bitter melon, mung bean, chrysanthemum, watermelon, celery | Anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antipyretic actions (flavonoids/saponins inhibit NF-κB/PGE₂ pathways) |
| Warm/hot | Fresh ginger, garlic, lamb, longan, Sichuan pepper | Thermogenesis and increased blood flow: gingerols/capsaicin activate TRPV1→sensory nerves release CGRP→vasodilation; may promote brown-fat thermogenesis |
| Neutral | Yam, Poria, jujube, black sesame | Polysaccharides modulate the microbiome; bidirectional immune regulation; relatively gentle effects |
| (Nature/flavor/meridian classification) | — | Cold/warm may roughly correspond to the net effect on “vasodilation/constriction and metabolic rate” |

### 2.2 Five flavors (sour, bitter, sweet, pungent, salty)

| Flavor | Examples | Active constituents | Modern correspondence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pungent | Garlic, onion, ginger, chili | Allicin, quercetin, gingerols, capsaicin | TRPV1 activation, eNOS/NO upregulation, antimicrobial and antiplatelet actions |
| Sweet | Yam, jujube, honey, goji | Polysaccharides, oligosaccharides, cyclic AMP (cAMP) | Prebiotics→short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs)→gut barrier and immunity; jujube is among the plant foods with the highest cAMP content |
| Sour | Hawthorn, smoked plum, vinegar | Organic acids, flavonoids | Promotes digestion, chelates minerals; hawthorn acids may promote lipid metabolism |
| Bitter | Bitter melon, chrysanthemum, green tea | Flavonoids, alkaloids, tea polyphenols | TAS2R bitter receptors→GLP-1 secretion→metabolic regulation; EGCG is antioxidant |
| Salty | Seaweed, table salt | Minerals (iodine, sodium) | Modern nutrition clearly recommends **sodium restriction** (<5 g salt/day); the traditional “salty enters the kidney” concept must be modified by salt-restriction consensus |

### 2.3 Five colors and five organs

| Color | Associated organ | Representative foods | Phytochemicals | Modern evidence direction |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Green/blue-green | Liver | Green tea, spinach, celery | EGCG catechins, nitrate, chlorophyll | eNOS activation + dietary nitrate→NO (see 02-vascular/02-dietary-nitrate.md) |
| Red | Heart | Hawthorn, red jujube, goji, tomato | Hawthorn flavonoids, cAMP, zeaxanthin, lycopene | Lipid lowering, endothelial protection, eye protection |
| Yellow | Spleen | Yam, pumpkin, millet, corn | Diosgenin, beta-carotene, starch | Prebiotic effects and glycemic regulation |
| White | Lung | Lily bulb, tremella, yam, white radish | Polysaccharides (tremella polysaccharide), glucosinolates | Immune regulation (beta-glucans) |
| Black | Kidney | Black sesame, black soybean, black fungus, mulberry | Anthocyanins, melanin, lignans, fungus polysaccharides | Antioxidant/antiplatelet actions, hair and bone support (traditional “kidney tonification”) |

The modern kernel of the “five colors” framework is **matching colors with phytochemical profiles**—color acts as a molecular fingerprint (anthocyanins are purple-black, lycopene red, chlorophyll/nitrate green), a concept independently supported in nutrition by “eat the rainbow.” Though simple, its emphasis on diversity and correspondence is consistent with modern recommendations for phytochemical diversity.

## III. Molecular-mechanism chain: dietary active constituents → NO/longevity pathways

### 3.1 Qi-tonifying foods: polysaccharides and saponins → AMPK/eNOS

**Yam (*Dioscorea*):** Principal constituents include **diosgenin**, yam polysaccharides, and mucoproteins. Mechanistic chain:

```
Yam polysaccharides ─► gut-microbiota fermentation ─► Bifidobacterium/Lactobacillus ↑ ─► SCFAs (butyrate) ↑ ─► GPR41/43 → gut barrier + anti-inflammation
Diosgenin ─► structurally resembles estrogen/steroid precursors ─► weak estrogen-like/metabolic regulation
Yam polysaccharides ─► amylase inhibition, delayed glucose absorption ─► postprandial glucose AUC ↓
```

In animal experiments, yam polysaccharides improve insulin resistance and modulate microbiome composition; diosgenin has lipid-lowering and anti-inflammatory evidence. The modern focus of yam’s traditional “spleen-strengthening” action is mainly the **gut-microbiome–metabolism axis**—the spleen is the postnatal foundation in TCM, while the gut is a hub of immunity and nutrient absorption.

**Jujube (*Ziziphus jujuba*):** Jujube is among the plant foods known to have the **highest cyclic AMP (cAMP) content**, and also contains jujube polysaccharides and triterpenic acids (oleanolic and betulinic acids). cAMP is an upstream messenger in PKA/Epac signaling; qi tonification (energy signaling) thus has a molecular parallel with modern “cAMP-PKA-CREB metabolic adaptation.” Jujube polysaccharides may improve gut ecology via prebiotic mechanisms.

**Longan (longan aril):** Rich in glucose, iron, and polysaccharides, and traditionally used to “tonify heart and spleen and benefit qi and blood.” Modern mechanistic evidence is limited; its value is closer to rapid energy provision plus mild antioxidant action.

**Astragalus chicken soup (medicinal cuisine):** Astragaloside IV and APS directly increase NO synthesis through **PI3K/Akt→eNOS-Ser1177 phosphorylation** and promote mitochondrial biogenesis via AMPK→PGC-1α (see the Astragalus section in 06-tcm/02-herbs.md). The distinctive value of medicinal cuisine is that it dilutes the “medicinal” dose into prolonged, low-dose everyday intake, potentially avoiding the warm/drying side effects of high-dose Astragalus in exchange for continuity.

### 3.2 Blood-activating foods: sulfur compounds, flavonoids, and polyphenols → eNOS/NO + antiplatelet action

**Garlic (*Allium sativum*)—allicin:** Allicin is generated when **alliinase** catalyzes the conversion of alliin after garlic is crushed, and is highly unstable (degrading within hours into ajoene, diallyl sulfides, and other products). Mechanistic chain:

```
Crushed garlic ─► alliin + alliinase ─► allicin
   │
   ├──► Sulfur compounds (S-allyl cysteine, SAC, etc.) ─► eNOS upregulation, increased NO release
   ├──► Sulfides generated by CSE ─► H₂S (hydrogen sulfide) ─► H₂S and NO synergize in vasodilation and platelet inhibition
   ├──► Inhibits HMG-CoA reductase ─► cholesterol synthesis ↓ (lipid lowering)
   └──► Inhibits platelet COX-1/TXA₂ ─► antiplatelet aggregation (mild aspirin-like effect)
```

**H₂S–NO synergy** is a mechanism worth emphasizing: garlic is one of the few foods that provide precursors for both **NO and H₂S**, two gaseous signaling molecules. They reinforce each other at the vascular smooth-muscle level (H₂S inhibits PDE, prolonging cGMP half-life), providing a dual-pathway molecular explanation for garlic’s “blood-invigorating” action.

**Onion (*Allium cepa*)—quercetin:** Red onion contains approximately 20–50 mg quercetin/100 g and is a major dietary source. Quercetin phosphorylates eNOS-Ser1177 via both **PI3K/Akt and AMPK**, increases eNOS expression, and inhibits NOX-derived O₂− (protecting NO from inactivation). Quercetin glycosides are hydrolyzed by gut microbiota before absorption; bioavailability varies substantially between individuals.

**Ginger (*Zingiber officinale*)—6-gingerol:** 6-Gingerol inhibits both COX and LOX pathways and platelet TXA₂ synthesis, and has anti-inflammatory, antiplatelet, and mild vasodilatory effects. Its activation of TRPV1 and heat sensation may reflexively increase peripheral blood flow.

**Hawthorn (*Crataegus pinnatifida*)—hawthorn flavonoids:** Flavonoids such as vitexin and hyperoside increase eNOS and endothelial NO release, while inhibiting **hepatic HMG-CoA reductase** (lowering cholesterol) and **pancreatic lipase** (reducing dietary fat absorption)—a modern footnote to traditional “food-digesting and accumulation-resolving” use. The European hawthorn leaf/flower extract WS 1442 has been tested in a large heart-failure trial (see Section V).

**Black fungus (*Auricularia auricula*)—fungus polysaccharides:** Acidic polysaccharides have **heparin-like anticoagulant activity** (enhancing antithrombin III, inhibiting thrombin and platelet GPIIb/IIIa activation). Animal studies report lipid-lowering and antithrombotic effects; human evidence is weak, and combination with anticoagulants may add bleeding risk.

**Green tea (*Camellia sinensis*)—EGCG:** The catechin EGCG is among the best-studied food-derived eNOS activators:

```
EGCG ─► binds 67-kDa laminin receptor/rafts ─► PI3K ─► Akt ─► eNOS-Ser1177 phosphorylation
   │                                                        │
   ├──► AMPK activation (AMP/ATP ↑ + LKB1) ─► eNOS ↑ / SIRT1 ↑ ─► NO
   ├──► Inhibits NADPH oxidase, scavenges ROS ─► NO bioavailability ↑ (prevents ONOO−)
   └──► VCAM-1/ICAM-1 downregulation ─► monocyte adhesion ↓ (anti-atherosclerosis)
```

### 3.3 Nitrate-containing vegetables: an eNOS-independent NO bypass

Leafy greens such as spinach (150–300 mg/100 g), celery (100–250 mg/100 g), and beetroot (110–250 mg/100 g) supply NO through the **nitrate–nitrite–NO pathway**—independent of oxygen and eNOS, and more active under ischemia/hypoxia. After a single dose of beetroot juice, plasma nitrite peaks in 2–3 hours and systolic blood pressure falls 4–10 mmHg (meta-analysis: mean SBP reduction 4.4 mmHg; Siervo 2013, 16 trials). Older adults, whose eNOS function is impaired, may benefit most; the pathway depends on oral bacteria for nitrate reduction (excessive mouthwash use can abolish the effect). Full mechanism: **02-vascular/02-dietary-nitrate.md**. This is the **highest-evidence** category in the dietary-therapy list (grade-A RCT meta-analysis).

### 3.4 Dark-colored foods: antioxidant and epigenetic regulation by anthocyanins and lignans

**Black soybean (black soybean seed coat):** The seed coat contains anthocyanins, particularly **cyanidin-3-glucoside (C3G)**, at 100–500 mg/100 g dry weight. C3G antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and endothelial-protective effects (eNOS upregulation, NF-κB inhibition) are well supported in cell and animal experiments. C3G and its metabolite protocatechuic acid may activate **AMPK/SIRT1**, coupling to the NAD+ network. **Black sesame:** Melanin (a eumelanin-like polymer) has broad free-radical-scavenging activity. **Sesame lignans** (sesamin and sesamolin) inhibit Δ5 desaturase, modulate lipid metabolism, and may **spare vitamin E (γ-tocopherol)**. The traditional uses “tonify liver and kidney, benefit essence and blood” thus point molecularly to antioxidant reserves and lipid metabolism.

### 3.5 Integrated pathway map

```
[Qi-tonifying group] yam polysaccharides/diosgenin, jujube cAMP, APS
    │
    ├──► Gut microbiota→SCFAs→gut barrier/anti-inflammation    (spleen = gut axis)
    └──► AMPK ─► eNOS (Ser1177) ──┐
                                   │
[Blood-activating group] allicin/H₂S, quercetin, EGCG, hawthorn flavonoids ─► PI3K/Akt→eNOS, antiplatelet, antioxidant
                                   │
                                   ▼
                           NO bioavailability ↑
        ▲                          │
        │                          ▼
[Nitrate group] spinach/celery/beetroot ─► NO₃−→NO₂−→NO (bypass; eNOS-independent, more active in hypoxia)
                                   │
                                   ▼
                           sGC→cGMP→PKG→vasodilation/antithrombosis/anti-atherosclerosis
                                   │
                                   ▼
        SIRT1/AMPK/mitochondrial biogenesis ←── NO positive feedback ←── NAD+ longevity network
[Dark-food group] C3G anthocyanins, lignans ─► antioxidant/AMPK/SIRT1 (parallel pathways)
```

## IV. Classic medicinal cuisines

| Medicinal cuisine | Source/tradition | Composition and mechanism | NO/longevity annotation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Angelica, ginger, and lamb soup** | *Jingui Yaolue*, chapter on women | Angelica (ferulic acid→eNOS; nourishes/invigorates blood) + ginger (6-gingerol→anti-inflammatory/antiplatelet) + lamb (L-carnitine→fat oxidation, carnosine→antioxidant, iron/zinc→hematopoiesis) | “Nourish blood and dispel cold” = coupled supply of eNOS-related constituents (ferulic acid), thermogenesis/perfusion (ginger), and mitochondrial fuel (carnitine) |
| **Goji–chrysanthemum tea** | Folk eye-health preparation | Goji (zeaxanthin, goji polysaccharides/LBP) + chrysanthemum (luteolin, apigenin→anti-inflammatory flavonoids/eNOS activation) | Zeaxanthin protects the macula from light damage; LBP has immunomodulatory and antioxidant actions |
| **Red-jujube and longan soup** | Folk blood-nourishing/calming preparation | Jujube (cAMP, polysaccharides) + longan (glucose, iron, polysaccharides) | Rapid energy plus mild antioxidant effects; metabolic regulation through cAMP signaling |

The common logic of medicinal cuisine is combining **dual-use food/medicinal substances with nutrient-dense foods**: medicinal constituents provide pathway targets at low, long-term doses, while food ingredients provide substrates and energy (carnitine, iron, protein). This accords with the TCM formulation idea of “food as the base, medicine as the guide.”

## V. Evidence grading

| Finding | Evidence grade | Key literature/basis |
|---|---|---|
| Dietary nitrate lowers blood pressure (beetroot/leafy greens) | **A (RCT meta-analysis)** | Siervo 2013, *Hypertension*, 16 trials, SBP −4.4 mmHg; mouthwash-blockade studies support causality |
| EGCG activates eNOS (PI3K/Akt-dependent) | Established (in vitro/ex vivo) | Lorenz 2004, *J Biol Chem*; endothelium-dependent vascular-ring relaxation |
| Garlic preparations lower blood pressure | Moderate (RCT meta-analysis) | Ried 2008, 11 trials; SBP decrease about 8 mmHg in hypertension |
| Green-tea consumption and cardiovascular risk | Strong association (large cohort meta-analysis) | Each additional cup/day associated with ~8–10% lower coronary risk |
| Hawthorn lowers lipids | Moderate (small Chinese RCTs) | TC/TG reductions of about 10–20%, with varying dose/duration |
| Hawthorn extract WS 1442 in heart failure | Moderate to moderately strong (large RCT, primary endpoint negative) | Holubarsch 2008, SPICE trial, n=2,681 |
| Goji zeaxanthin improves macular pigment | Moderate (small RCT) | Bucheli 2011, n=30, 15 g/day for 90 days |
| Lutein + zeaxanthin prevents AMD progression | **Strong (large RCT)** | AREDS2 2013, *JAMA*; ~26% lower risk in low-intake subgroup |
| Black-fungus polysaccharide antiplatelet/anticoagulant action | Animal support (strong ex vivo, weak human evidence) | Heparin-like activity; additive bleeding risk with anticoagulants |
| Yam polysaccharides modulate gut microbiota | Animal support | Increased Bifidobacteria and improved glycemia |
| Curcumin→SIRT1/AMPK | Animal support (direct in-vivo targets disputed) | Note oral bioavailability <1% |
| Quercetin→eNOS (onion) | Established (in vitro) + moderate (human observational) | Mainly cell experiments; human effects may require high-dose supplements |
| Five colors/four natures and meridian correspondences | Theoretical framework (mechanistic hypothesis) | Nutritional support for color–phytochemical associations; no direct evidence for organ correspondences |

**Overall evidence structure:** Dietary approaches show a “**strong at both ends, weak in the middle**” pattern. Whole foods with nitrate, green tea, and goji have stronger evidence because they can be standardized and tested in RCTs; isolated molecules have solid in-vitro mechanisms but may not reach relevant concentrations in vivo; the middle layer (whole-food extracts and medicinal cuisine) is chemically complex and poorly standardized, with human evidence mostly from small studies using surrogate endpoints.

## VI. Details of clinical and experimental evidence

- **Garlic and blood pressure:** The 2008 meta-analysis by Ried et al. (11 trials, 399 participants, mostly people with hypertension) found that garlic preparations (powder 600–900 mg/day, equivalent to about 1.3% allicin) lowered systolic pressure by about 8 mmHg on average (about 4–5 mmHg in normotensive participants). **Key detail:** allicin decomposes with heat, so cooked garlic may have substantially less activity. Enteric-coated preparations and crushing garlic and letting it stand for 10 minutes before cooking are two ways to preserve activity.
- **EGCG and endothelial function:** Lorenz et al. (2004) showed that EGCG (10–100 μM) phosphorylates eNOS-Ser1177 through PI3K/Akt and induces endothelium-dependent vasodilation. Acute consumption of two cups of green tea (about 300–400 mg catechins) can increase FMD within 30 minutes in healthy people, with effects lasting about two hours. A dietary reference is 3–5 cups daily (total catechins 500–1,000 mg), an epidemiologically associated range.
- **Dietary nitrate:** 200–400 g/day of spinach, celery, and beetroot provides 300–1,000 mg nitrate, exceeding the doses in many clinical trials. See 02-vascular/02-dietary-nitrate.md for effects (meta-analysis SBP −4.4 mmHg; a 2026 dose-response RCT reportedly suggests 400 mg for endothelial function and 800 mg for blood pressure).
- **Goji and the macula:** Bucheli 2011 (30 healthy adults aged 55–65, goji 15 g/day for 90 days) found significantly increased plasma zeaxanthin and improved macular pigment optical density (MPOD). AREDS2 (4,203 participants, five-year follow-up) found that lutein 10 mg + zeaxanthin 2 mg was safer than beta-carotene; in a subgroup with low dietary carotenoid intake, late AMD risk was about 26% lower. Dried goji contains about 2–4 mg zeaxanthin/100 g dry weight, often as dipalmitate, among the highest levels in common foods.
- **Hawthorn, lipids, and heart failure:** Small Chinese RCTs report 10–20% reductions in TC/TG with hawthorn flavonoids. In the European WS 1442 SPICE trial (2,681 heart-failure patients; 900 mg/day for 24 months), the primary composite endpoint was not significantly different; a subgroup with LVEF >35% showed a trend toward lower mortality. The traditional “cardiotonic” positioning of hawthorn remains exploratory in evidence-based terms.
- **Curcumin:** Oral bioavailability is extremely low (<1% in rats). The classic 1998 Shoba experiment found that coadministration with piperine (black pepper) increased curcumin plasma concentration by about 2,000%. In-vivo SIRT1/AMPK activation has generally been reported at high doses (500–1,000 mg/day plus piperine) or with specialized formulations (nanoparticles, liposomes); ordinary curry intake (about 50–100 mg curcumin) is far below pharmacological doses.

## VII. Links to NO, NAD+, and longevity networks

From this knowledge base’s perspective, dietary therapy is not a collection of isolated “folk remedies,” but a **dietary expression of longevity networks**:

```text
                         ┌──────── NAD+/SIRT1 longevity network ────────┐
                         │                                               │
EGCG/curcumin/anthocyanins ─► AMPK ─► SIRT1 ─► eNOS deacetylation/activation ─► NO
                         │                                               │
Garlic/onion/hawthorn ─► eNOS-Ser1177 ─► NO ─► sGC/cGMP/PKG (vascular)
                         │                   │
Dietary nitrate ─► NO₂−→NO (bypass) ─► AMPK/SIRT1 positive feedback ─► mitochondrial biogenesis
                         │
Resveratrol (mulberry/grape/peanut skin) ─► PDE4 inhibition → cAMP↑ → Epac1 → AMPK → SIRT1
```

Key coupling points:

1. **EGCG is a “triple-coupling” molecule:** eNOS activation (NO) + AMPK activation (energy sensing) + SIRT1 upregulation (NAD+ network). Green tea therefore has one of the most comprehensive links to longevity pathways among the foods discussed.
2. **Garlic’s dual H₂S–NO gasotransmission:** unlike a single-target drug, garlic provides precursors for both NO and H₂S, which reinforce each other via cGMP/PDE pathways. The molecular basis for “blood activation” is more layered than eNOS activation alone.
3. **The nitrate bypass as an “aging-compensation” pathway:** when eNOS function declines with age (uncoupling, BH4 depletion), dietary nitrate provides an eNOS-independent source of NO (see 02-vascular/02-dietary-nitrate.md). NO→AMPK/SIRT1 positive feedback also overlaps mechanistically with endothelial benefits of calorie restriction and exercise.
4. **The “indirect SIRT1” lesson from resveratrol:** Park 2012 (*Cell*) showed that in vivo resveratrol acts mainly by inhibiting PDE4→cAMP→Epac1→AMPK to activate SIRT1 indirectly, rather than binding SIRT1 directly. This cautions against simplistic attribution of “classic pathways” to food compounds; their true in-vivo targets are often indirect.
5. **“Regulate rather than supplement”:** the goal of food-derived NO interventions is to restore physiological NO bioavailability (increase eNOS, prevent oxidative inactivation, provide substrates), not to pursue high NO concentrations. Avoid iNOS-derived NO bursts and nitrosative stress (see the double-edged-sword principle in 01-foundations/03-no-basics.md).

## VIII. Safety: dietary therapy is not drug therapy

1. **Scope and limits:** The food-medicine catalogue admits substances on the basis that they are “safe for long-term consumption in normal amounts and traditional forms,” **not as an endorsement of efficacy**. Using catalogue substances to treat disease or replace medicines is outside their regulatory purpose. “Upper-grade substances extend life when taken long term” is a historical statement; modern evidence supports, at most, adjunctive improvement in healthspan.
2. **Constitution and pattern differentiation:** Dietary therapy also has contraindications. Excessive lamb, longan, ginger, or garlic may cause dry mouth, insomnia, or higher blood pressure in people described as yin-deficient with fire or excess heat. Large amounts of bitter-cold foods (bitter melon, mung bean, chrysanthemum) may worsen diarrhea in people with spleen/stomach cold deficiency. Seasonal hot/cold choices should match the individual.
3. **Dose and bioavailability traps:** Oral bioavailability of polyphenols is generally <10% (EGCG ~1–2%, quercetin ~2%, curcumin <1%), and there is **no universal answer to “how much is enough.”** Extract/supplement doses far above food doses do not necessarily produce greater efficacy and can reduce safety (e.g., case reports of liver injury from high-dose green-tea extract).
4. **Heavy metals and pesticide residues:** Goji, tea, and other catalogue items have sometimes failed pesticide/heavy-metal spot checks. Root vegetables (yam, ginger) and fungi (black fungus) may accumulate soil heavy metals. Practical measures include buying from reputable sources, washing/blanching (blanching nitrate-rich vegetables can remove some oxalate and nitrate), and rotating varieties.
5. **Drug interactions (important):** Garlic, black fungus, hawthorn, Angelica, and ginger have antiplatelet/anticoagulant activity; combination with warfarin, aspirin, or direct oral anticoagulants may entail **additive bleeding risk** (see interaction section in 06-tcm/02-herbs.md). Nitrate-rich vegetables may add to blood-pressure-lowering drugs; people with high potassium should use caution with beetroot.
6. **Special populations:** Avoid high-nitrate vegetable juice in infants under six months (susceptibility to methemoglobinemia); pregnant people can maintain ordinary vegetable intake; people with chronic kidney disease should consider potassium/phosphorus load from black beans and black fungus.

## IX. Intervention directions and actionable suggestions

**An evidence-ranked “dietary prescription” for supportive health management, not treatment:**

1. **Start with at least 300 g of leafy vegetables daily** (rotate spinach, celery, arugula, and beetroot)—the nitrate bypass has grade-A evidence. Avoid excessive antiseptic mouthwash, which can eliminate oral nitrate-reducing bacteria and disrupt the pathway.
2. **Drink 2–3 cups of green tea daily** (about 500 mg catechins)—EGCG’s eNOS/AMPK/SIRT1 coupling. Avoid strong tea on an empty stomach and before bed.
3. **Eat garlic 3–5 times per week** (crush and let stand for 10 minutes before cooking, or use an enteric-coated preparation)—NO+H₂S signaling and blood-pressure evidence. Consult a clinician if taking anticoagulants.
4. **Use onion, hawthorn, and black fungus routinely in meals**—everyday low-dose exposure to quercetin, hawthorn flavonoids, and fungus polysaccharides; people with bleeding tendency should be cautious with black fungus.
5. **Goji, 15 g/day** (eat dried berries or drink as tea while consuming the berries)—zeaxanthin for eye health (consistent with AREDS2 dose logic), plus antioxidant LBP.
6. **Rotate dark-colored foods** (black beans, black sesame, mulberry)—anthocyanin/lignan pathways parallel to AMPK/SIRT1.
7. **Match to constitution:** people with cold deficiency may eat Angelica–ginger–lamb soup in autumn/winter; those with damp-heat may choose mung bean, chrysanthemum, or winter melon. “Food selected by pattern” and “eat the rainbow” are compatible: the former guides direction, the latter diversity.
8. **Monitor:** blood pressure, lipids, glucose, and endothelial measures (FMD/cfPWV). Dietary therapy should work alongside exercise, salt restriction, and smoking cessation, not replace medication.

## X. Open questions and controversies

1. **The constituent–food gap:** isolated-compound mechanisms (EGCG, quercetin) are robust in vitro, but bioavailability in whole foods, metabolite activity, and individual microbiome differences leave “how much is effective?” unresolved. The active in-vivo species may often be metabolites rather than parent compounds (e.g., quercetin glycoside→aglycone→phenolic acids).
2. **Testability of the four natures/five flavors:** Can metabolomics/transcriptomics define “cold” and “hot”? TRPV1/thermogenesis mapping covers only pungent-warm foods; molecular taxonomy of sweet, sour, bitter, and salty remains undeveloped.
3. **Dose response and bidirectional regulation:** How to define the bidirectional effects of ginseng/Astragalus (low-dose stimulation, high-dose suppression) within dietary doses? Recommended intakes for the nine newer catalogue substances still need validation against long-term exposure data.
4. **Lack of hard endpoints:** Apart from nitrate-related blood-pressure reduction and AREDS2, dietary interventions have almost no RCTs with hard cardiovascular, cancer, or healthspan endpoints. Green-tea epidemiological benefit (~8–10% per additional cup) is an association, not proof of causality; residual confounding by tea drinkers’ lifestyles is hard to exclude.
5. **Connecting patterns with biomarkers:** Whether “qi deficiency” and “blood stasis” can be quantified by ADMA, NO metabolites, or FMD (see 06-tcm/01-yiqi-huoxue.md) to personalize dietary prescriptions remains unresolved.
6. **Tension between catalogue regulation and efficacy claims:** The food-medicine catalogue regulates safety, not efficacy, while market claims for “dietary anti-aging” often exceed the evidence. The gap between regulatory boundaries and consumer understanding is a real governance challenge.
