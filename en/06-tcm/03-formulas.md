# Classic Formulas and Herb Combinations: Formulation Logic, NO-Pathway Annotation, and Evidence

> **Core thesis:** Formulas are the basic unit of TCM intervention and a molecular-biological entry point for understanding the principle of tonifying qi and invigorating blood. Their value lies not in having “more ingredients,” but in **emergent properties generated by combination**: herbs are organized according to the hierarchy of chief, deputy, assistant, and envoy, and their relationships are governed by the traditional compatibility framework. They interact across absorption, metabolism, targets, and pathways to produce an integrated effect of “enhancing efficacy, reducing toxicity, and correcting imbalance.” Starting from formulation principles, this chapter analyzes nine classic formulas—Buyang Huanwu Decoction, Xuefu Zhuyu Decoction, Shengmai San, Danshen Yin, Taohong Siwu Decoction, Liuwei Dihuang Pill, Jingui Shenqi Pill, Zuogui/Yougui Pills, and Guipi Decoction—and connects modern mechanistic research to the NO pathway and longevity networks.

## 1. Core concepts

Classic formulas are fixed combinations recorded in historical works such as *Jingui Yaolue*, *Yilin Gaicuo*, and *Jingyue Quanshu*, and repeatedly tested in clinical practice over centuries. Compared with single herbs (see 02-herbs.md), formulas have three defining features: **hierarchical structure**—the chief/deputy/assistant/envoy system assigns each herb a role and dose weight; **relational regulation**—the seven interaction categories define synergy, constraint, and contraindications; and **whole-formula emergence**—the pharmacological effect of the whole is not a simple sum of the effects of its constituents. Formula-decomposition studies repeatedly report that the complete formula outperforms single herbs or partial combinations. In longevity research, classic formulas are natural tools for “testing patterns through formulas and testing mechanisms through formulas”: a formula corresponds to a pattern (such as qi deficiency with blood stasis), which at the molecular level may map to endothelial dysfunction and reduced NO bioavailability (see 01-yiqi-huoxue.md). Formulas can therefore bridge TCM pattern differentiation with modern vascular and aging biology.

## 2. Formulation principles: chief, deputy, assistant, envoy, and the seven interactions

### 2.1 Chief, deputy, assistant, and envoy: hierarchy and dose weighting

A systematic account of the “chief–deputy–assistant–envoy” framework appears in the *Suwen: Treatise on the True Meaning of the Essentials*: “The herb that treats the principal disease is the chief; those that assist the chief are deputies; those that respond to the deputies are envoys.” Their roles are:

- **Chief herb:** the principal therapeutic agent for the main disease and pattern; generally the largest dose and indispensable.
- **Deputy herb:** reinforces the chief’s effect or treats a secondary accompanying pattern.
- **Assistant herb:** serves one of three functions—assisting the chief/deputy, moderating their toxicity or harshness, or counterbalancing them (for example, combining cold and warm properties).
- **Envoy herb:** guides the formula to a particular region (for example, Platycodon raises the action upward, while Achyranthes directs blood downward) and/or harmonizes the other herbs (as licorice often does).

Buyang Huanwu Decoction exemplifies this hierarchy: **a large dose of raw Astragalus, 120 g, is the chief**, powerfully replenishing original qi to address the root of “qi deficiency.” The tail of Angelica is the deputy, invigorating and nourishing blood; red peony, Chuanxiong, peach kernel, and safflower assist in dispelling stasis, while earthworm opens the channels and collaterals as an assistant/envoy. The chief herb’s dose is several times the combined dose of the deputy and assistant herbs. This “dose determines hierarchy” design is also dose–response engineering in molecular pharmacology: astragaloside and astragalus-polysaccharide AMPK/eNOS signals may require sufficient dose to activate the pathway.

### 2.2 The seven interactions: a system for classifying relationships among herbs

The *Shennong Bencao Jing* states that herbs may act singly, reinforce one another, assist one another, inhibit one another, counteract one another, oppose one another, or neutralize one another—the “seven interactions”:

| Relationship | Meaning | Classic example | Clinical significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mutual reinforcement | Herbs with similar actions are combined to markedly enhance efficacy | Peach kernel with safflower (mutually reinforcing stasis-resolving action in Taohong Siwu Decoction) | Common efficacy-enhancing combination |
| Mutual assistance | A supporting herb enhances the principal herb | Sandalwood and Amomum move qi to assist Danshen’s blood-invigorating action in Danshen Yin (“when qi moves, blood moves”) | Common efficacy-enhancing combination |
| Mutual restraint | One herb suppresses another’s toxicity | Pinellia is restrained by fresh ginger | Toxicity-reducing combination |
| Mutual detoxification | One herb removes another’s toxicity | Fresh ginger neutralizes Pinellia toxicity | Toxicity-reducing combination |
| Mutual weakening | One herb diminishes another’s efficacy | Ginseng is weakened by radish seed | Avoid combination |
| Mutual opposition | Combined use produces or increases toxicity | The “Eighteen Incompatibilities,” e.g., aconite opposed to Pinellia | Contraindicated combination |

In modern pharmacological terms, the seven interactions are an empirical encoding of “multi-constituent interactions.” Mutual reinforcement/assistance can correspond to pathway synergy (for example, blood-activating herbs preserve NO bioavailability while qi-tonifying herbs activate eNOS synthesis); restraint/detoxification can correspond to chemical neutralization of toxic constituents (for example, gingerols in ginger suppress the pro-inflammatory activity of toxic Pinellia proteins); weakening/opposition can correspond to pharmacodynamic antagonism or additive toxicity. Structurally, this millennia-old empirical system resembles modern network pharmacology, with its focus on multi-target synergy and toxicity-network alerts.

### 2.3 Multi-target synergy in formulas: a network-pharmacology perspective

Single herbs already act on multiple targets (see 02-herbs.md); formulas link the multiple targets of multiple herbs into a **three-tier network**: upstream signal activation (ginsenosides Rb1/Rg1→PI3K/Akt→eNOS-Ser1177; APS→AMPK), midstream NO protection (salvianolic acids and Schisandra lignans scavenge superoxide and prolong NO half-life), and downstream execution (ligustrazine regulates vascular tone, hirudin inhibits thrombin, and earthworm-derived components promote fibrinolysis). Network-pharmacology analyses find that active constituents in classic formulas may be enriched in pathways involving vascular smooth-muscle contraction, platelet activation, NF-κB inflammation, and HIF-1 hypoxia responses. The redundant design—“multiple targets per herb, shared targets across herbs”—may help formulas address drug resistance and compensatory mechanisms that can limit single-target drugs.

## 3. The molecular-mechanism chain: from pattern to the NO network

The shared mechanisms of the nine formulas can be compressed into one pathway chain:

```text
Qi deficiency with blood stasis (endothelial dysfunction)
   │
   ├─ Upstream · qi-tonifying herbs: ginsenosides Rb1/Rg1 → PI3K/Akt → eNOS-Ser1177 phosphorylation → NO synthesis ↑
   │                           APS/astragaloside IV → AMPK → eNOS ↑ + improved endothelial energy metabolism
   │
   ├─ Midstream · blood-activating herbs: salvianolic acids/tanshinones/ligustrazine → scavenge O₂− → NO half-life and bioavailability ↑
   │                           inhibit NF-κB → VCAM-1/ICAM-1 ↓ → leukocyte adhesion ↓
   │                           inhibit platelet αIIbβ3 activation and TXA₂ → thrombotic tendency ↓
   │
   └─ Downstream · channel-guiding/collateral-opening herbs: Platycodon guides upward + Achyranthes directs blood downward (Xuefu Zhuyu Decoction)
                               hirudin anticoagulation, earthworm fibrinolysis (animal-derived channel-opening agents)
   │
   ▼
Restore NO bioavailability → vasodilation ↑ / anti-atherosclerosis ↑ / antithrombosis ↑ / endothelial repair ↑
   │
   └─ Simultaneously suppress excessive iNOS expression (Astragalus, salvianolic acids) → prevent NO bursts and ONOO− nitrosative stress
      = “bidirectional regulation” of eNOS/iNOS (see Section 7)
```

Each link has a corresponding TCM concept: tonifying qi = replenishing NO-synthetic capacity; invigorating blood = protecting the environment in which NO survives; opening collaterals = improving perfusion and microcirculation. This is the fundamental distinction between a formula and a single herb: a single herb usually occupies only one link, whereas a formula, through its combinations, **covers upstream, midstream, and downstream simultaneously**, creating a synthesis–protection–execution loop.

## 4. In-depth review of nine representative formulas

### 4.1 Buyang Huanwu Decoction: a paradigm of tonifying qi and invigorating blood

**Source and composition** (Qing dynasty, Wang Qingren, *Yilin Gaicuo*): raw Astragalus 120 g, tail of Angelica 6 g, red peony 4.5 g, earthworm 3 g, Chuanxiong 3 g, peach kernel 3 g, safflower 3 g.

**Indications and rationale:** qi deficiency with blood stasis after stroke—hemiplegia, facial deviation, impaired speech, drooling, frequent urination or incontinence, a dark-pale tongue with white coating, and a slow, weak pulse. Wang Qingren attributed post-stroke hemiplegia to “qi deficiency and blood stasis,” rather than external wind, and used a large dose of Astragalus (four taels) to powerfully replenish original qi: “when qi is abundant, blood flows; when stasis is removed, the collaterals open.” The tail of Angelica breaks stasis while nourishing blood; red peony, Chuanxiong, peach kernel, and safflower invigorate blood and dispel stasis; earthworm opens channels and collaterals. Modern textbooks and clinical practice commonly retain these proportions, with Astragalus at more than six times the total dose of the blood-activating herbs. **Qi tonification is primary and blood activation secondary**—a dose-based declaration of the “tonify qi and invigorate blood” principle.

**Modern mechanistic chain** (principally cerebral ischemia–reperfusion/stroke models; evidence: animal grade A, human grades B/C):

- Improves cerebral blood flow: increases regional cerebral blood flow (rCBF) in ischemic areas, reduces infarct volume, and improves neurological-deficit scores.
- Neuroprotection: anti-apoptotic effects (Bcl-2↑, Bax↓, caspase-3 activity↓), antioxidant effects (SOD↑, MDA↓), and reduced blood–brain-barrier disruption.
- Anti-inflammatory effects: inhibits NF-κB nuclear translocation, lowers TNF-α and IL-6, and attenuates the post-ischemic inflammatory cascade.
- Promotes angiogenesis: increases VEGF, Ang-1, bFGF, and CD34+ endothelial progenitor cells, supporting vascular reconstruction in the ischemic penumbra.
- **NO regulation:** increases eNOS expression and Ser1177 phosphorylation and raises NO, while suppressing excessive iNOS induction—a bidirectional “support the normal, suppress the pathological” regulation of NOS isoforms.
- Antiplatelet effects: lowers platelet aggregation and improves hemorheology.

**Formula-decomposition studies:** Comparisons of the full formula, a formula without Astragalus, blood-activating herbs alone, and other decompositions mostly report that **the complete formula performs best**. Astragalus alone or blood-activating herbs alone do not match the complete formula, directly supporting the synergy of qi tonification and blood activation and the molecular rationale of Buyang Huanwu Decoction’s chief/deputy/assistant/envoy structure.

### 4.2 Xuefu Zhuyu Decoction: treating qi and blood together through qi movement and blood activation

**Source and composition** (Qing dynasty, Wang Qingren, *Yilin Gaicuo*): peach kernel 12 g, safflower 9 g, Angelica 9 g, raw Rehmannia 9 g, Chuanxiong 4.5 g, red peony 6 g, Achyranthes 9 g, Platycodon 4.5 g, Bupleurum 3 g, bitter orange 6 g, licorice 3 g.

**Formulation analysis:** The formula nests three classic structures: (1) Taohong Siwu Decoction—peach kernel and safflower with Siwu Decoction (white peony replaced by red peony, prepared Rehmannia by raw Rehmannia)—to invigorate blood, resolve stasis, and nourish blood; (2) Sini San (Bupleurum, bitter orange, peony, licorice) to soothe the liver and regulate qi—**when qi moves, blood moves**; (3) Platycodon with Achyranthes, one ascending and one descending, to regulate qi movement in the chest. The eleven-herb formula treats qi and blood together and coordinates ascent and descent.

**Indications and rationale:** blood stasis in the chest—persistent chest or head pain, stabbing and fixed pain, palpitations, insomnia, irritability, evening low-grade fever, a dark-red tongue with stasis spots, and a choppy pulse. Wang Qingren called it a general formula for “blood stasis in the chest’s blood mansion”; it is one of the foundational formulas of the TCM blood-stasis-resolving tradition.

**Modern mechanisms** (evidence grade B, mainly small RCTs and meta-analyses):

- Antiplatelet action: inhibits platelet aggregation, lowers TXB₂, increases 6-keto-PGF₁α (a prostacyclin metabolite), and restores the TXA₂/PGI₂ balance.
- Improves hemorheology and microcirculation: lowers whole-blood and plasma viscosity and the red-cell aggregation index.
- Anti-inflammatory/endothelial protection: lowers IL-6, TNF-α, and CRP and improves endothelium-dependent vasodilation.
- Anti-atherosclerotic effects: animal studies report lipid lowering, inhibition of vascular smooth-muscle proliferation/migration, and smaller plaque area.
- Anti-ischemic effects: reduces myocardial infarct size and ischemia–reperfusion injury in animal models.

**Clinical use:** widely used for coronary angina (chest impediment), migraine, insomnia, and postherpetic neuralgia when attributed to blood stasis. RCTs and systematic reviews for coronary angina report improvement in symptom scores, ischemic ECG changes, and some endothelial measures, but sample sizes are generally small and methodological quality moderate, limiting the evidence grade.

### 4.3 Shengmai San (Shengmai Yin): a qi- and yin-tonifying cardiotonic formula

**Source and composition** (Jin dynasty, Zhang Yuansu, *Yixue Qiyuan*, also included in Li Gao’s *Neiwaishang Bianhuo Lun*): ginseng 9 g, Ophiopogon 9 g, Schisandra 6 g.

**Indications and rationale:** qi and yin deficiency—profuse sweating, fatigue, weakness, shortness of breath, reluctance to speak, dry throat and thirst, a dry red tongue with little coating, and a weak, rapid pulse—as well as chronic cough with lung deficiency and depletion of qi and yin. Sweet-warm ginseng tonifies qi and generates fluids as chief; sweet-cool Ophiopogon nourishes yin and clears heat as deputy; sour-astringent Schisandra restrains the lung and stops sweating as assistant/envoy. **One replenishes, one moistens, and one astringes**; together they restore the pulse by replenishing qi. The name “Shengmai” means restoring the pulse by replenishing qi and yin.

**Modern mechanisms** (animal evidence grade A; human B/C):

- Cardiotonic action: positive inotropy, increased myocardial contractility and cardiac output, and improved LVEF in heart failure.
- Anti-shock effects: raises blood pressure and improves microcirculatory perfusion; Shengmai injection is used as adjunctive treatment in septic/cardiogenic shock.
- Myocardial protection: reduces ischemia–reperfusion injury, apoptosis, and arrhythmia and reduces infarct size.
- Endothelium and NO: multiple clinical studies report that Shengmai injection raises serum NO, lowers ET-1, and improves FMD/endothelial function.
- Constituent mechanisms: ginsenosides Rb1/Rg1→PI3K/Akt/eNOS; Ophiopogon polysaccharides are antioxidant; Schisandra lignans (schisandrin B) protect myocardial mitochondria and inhibit lipid peroxidation.

**Clinical use:** Shengmai injection, Shenmai injection, and oral Shengmai preparations are widely used for coronary disease, heart failure, shock, and fatigue syndromes attributed to qi-yin deficiency. Meta-analyses report that Shengmai injection added to standard heart-failure treatment may further improve LVEF, NYHA class, and six-minute walk distance; methodological quality is generally fair and findings require cautious interpretation.

### 4.4 Danshen Yin: a small qi-moving, blood-activating formula for heart and stomach

**Source and composition** (Qing dynasty, Chen Xiuyuan, *Shifang Geku*): Danshen 30 g, sandalwood 5 g, Amomum 5 g.

**Indications and rationale:** heart and epigastric pain due to blood stasis and qi stagnation. Danshen, used in a large dose, invigorates blood, resolves stasis, and relieves pain as chief. Sandalwood warms the middle and moves qi; Amomum moves qi, opens the appetite, and harmonizes the middle as deputy/assistant. Though it has only three herbs, it embodies “treating heart and stomach together”: chest impediment from cardiac-vessel stasis often accompanies epigastric fullness; qi-moving herbs assist blood activation (“when qi moves, blood moves”) and harmonize the stomach to relieve middle-jiao stagnation. **Mechanisms:** tanshinol and salvianolic acids activate eNOS/increase NO, act as antioxidants, and inhibit platelets; sandalwood volatile oil relieves spasm and pain; Amomum promotes gastrointestinal motility and protects gastric mucosa. Used for coronary angina with gastrointestinal symptoms, chronic gastritis, and stomach pain; evidence grade C, mainly clinical observation and small studies.

### 4.5 Taohong Siwu Decoction: a foundational formula for nourishing and invigorating blood

**Source and composition** (Qing dynasty, Wu Qian, *Yizong Jinjian*): Siwu Decoction (prepared Rehmannia, Angelica, white peony, and Chuanxiong—for nourishing and regulating blood, originating in *Taiping Huimin Heji Ju Fang*) plus peach kernel and safflower.

**Indications and rationale:** blood stasis with blood deficiency—dysmenorrhea, irregular menstruation, postpartum abdominal pain due to stasis, and traumatic bruising/swelling. Siwu Decoction nourishes and replenishes blood, while peach kernel and safflower invigorate blood and resolve stasis: “nourish and invigorate blood, remove stasis and generate anew,” avoiding damage to healthy qi from simply breaking stasis. **Mechanisms:** improves hemorheology and inhibits platelet aggregation; animal models report angiogenesis (increased VEGF) and regulation of estrogen/progesterone. Its more important value is as a **building block for formulas**: Xuefu Zhuyu Decoction is based on Taohong Siwu Decoction. Understanding it helps explain the combination principle “invigorate blood without harming it; nourish blood without retaining stasis.”

### 4.6 Liuwei Dihuang Pill: a millennium-old representative kidney-tonifying/anti-aging formula

**Source and composition** (Song dynasty, Qian Yi, *Xiao’er Yaozheng Zhijue*; derived from Jingui Shenqi Pill by removing cinnamon twig and aconite): prepared Rehmannia 24 g, Cornus 12 g, Chinese yam 12 g, Alisma 9 g, Poria 9 g, Moutan bark 9 g.

**Indications and rationale:** kidney-yin deficiency—soreness and weakness of the lower back and knees, dizziness, tinnitus or hearing loss, night sweats, seminal emission, tidal bone-steaming fever, thirst-wasting, and perimenopausal syndrome. The formula is known for “three tonifying and three draining” herbs: prepared Rehmannia nourishes kidney yin, Cornus secures essence, and yam tonifies the spleen and secures the kidney; Alisma drains kidney turbidity, Moutan clears deficient heat, and Poria drains spleen dampness. It tonifies while draining, preventing yin nourishment from becoming cloying. **Anti-aging position:** the leading TCM formula for “tonifying the kidney to resist aging”; the kidney stores essence and governs bones, marrow, and communication with the brain, and kidney deficiency is regarded in TCM as a core mechanism of aging.

**Modern research** (animal evidence grade A; human C):

- Antioxidant/anti-aging effects: in D-galactose-induced aging mice, increases SOD and GSH-Px, lowers MDA, and improves learning/memory (Morris water maze); some studies report increased SIRT1/AMPK signaling (to be verified).
- Endocrine regulation: clinical observations in perimenopausal women report improved hot flashes, irritability, and other menopausal symptoms and modulation of sex hormones.
- Immune regulation: increases T- and B-lymphocyte proliferation and NK-cell activity, with bidirectional regulation.
- Metabolism: RCTs combining it with metformin for type 2 diabetes report better glycemic control (moderate evidence).
- NO: animal studies report improved endothelial function and modulation of NO metabolism (specific data to be verified).

### 4.7 Jingui Shenqi Pill: warming kidney yang through “a small fire that generates qi”

**Source and composition** (Han dynasty, Zhang Zhongjing, *Jingui Yaolue*): dried Rehmannia 24 g, yam 12 g, Cornus 12 g, Alisma 9 g, Poria 9 g, Moutan bark 9 g, with cinnamon twig and prepared aconite 3 g each.

**Indications and rationale:** kidney-yang deficiency—lower-back pain and weak legs, coldness of the lower body, lower-abdominal tightness, difficult or excessive urination, phlegm-fluid retention, edema, and thirst-wasting. Liuwei Dihuang Pill is derived from this formula by **removing cinnamon and aconite**. Jingui Shenqi Pill adds small amounts of cinnamon and aconite to a larger group of yin-nourishing herbs, seeking yang within yin and kindling a gentle fire—the notion of “a small fire generating qi,” rather than directly using strongly hot herbs to replenish yang. **Modern research** (mostly evidence grade C): regulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis, improved gonadal and erectile function, anti-fatigue and antioxidant effects; used clinically for kidney-yang-deficiency patterns in chronic kidney disease and diabetic neuropathy. It contains aconite, which must be properly processed and decocted for a prolonged period; aconitine toxicity requires caution.

### 4.8 Zuogui and Yougui Pills: Zhang Jingyue’s paired tonics embodying “yang within yin, yin within yang”

**Source and composition** (Ming dynasty, Zhang Jingyue, *Jingyue Quanshu*): Zuogui Pill—prepared Rehmannia 24 g; yam, goji berry, Cornus, dodder seed, deer-antler gelatin, and tortoise-plastron gelatin 12 g each; Achyranthes 9 g. Yougui Pill—prepared Rehmannia 24 g; yam, goji berry, dodder seed, deer-antler gelatin, and Eucommia 12 g each; Cornus and Angelica 9 g each; cinnamon and aconite 6 g each.

**Rationale:** Zuogui Pill is made from Liuwei Dihuang Pill by **removing the three draining herbs (Alisma, Poria, Moutan bark) and adding nourishing agents**, thereby strongly replenishing true yin. Yougui Pill is made from Jingui Shenqi Pill by **removing the three draining herbs and adding warming, essence-filling agents**, thereby warming kidney yang and replenishing essence and blood. Zhang Jingyue wrote: “Those skilled at tonifying yang must seek yang within yin, so yang receives yin’s support and transformation is inexhaustible; those skilled at tonifying yin must seek yin within yang, so yin rises with yang and its source remains unexhausted.” Zuogui contains “yin within yang” (warming support from deer-antler gelatin), while Yougui contains “yang within yin” (yin-nourishing blood from prepared Rehmannia and Angelica). **Modern research** (evidence grade C): Zuogui promotes bone formation and increases bone density in ovariectomized osteoporosis models (specific effect size to be verified); Yougui regulates reproductive endocrine axes. Both are potent tonics and are unsuitable for prolonged use in people without marked deficiency.

### 4.9 Guipi Decoction: tonifying qi and blood, treating heart and spleen together

**Source and composition** (Song dynasty, Yan Yonghe, *Jisheng Fang*): Atractylodes, Poria, Astragalus, longan, sour jujube seed, ginseng, Aucklandia, honey-fried licorice, Angelica, and Polygala (with fresh ginger and jujube).

**Indications and rationale:** heart/spleen deficiency and insufficiency of qi and blood—palpitations, poor memory, insomnia, night sweats and deficient heat, poor appetite, fatigue, sallow complexion, pale tongue, and a thin weak pulse; also bleeding due to spleen failure to contain blood (heavy menstruation, blood in stool). Astragalus, ginseng, Atractylodes, and honey-fried licorice tonify qi and strengthen the spleen as chief (the spleen generates and contains blood); Angelica and longan nourish blood and heart as deputies; sour jujube seed, Polygala, and Poria calm the mind, while Aucklandia regulates qi and awakens the spleen so that “tonification does not cause stagnation.” **Modern research** (evidence grade C): possible improvement in anemia and fatigue (the qi-and-blood-tonifying concept is hypothesized to correspond to modern regulation of erythropoiesis), sleep, depression-like behavior in mice, and immune function. Guipi Decoction embodies the theory “qi generates blood”: qi tonics (Astragalus, ginseng) plus blood-nourishing herbs (Angelica, longan) may correspond molecularly to regulation of EPO and the hematopoietic microenvironment (to be verified).

## 6. Details of clinical and experimental evidence

- **Buyang Huanwu Decoction:** Repeated MCAO (middle cerebral artery occlusion) rat studies report infarct-volume reductions (approximately 20–40%, varying by regimen) and improved neurological-deficit scores. Mechanistic studies report that dosing within 24–72 hours increases eNOS phosphorylation and suppresses iNOS. Human evidence is concentrated in recovery after ischemic stroke: small RCTs and systematic reviews report improvement in NIHSS and activities of daily living (Barthel Index) when added to conventional treatment, but large hard-endpoint studies are lacking.
- **Xuefu Zhuyu Decoction:** Meta-analyses for coronary angina report that addition to conventional Western treatment improves overall response rates for angina symptoms and ECG changes. Some studies detect lower platelet aggregation and TXB₂, consistent with antiplatelet activity of peach kernel, safflower, and Chuanxiong.
- **Shengmai San:** Meta-analyses of Shengmai injection as adjunctive heart-failure therapy suggest an LVEF increase of about 4–8 percentage points and reductions in BNP/NT-proBNP (values vary among meta-analyses). Clinical reports in septic/cardiogenic shock describe stabilized blood pressure and reduced vasopressor requirements. Evidence for eNOS activation by ginsenosides is among the most complete constituent chains (PI3K/Akt/eNOS-Ser1177, consistent ex vivo and in vivo).
- **Liuwei Dihuang Pill:** In D-galactose aging models (approximately 100–500 mg/kg/day by gavage), SOD and GSH-Px activity increase significantly, MDA decreases, and Morris-water-maze escape latency shortens. Perimenopausal observations and small RCTs report lower Kupperman menopausal-symptom scores. Note: no high-quality human data yet support anti-aging endpoints such as telomeres or epigenetic clocks.
- **Modern patent-medicine benchmarks:** The Tongxinluo CTS-AMI study (124 centers; 3,797 patients with STEMI within 24 h of onset; randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled) reported significant reductions in 30-day major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events and cardiac-death risk—the highest-level evidence for a Chinese medicine in myocardial infarction (see the end of 01-yiqi-huoxue.md). Compound Danshen Dripping Pills completed an FDA international phase II study (2010) and a phase III trial, a milestone for internationalization of Chinese medicine, but the phase III primary endpoint did not meet the prespecified statistical threshold; the FDA requested additional studies starting in 2017 (details to be verified). The multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled MUST study of Shexiang Baoxin Pill reported improved angina stability and quality of life, but no significant benefit on its principal hard endpoint.

## 7. Links to NO, NAD+, and longevity networks

- **Bidirectional regulation of eNOS/iNOS is a defining NO feature of formulas compared with single herbs:** qi tonics (ginseng, Astragalus) activate PI3K/Akt→eNOS to restore physiological NO synthesis; blood-activating/heat-clearing herbs (Danshen, Moutan bark) inhibit NF-κB→iNOS transcription to prevent inflammatory NO bursts and ONOO− nitrosative stress. The net effect of Buyang Huanwu Decoction—“eNOS up + iNOS down”—is precisely the desired window for NO-based anti-aging intervention: “regulate, rather than simply supplement” (see the conclusion in .assets/no-tcm-report.txt).
- **Antioxidation protects NO:** salvianolic acids, Schisandra lignans, and Cornus tannins scavenge O₂−, reducing chemical inactivation of NO (O₂−+NO reaction rate about 10¹⁰ M⁻¹s⁻¹) and ONOO− formation. Protecting NO bioavailability is the molecular substance of “invigorating blood.”
- **Hypothesized connection with the NAD+ network:** antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects may reduce DNA-damage-induced overactivation of PARP, indirectly conserving NAD+ and maintaining SIRT1 activity. Ginsenoside Rb1 and astragaloside IV have been reported to activate AMPK, which is coupled to NAD+ metabolism and mitochondrial biogenesis; some Liuwei Dihuang studies suggest SIRT1 upregulation (to be verified). This link remains at the **mechanistic-hypothesis** level and lacks direct human evidence.
- **Connections with the 12 hallmarks of aging:** anti-inflammatory effects (chronic inflammation), antioxidant/mitochondrial protection (mitochondrial dysfunction), anti-aging animal studies (cellular senescence), and AMPK activation (deregulated nutrient sensing) mean a single formula may touch several hallmarks, consistent with the multi-target nature of formulas.

## 8. Intervention directions and actionable suggestions

1. **Pattern differentiation is a prerequisite; matching formula to pattern is essential:** qi deficiency with blood stasis (recovery after stroke, coronary disease) → Buyang Huanwu approach; qi-yin deficiency (heart failure, fatigue) → Shengmai San/Yin; kidney-yin deficiency (menopause, weak/sore back and knees) → Liuwei Dihuang Pill; kidney-yang deficiency (cold intolerance, cold limbs, nocturia) → Jingui Shenqi/Yougui Pill; chest blood stasis (fixed stabbing pain, purple-dark tongue) → Xuefu Zhuyu Decoction; heart/spleen deficiency (insomnia, poor appetite, fatigue) → Guipi Decoction. Do not match a formula to a disease name alone.
2. **Dose and processing:** the 120-g Astragalus dose in Buyang Huanwu is a powerful tonic and requires guidance from a TCM clinician. Formulas containing aconite (Jingui Shenqi and Yougui) must use processed material and prolonged decoction to reduce toxicity.
3. **Combine with lifestyle measures:** formula treatment should be accompanied by aerobic exercise (shear stress→eNOS→NO) and dietary nitrate from dark-green leafy vegetables/beetroot. Lifestyle is the most reliable foundation for increasing NO bioavailability.
4. **Safety monitoring:** blood-activating formulas (Xuefu Zhuyu, Taohong Siwu, Danshen Yin) combined with anticoagulant/antiplatelet Western drugs may add bleeding risk. Excessive ginseng preparations may cause “ginseng abuse syndrome” (hypertension, insomnia). With prolonged use of kidney-yin-nourishing formulas, periodically assess digestive function.
5. **Monitoring measures:** blood pressure, lipids, glucose, FMD (endothelium-dependent dilation), cfPWV (carotid–femoral pulse-wave velocity), serum NO metabolites (nitrate/nitrite), and ADMA—translate “pattern improvement” into vascular-function measurements.

## 9. Open questions and controversies

1. **Debate over converting historical doses:** one Qing-dynasty tael is approximately 37 g, unlike modern textbook doses. Whether the “four taels” of Astragalus in Buyang Huanwu corresponds to 120 or 150 g, and its dose-response curve, lack pharmacokinetic evidence.
2. **Insufficient molecular validation of chief/deputy/assistant/envoy:** most decomposition studies compare only the full formula with a formula lacking one herb or with a single herb; systematic optimization of the “best dose ratio” (response-surface methods, orthogonal designs) is not yet standard.
3. **Black box of multi-constituent pharmacokinetics:** oral bioavailability of saponins and phenolic acids is generally low; which constituents reach target tissues and how much they contribute remain unclear.
4. **Gap between patterns and molecular biomarkers:** whether ADMA, NO metabolites, or FMD can guide formula choice and dose titration for qi tonification/blood activation remains unvalidated.
5. **Scarcity of hard-endpoint evidence:** apart from Tongxinluo CTS-AMI, classic formulas lack large, international, multicenter RCTs with hard clinical endpoints. Studies of “kidney-tonifying anti-aging” formulas such as Liuwei Dihuang are small and short.
6. **Interactions/bleeding risk with combined Chinese and Western medicines:** pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic interaction data for blood-activating formulas with P2Y12 inhibitors and DOACs are nearly absent.
