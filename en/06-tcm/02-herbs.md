# Mechanisms of Individual Herbs: Molecular Pharmacology of Ginseng, Danshen, Sanqi, Astragalus, and Representative Qi-Tonifying/Blood-Activating Herbs

## Core concepts

Representative herbs for tonifying qi and invigorating blood—ginseng, Danshen, Sanqi (notoginseng), and Astragalus (the “four Shen/Qi herbs”), as well as Ligusticum chuanxiong, kudzu root, Angelica sinensis, and Ginkgo leaf—converge at different molecular levels on the **nitric oxide (NO) pathway** and endothelial health. Their actions on the NO system fall into three broad strategies: **upstream activation** (kinase phosphorylation through PI3K/Akt, AMPK, etc. activates eNOS-Ser1177 and increases NO synthesis); **NO protection** (potent antioxidant activity scavenges radicals and ONOO−, reduces NO inactivation, and restores bioavailability); and **vascular-tone/coagulation regulation** (direct effects on smooth-muscle calcium signaling and inhibition of platelet TXA₂/αIIbβ3 pathways). This “supply the materials (tonify qi) + enhance activity (invigorate blood)” multi-target, multi-pathway approach is consistent with the basic principle of NO biology: maintain physiological NO rather than simply drive it higher (see 01-foundations/03-no-basics.md). A central insight of this knowledge base is that an isolated compound is not an extract, and an extract is not the whole herb. Each herb must be assessed independently across **chemical-constituent level, evidence grade, and bioavailability** to avoid misreading in-vitro mechanisms as clinical efficacy.

## The molecular mechanism chain

### 1. Ginseng (*Panax ginseng*): ginsenosides and PI3K/Akt/eNOS

The principal active constituents of ginseng are **ginsenosides**, dammarane-type triterpenoid saponins. By aglycone type, they are divided into **protopanaxadiols** (Rb1, Rb2, Rc, Rd) and **protopanaxatriols** (Rg1, Re, Rf, Rg2). Rg1 (triol type) and Rb1 (diol type) are the best-studied; they differ in how they target endothelial signaling.

**Rg1 → eNOS activation:**

```
Rg1 ──binds membrane/intracellular receptor──► PI3K ──► PIP3 ──► Akt (Thr308/Ser473 phosphorylation)
                                                                    │
                                                                    ▼
                                                       eNOS-Ser1177 phosphorylation (activating site)
                                                                    │
                                                                    ▼
                                                L-Arg ──eNOS──► NO ↑ ──► sGC/cGMP/PKG
                                                                    │
                                                     ┌──────────────┴──────────────┐
                                                     ▼                             ▼
                                               Vasodilation             Antiplatelet/anti-smooth-muscle proliferation
```

Rg1 also enhances eNOS activation by inhibiting **PTEN** (a negative regulator of PI3K) and promoting dissociation of eNOS from caveolin-1 (see the eNOS subcellular-localization mechanism in 01-foundations/03-no-basics.md). Mechanistic studies indicate that Rg1 dose-dependently increases endothelial-cell eNOS mRNA and protein expression and promotes NO release.

**Rb1 → antioxidant/anti-apoptotic chain:** Rb1 acts mainly through **antioxidant and anti-apoptotic** pathways rather than as a potent direct eNOS activator. It increases antioxidant enzymes including SOD, catalase (CAT), and HO-1, suppresses ROS generation, activates the Nrf2/ARE antioxidant response, and inhibits mitochondrial apoptosis (reducing the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio, cytochrome-c release, and caspase-3 activation). In ischemia–reperfusion models, Rb1 protection involves both restoration of eNOS phosphorylation and suppression of oxidative stress. Its anti-inflammatory action includes inhibition of NF-κB nuclear translocation and downregulation of TNF-α/IL-6.

**Ginsenosides and longevity networks:** Rg1/Rb1 can activate **AMPK** (through an increased AMP/ATP ratio and LKB1 phosphorylation), which in turn phosphorylates eNOS and upregulates **SIRT1**. Rb1 can also upregulate FOXO3a and antioxidant target genes, creating positive coupling between the “ginsenoside→AMPK/SIRT1→eNOS” axis and the NAD+ longevity network. Ginsenosides Re and Rd also have evidence of AMPK activation and improved insulin sensitivity. Ginseng’s **adaptogenic** action is expressed as gentle regulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis and optimization of energy metabolism to maintain homeostasis under stress.

### 2. Danshen (*Salvia miltiorrhiza*): tanshinone IIA and salvianolic acid B

Danshen’s active constituents fall into two major groups: **lipid-soluble diterpene quinones** (tanshinone IIA and its oxidized derivative sodium tanshinone IIA sulfonate) and **water-soluble phenolic acids** (salvianolic acids B and A, protocatechualdehyde, and rosmarinic acid).

**Tanshinone IIA → eNOS/NO:** Tanshinone IIA is lipid-soluble and can penetrate endothelial membranes. It directly increases eNOS expression and activity, promotes Ser1177 phosphorylation, and increases NO production. It also inhibits membrane translocation of the p47phox subunit of NADPH oxidase (NOX), reducing O₂− generation—both “increasing production” and “preventing inactivation.”

**Salvianolic acid B (SalB) → antioxidant protection:** Salvianolic acid B is a potent free-radical scavenger. Its phenolic hydroxyl groups directly quench O₂−, hydroxyl radicals, and lipid peroxyl radicals, preventing NO from reacting with O₂− to form **ONOO−** (peroxynitrite; diffusion-limited rate ~10⁹–10¹⁰ M⁻¹s⁻¹). Thus NO bioavailability is not consumed by oxidative inactivation. SalB also inhibits the NF-κB inflammatory pathway, downregulates VCAM-1/ICAM-1, and blocks monocyte adhesion, a key step in anti-atherosclerosis.

**Cryptotanshinone → antiplatelet/anticoagulant effects:** Cryptotanshinone inhibits platelet activation and TXA₂ release and lowers P-selectin expression. The mild anticoagulant effects of tanshinones, including inhibition of thrombin-induced platelet aggregation, are among the molecular bases of Danshen’s “blood-invigorating” action. **Danshen polyphenolic-acid injections**, principally containing salvianolic acids, are widely used in China as adjunctive treatment for coronary heart disease and cerebral infarction; small RCTs support increases in circulating NO, decreases in ET-1, and improved flow-mediated dilation (FMD).

### 3. Sanqi (*Panax notoginseng*): Panax notoginseng saponins (PNS)

The active constituents of Sanqi include **Panax notoginseng saponins (PNS)**, with **notoginsenoside R1** and **ginsenosides Rg1/Rb1** (Sanqi and ginseng are both Araliaceae and have overlapping saponin profiles) among the key components.

**PNS → antiplatelet/NO chain:**

```
PNS (Rg1/Rb1/R1)
      │
      ├──► Inhibit platelet activation: lower TXA₂, P-selectin, αIIbβ3 activation → anti-aggregation
      ├──► Anti-inflammatory: inhibit NF-κB → lower IL-6/TNF-α and VCAM-1
      ├──► Promote angiogenesis: increase VEGF and eNOS → collateral circulation
      └──► Activate eNOS: PI3K/Akt → Ser1177 phosphorylation → mild vasodilation
```

**Comparison with aspirin:** Mechanistic studies suggest that PNS and aspirin (which inhibit COX-1→TXA₂) partly, but not completely, overlap in their sites of action. PNS emphasizes multi-pathway inhibition of platelet activation, including P2Y12 and αIIbβ3, rather than a single COX blockade. Its antiplatelet effect is weaker than aspirin’s, but it is described as having **lower bleeding risk and no gastrointestinal injury**; it is therefore often used as an alternative or adjunct to aspirin in people at low bleeding risk (with monitoring). Promotion of angiogenesis by PNS (increased VEGF/eNOS and mobilization of endothelial progenitor cells) is a molecular basis for “invigorating blood and generating anew,” and may have distinct value in ischemic cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease.

### 4. Astragalus (*Astragalus membranaceus*): astragalus polysaccharide (APS) and astragaloside IV (AST IV)

The two principal active constituents are **astragalus polysaccharide (APS)** and **astragaloside IV (AST IV)**, a tetracyclic triterpenoid saponin.

**APS → AMPK/dual immune-regulation chain:**

```
APS ──► AMPK activation (LKB1/AMP dependent)
        ├──► eNOS phosphorylation → NO ↑ (qi tonification → endothelium)
        ├──► PGC-1α/mitochondrial biogenesis → improved endothelial metabolism
        └──► Bidirectional immune regulation:
              ├── Immune deficiency: enhance macrophage phagocytosis, NK activity, T/B-cell proliferation
              └── Excess inflammation: inhibit pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6/TNF-α) and excessive iNOS expression
```

**AST IV → PI3K/Akt/eNOS:** Astragaloside IV is a stronger endothelial-protective constituent than APS. It directly activates **PI3K/Akt**, phosphorylates eNOS-Ser1177, increases eNOS expression, inhibits NOX-derived ROS, and protects mitochondria. Studies show improved endothelial and cardiac function with AST IV in ischemia–reperfusion, diabetic vasculopathy, and pressure-overload cardiac hypertrophy models.

**Astragalus, telomeres, and aging:** In cellular and animal models, AST IV can **increase telomerase (TERT) expression and activity**, reduce telomere shortening, and protect hematopoietic stem cells and endothelial progenitor cells from senescence. This “telomere-protection” pathway directly corresponds to **telomere attrition**, one of the 12 hallmarks of aging (see 01-foundations/01-aging-hallmarks.md). Astragalus’s bidirectional immune regulation also relates to **immunosenescence**: improving thymic output and T-cell function and reducing chronic inflammation (inflammaging).

### 5. Other qi-tonifying and blood-activating herbs

**Angelica (*Angelica sinensis*):** Key constituents are **ferulic acid** and ligustilide. Ferulic acid scavenges free radicals, inhibits LDL oxidation, increases eNOS/NO, and inhibits platelet TXA₂. It is a representative “nourish-blood and invigorate-blood” herb, both replenishing and promoting blood.

**Chuanxiong (*Ligusticum chuanxiong*):** Its key constituent is **ligustrazine (tetramethylpyrazine)**, a **calcium-channel antagonist**. It suppresses intracellular Ca²⁺ rises in vascular smooth muscle and platelets, thereby dilating vessels and inhibiting platelet activation; it also inhibits TXA₂ synthesis, improves microcirculation, and improves red-cell deformability. Its immediate vascular-tone regulation as a “qi-moving, blood-invigorating” herb makes it a frequently used “mobilizing” component in formulas.

**Kudzu (*Pueraria lobata*):** Its key constituent is **puerarin**. Through PI3K/Akt, puerarin activates eNOS, increases NO, dilates coronary and peripheral vessels, and lowers blood viscosity. It has both estrogen-like and beta-blocker-like effects. Like Chuanxiong, it emphasizes immediate vascular regulation associated with “moving qi.”

**Ginkgo leaf (*Ginkgo biloba*):** Its key constituents are **ginkgolides** (PAF-receptor antagonism and antiplatelet action) and **ginkgo flavonoids** (potent antioxidants). Ginkgo leaf extract EGb761 can increase eNOS, inhibit platelet activity, and improve cerebral blood flow; it is widely used for cognitive and circulatory disorders.

## Evidence grading

### Evidence-grade framework

| Finding | Evidence grade | Key literature/basis |
|---|---|---|
| Ginsenosides Rg1/Rb1 activate PI3K/Akt→eNOS-Ser1177 and increase NO | Established (in vitro/ex vivo) | Leung KW, Ng TB. *Chin Med* 2013; multiple endothelial-cell/aortic-ring experiments |
| Rg1/Rb1 restore eNOS phosphorylation and reduce infarct size in ischemia–reperfusion models | Animal support | Rat ischemia–reperfusion and cardiac-infarction models |
| Ginsenosides activate AMPK/SIRT1 | Strong mechanistic association | Metabolic studies and SIRT1-pathway literature |
| Ginseng increases NO and improves FMD in healthy/cardiovascular populations | Moderate (human observational/small RCTs) | Small RCTs, generally 30–100 participants |
| Tanshinone IIA increases eNOS; compound Danshen dripping pills increase serum NO in coronary patients | Established (in vitro) + moderate (human) | Multiple cell/animal studies plus small RCTs |
| SalB antioxidant action/prevention of ONOO− generation | Established (in vitro) | Free-radical chemistry and cell experiments |
| Danshen polyphenolic-acid injection improves endothelial function in acute coronary syndrome | Moderate (human RCTs) | Chinese multicenter studies, relatively small samples |
| PNS antiplatelet effect (lower TXA₂/P-selectin) | Established (ex vivo/animal) | Platelet-function experiments and animal thrombosis models |
| PNS compared with aspirin for antiplatelet activity | Moderate (human observational) | Comparative studies and bleeding-risk assessment |
| APS activates AMPK; AST IV activates PI3K/Akt/eNOS | Established (in vitro/animal) | Ma X et al.; series of AST IV studies |
| AST IV increases telomerase/TERT and protects endothelial progenitor cells from senescence | Animal support | Cellular and animal aging models |
| APS bidirectionally regulates immunity | Strong association (in vitro/animal) | Immune-cell experiments and immunodeficiency models |
| Ligustrazine calcium antagonism; puerarin eNOS activation | Established (in vitro) + moderate (human formulas) | Smooth-muscle/endothelial-cell experiments; mostly formula studies |
| Ginkgolide PAF antagonism; EGb761 eNOS upregulation | Established (in vitro) + substantial human evidence | Numerous EGb761 cognition studies |

### Key points on evidence grading

- **The densest evidence is in vitro/ex vivo:** all the constituents above have substantial cellular or aortic-ring evidence for NO-pathway mechanisms, but this **cannot be extrapolated directly to clinical efficacy**.
- **Human evidence is generally “moderate”:** it consists mostly of small (30–150 participants), short-term RCTs or observational studies using **surrogate endpoints** (serum NO, ET-1, FMD, inflammatory markers). Long-term large trials focused on hard cardiovascular outcomes (MI, stroke, death) are lacking. The large RCT of Tongxinluo in CTS-AMI is an exception at the formula level (see 06-tcm/03-formulas.md).
- **The largest evidence gap is “constituent→whole herb”:** molecular mechanisms of isolated compounds are clearest, but extracts/whole herbs often have very low bioavailability (poor oral absorption of saponins and phenolic acids, strong first-pass effects), producing the common “potent ex vivo, attenuated in vivo” pattern.

## Details of clinical and experimental evidence

### Ginseng (Rg1/Rb1/ginseng extract)

- **Endothelial-cell experiments:** Rg1 (1–100 μM) dose-dependently increases eNOS phosphorylation and NO release; Rb1 (10–100 μM) activates the Nrf2/HO-1 antioxidant pathway.
- **Animals:** In rat cerebral ischemia–reperfusion, Rg1 pretreatment significantly reduces infarct area and restores eNOS phosphorylation in infarcted tissue. In myocardial ischemia models, Rb1 reduces infarct size and improves post-reperfusion vasodilation.
- **Humans:** Small RCTs report that ginseng extract increases circulating NO and improves endothelium-dependent vasodilation and insulin sensitivity in healthy older adults and people with type 2 diabetes. Doses are commonly 100–400 mg/day of ginsenosides. Human cardiovascular-protection evidence is moderate; hard-endpoint trials are still lacking.
- **Bioavailability:** Oral bioavailability of ginsenosides is low (Rg1 ~1–2%). They are mainly transformed by gut microbiota into secondary metabolites (e.g., Rg1→Rh1); some in-vivo effects may be mediated by these metabolites.

### Danshen (tanshinone IIA/salvianolic acid B/compound Danshen dripping pills)

- **Cells/animals:** Tanshinone IIA (1–10 μM) increases eNOS and inhibits NOX-derived O₂−; salvianolic acid B scavenges radicals and prevents oxidative inactivation of NO. Sodium tanshinone IIA sulfonate improves neurological function in cerebral ischemia models.
- **Humans:** Multiple small RCTs of compound Danshen dripping pills in coronary patients report increased serum NO, decreased ET-1, and improved FMD and exercise tolerance. Danshen polyphenolic-acid injection improves endothelial function and inflammatory markers in acute coronary syndrome/unstable-angina patients.
- **Caution:** Most RCTs were conducted by Chinese teams, had limited sample sizes (50–300) and short follow-up, and do not meet the evidence-quality threshold for strong recommendations in major international cardiovascular guidelines. Evidence of hard cardiovascular outcomes for Danshen products comes from formulas (e.g., Tongxinluo), not single-herb Danshen.

### Sanqi (PNS/notoginsenoside R1)

- **Platelet function:** PNS (0.1–1 mg/mL) inhibits platelet aggregation induced by ADP, collagen, and arachidonic acid and lowers TXA₂ and P-selectin. R1 significantly promotes angiogenesis (increased VEGF/eNOS) in ischemic-limb models.
- **Comparison with aspirin:** Small comparative studies suggest PNS antiplatelet potency is about 50–70% that of aspirin, with less prolongation of bleeding time and fewer gastrointestinal adverse effects. It may be used as an alternative/adjunct in people at low bleeding risk, with individualized monitoring.
- **Bioavailability:** PNS has poor oral absorption. Clinically it is often given intravenously (Xuesaitong/Xueshuantong injections) or in oral combination products; oral use requires attention to dose-response and individual variation.

### Astragalus (APS/AST IV)

- **Cells/animals:** APS activates AMPK and improves endothelial glucose metabolism and eNOS activity. AST IV (1–50 μM) activates eNOS through PI3K/Akt, protects against ischemia–reperfusion injury, and inhibits cardiac hypertrophy.
- **Telomeres/aging:** In senescent endothelial-cell models, AST IV increases TERT, reduces telomere shortening, and delays replicative senescence; it preserves stemness in hematopoietic-stem-cell aging models.
- **Humans:** Astragalus extract improves insulin sensitivity and lowers inflammatory markers in people with metabolic syndrome. Observational support exists for immune regulation in older adults with low immunity and after cancer chemotherapy/radiotherapy. **Human evidence is mainly based on immune and metabolic surrogate endpoints; evidence for hard anti-aging endpoints (telomere length, healthspan) remains early.**

### Other constituents (ferulic acid/ligustrazine/puerarin/EGb761)

- Ferulic acid inhibits LDL oxidation and increases eNOS; Angelica, in the context of “nourishing blood,” supports circulation and hematopoiesis.
- Ligustrazine antagonizes calcium and dilates cerebral vessels; it is often combined with Danshen for ischemic cerebrovascular disease. Human evidence mainly concerns combination preparations.
- Puerarin dilates coronary arteries and lowers blood viscosity. Its eNOS activation and estrogen-like effects are well supported ex vivo.
- Ginkgo EGb761 increases eNOS and antagonizes PAF as an antiplatelet agent. Human evidence for cognitive improvement is moderate, although some large trials have produced inconsistent results.

## Isolated compounds vs extracts vs whole herbs: three evidence levels must not be conflated

The largest methodological trap when interpreting evidence on qi-tonifying/blood-activating herbs is **conflating three levels**:

| Level | Definition | Evidence characteristics | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Isolated compounds | A single purified molecule (e.g., ginsenoside Rg1, tanshinone IIA) | Clearest mechanisms and strongest in-vitro/animal evidence, but achievable in-vivo concentrations may be low and metabolism uncertain | Rg1, salvianolic acid B, AST IV, ligustrazine |
| Extracts | A constituent mixture enriched using a particular solvent (e.g., PNS, Danshen polyphenolic acids, EGb761) | Relatively stable profile and amenable to standardization; evidence between isolated compounds and whole herbs | Compound Danshen dripping pills, Xuesaitong, EGb761 |
| Whole herbs/formulas | Crude medicinal material or a formula compounded according to TCM theory | Reflects real clinical use, but is chemically complex, varies by batch, and is difficult to assign mechanistically | Ginseng slices, combined Shen/Danshen/Sanqi, Tongxinluo |

One level cannot be directly extrapolated to another: **an isolated compound effective ex vivo at the μM level does not mean an oral whole herb reaches an effective in-vivo concentration**. Oral bioavailability of ginsenoside Rg1 is only about 1–2%; it is partly absorbed after gut-microbial conversion to secondary saponins. The active in-vivo molecules after oral ginseng may therefore not be Rg1 itself, creating a risk that attribution of ex-vivo effects to the “main constituent” is misleading. Salvianolic acids and astragaloside IV likewise face strong first-pass effects and poor oral absorption. Conversely, clinical benefits of whole formulas (such as improved hard outcomes in the Tongxinluo CTS-AMI study) cannot be attributed to a single constituent. This is the enduring interpretive tension between “multi-target formulas” and “clear single-compound targets.”

**Drug interactions and bleeding risk:** Combining blood-moving herbs (Sanqi, Danshen, Ginkgo, Angelica, Chuanxiong) with anticoagulants/antiplatelet drugs poses an **additive bleeding risk**, supported by clinical reports. Danshen with warfarin can markedly raise INR and bleeding events; intracranial bleeding case reports exist with Ginkgo preparations plus aspirin/warfarin; bleeding tendency should be monitored with Sanqi plus anticoagulants. Although ginseng is not classified as a blood-activating herb, its combination with warfarin may alter anticoagulant intensity through CYP3A4/glucocorticoid-like effects. Principle: **people taking any anticoagulant or antiplatelet drug must consult a clinician before using blood-activating herbs and receive enhanced monitoring; do not combine them on one’s own.**

## Links to NO, NAD+, and longevity networks

From this knowledge base’s perspective, the effect of qi-tonifying/blood-activating herbs such as the “four Shen/Qi herbs” on NO is **not an isolated vascular effect, but a point of positive coupling within longevity networks**:

```
                         ┌──── NAD+/SIRT1 longevity network ────┐
                         │                                      │
Qi-tonifying/blood-activating herbs ─► AMPK ─► SIRT1 ─► eNOS deacetylation/activation ─► NO
        (APS/AST IV)          │                                  ↑
                              │                                  │
                              └──────── ginsenosides ────────────┘
                                                                   │
NO ─► sGC/cGMP/PKG ─► vasodilation/antithrombotic effects
NO ─► AMPK/SIRT1 (positive feedback) ─► mitochondrial biogenesis
NO protection (antioxidant action of salvianolic acid) ─► bioavailability ↑
```

Specific levels of coupling:

1. **eNOS activation is a common convergence point:** ginseng (Rg1), Astragalus (AST IV), and kudzu (puerarin) phosphorylate eNOS-Ser1177 through PI3K/Akt; tanshinone IIA increases eNOS expression. Together they support “qi tonification = greater capacity for NO synthesis.”
2. **NO protection is the essence of “blood activation”:** Danshen (salvianolic acid B), Sanqi (antiplatelet action), and Chuanxiong (calcium antagonism) protect already-generated NO through antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antiplatelet actions, preventing O₂−-mediated conversion to ONOO−. “Invigorating blood” thus means improving NO bioavailability.
3. **AMPK/SIRT1/NAD+ coupling:** APS and ginsenosides activate AMPK→SIRT1→eNOS, forming positive feedback with the NAD+ longevity network and overlapping mechanistically with endothelial benefits of calorie restriction and exercise (see 02-vascular/02-dietary-nitrate.md).
4. **Telomeres and immunosenescence:** Astragaloside IV increases TERT, while APS bidirectionally regulates immunity, directly corresponding to telomere attrition and immunosenescence among the 12 hallmarks of aging.
5. **The double-edged-sword principle:** all such herbs should follow the NO paradox of “protection at low levels, nitrosative stress at high levels” (see 01-foundations/03-no-basics.md). The goal is not unlimited NO elevation, but **restoration of physiological eNOS activity and suppression of excessive iNOS expression**—the immune-regulating action of Astragalus exemplifies “regulate, rather than simply supplement.”

## Intervention directions and actionable suggestions

1. **Prioritize combinations with the strongest mechanistic rationale:** ginseng + Danshen + Sanqi (Shen-Dan-Qi) form a complete NO-pathway chain—upstream activation (ginseng), preservation of activity/antioxidation (Danshen), and downstream antithrombotic action (Sanqi)—and provide a molecular basis for qi-tonifying/blood-activating formulas. Consider formula products (e.g., compound Danshen dripping pills or Tongxinluo) rather than indiscriminate use of single herbs.
2. **Poor oral bioavailability is the main limitation:** saponins and phenolic acids are poorly absorbed orally. Clinical effects may depend on prolonged regular use or intravenous administration (only under medical guidance and appropriate indications); the value of taking large amounts of saponin supplements without supervision is questionable.
3. **Safety first:** blood-activating herbs (Sanqi, Danshen, Ginkgo, Angelica) combined with anticoagulants/antiplatelets (aspirin, clopidogrel, warfarin, direct oral anticoagulants) entail **additive bleeding risk** and require monitoring of INR/bleeding tendency. Long-term high-dose ginseng can cause “ginseng abuse syndrome” (hypertension, insomnia, palpitations); Astragalus and other qi-tonifying herbs may be unsuitable for patterns described as excess heat or yin deficiency with fire.
4. **Alternative/adjunct strategies:** in people without bleeding risk, mild blood-activating herbs such as Sanqi/Danshen may be used as adjuncts to aspirin. Combine with exercise (shear stress→eNOS) and dietary nitrate (leafy greens, beetroot; see 02-vascular/02-dietary-nitrate.md) for potential synergy.
5. **“Regulate rather than supplement”:** the goal is to restore physiological eNOS function and NO bioavailability, not simply raise NO; avoid nitrosative stress from high concentrations of iNOS-derived NO.

## Open questions and controversies

1. **The constituent–whole-herb gap:** isolated-compound mechanisms are robust, but bioavailability, metabolism, and targeting of extracts/whole herbs remain unclear. Whether the active ingredient is the parent saponin or a gut-derived metabolite is unresolved.
2. **Dose-response and boundaries of bidirectional regulation:** the doses and physiological states at which ginseng shifts between stimulation and calming, Astragalus between immune enhancement and suppression, or Sanqi between improved circulation and anticoagulation are not precisely quantified.
3. **Optimal balance between antiplatelet activity and bleeding risk:** large prospective data are lacking on how to balance PNS/Danshen antithrombotic efficacy and safety and on the therapeutic window when combined with anticoagulants.
4. **Lack of hard-endpoint evidence:** nearly all human trials of single herbs/extracts use surrogate endpoints (NO, FMD, inflammatory markers), with few large, long-term RCTs using cardiovascular death, stroke, or healthspan as endpoints.
5. **Quality control and batch variation:** origin, processing, and extraction methods produce substantial differences in constituent profiles, making reproducibility across batches uncertain—a structural challenge for evidence-based evaluation of Chinese medicine.
6. **Molecularizing qi-tonifying/blood-stasis pattern classification:** it is not established whether ADMA (an endogenous eNOS inhibitor), NO metabolites, or FMD can serve as biomarkers to guide treatment (see 06-tcm/01-yiqi-huoxue.md).
