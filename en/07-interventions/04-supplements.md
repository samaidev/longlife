# Supplement Evaluation: An Evidence-Based Framework and Individualized Assessment of Anti-Aging Supplements

> Supplements occupy a gray area between drugs and food: the market is vast, regulation is weak, claims are exaggerated, yet a small number of products do have RCT-level evidence. This review does not offer a “cure-all tonic” regimen. Instead, it establishes a five-step assessment framework—**evidence level → safety window → dose → interactions → priority**—and examines NAD+ precursors, resveratrol, quercetin, curcumin, omega-3s, vitamin D, coenzyme Q10, taurine, urolithin A, spermidine, antioxidants, and trace elements, including how they connect with the NO/NAD+ longevity network.

---

## 1. Core Concepts

The essence of supplement evaluation is to strip “dietary supplements” of their marketing narrative and assess them using the standards of clinical epidemiology. Most anti-aging supplements act along three mechanistic axes: the **NAD+/sirtuin axis** (NMN, NR, nicotinamide), the **redox and mitochondrial axis** (CoQ10, lipoic acid, urolithin A, taurine), and the **inflammation and vascular axis** (omega-3s, curcumin, quercetin, nitrate). This resource takes the position that **the evidence level determines the strength of a recommendation, the safety window determines the upper dose limit, and an individual’s baseline (blood levels, medications, genotype) determines whether supplementation is worthwhile**. Animal experiments can provide “mechanistic clues”; human RCTs provide “evidence of benefit.” This is the recurring yardstick throughout.

## 2. Supplement Assessment Framework: Evidence Levels, Safety Windows, and Regulation

### 2.1 The Evidence Pyramid

| Evidence level | Definition | Representative examples | Recommendation strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| L1: Established | Large RCT/meta-analysis with hard clinical outcomes | REDUCE-IT (EPA reduces cardiovascular events), Q-SYMBIO (CoQ10 in heart failure) | ★★★★ |
| L2: Strong association | Medium-sized RCTs with surrogate outcomes (blood pressure, endothelial function, biomarkers) | Meta-analysis of magnesium for blood pressure, NR raises NAD+, nitrate lowers blood pressure | ★★★☆ |
| L3: Animal-supported | Lifespan/healthspan benefits in model organisms, with clear mechanisms | Taurine (Singh 2023), spermidine, urolithin A | ★★☆ |
| L4: Mechanistic speculation | In vitro/cellular data, not confirmed in vivo | Resveratrol and SIRT1 activation (original claim), quercetin alone as a senolytic | ★☆ |

**Key implication:** For L3/L4 supplements, a purchasing decision should be viewed as a “low-cost experimental investment,” not a guaranteed health return.

### 2.2 Safety Windows: The Two-Sided Dose–Response Cliff

Safety assessment must consider both the **lower threshold** (ineffectiveness) and the **upper threshold** (toxicity); only the range between them is the potential effective window. Typical examples:

- **Vitamin D:** 25(OH)D below 30 nmol/L constitutes deficiency (and is associated with elevated mortality risk), but prolonged intake above 10,000 IU/day may cause hypercalcemia and nephrocalcinosis. “More is better” does not apply.
- **Selenium:** The SELECT trial (Lippman 2009, n=35,533) found that 200 µg/day of selenium not only failed to prevent prostate cancer, but showed a trend toward increased risk (HR 1.13) and significantly increased diabetes risk. The **U-shaped curve of nutrients** is central to evaluating supplements.
- **Nicotinamide (NAM):** At low doses, it is a substrate for the NAD+ salvage pathway; at high doses, however, it is an **inhibitor of SIRT1** (NAM is a product inhibitor of the sirtuin-catalyzed reaction). Doses above 500 mg/day may be counterproductive.

### 2.3 Regulatory Reality: DSHEA and “Buyer Beware”

The U.S. Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act (DSHEA, 1994) stipulates that supplements **do not require FDA approval before marketing**; manufacturers bear responsibility for safety, and the adverse-event reporting system (serious events have been mandatorily reportable since 2007) is highly incomplete. Commercial products face three kinds of risk: **adulteration** (for example, a 2015 New York Attorney General DNA barcoding study found that 79% of 39 herbal supplements tested did not contain the labeled ingredients), **contamination** (heavy metals, pesticides, prohibited drugs such as sildenafil analogues), and **dose discrepancies** (measured ingredient content can range from 0–200% of the label claim). Practical countermeasures: choose brands tested by third parties (USP, NSF, ConsumerLab), avoid “all-in-one” proprietary blends, and prefer single-ingredient products so effects can be attributed.

## 3. NAD+ Precursors: NMN / NR / Nicotinamide (A Core Theme of This Resource)

### 3.1 The Complete Mechanistic Chain

```
Dietary precursors (NR/NMN/NAM)
      │
      ▼
Salvage pathway: NAM ──NAMPT (rate-limiting, declines with age)──► NMN ──NMNAT1-3──► NAD⁺
                  NR ──NRK1/2──► NMN ────────────────────────────┘
                  NMN ──(dephosphorylation → mainly enters cells as NR;
                         Slc12a8 transporter disputed)────────────┘
      │
      ▼
NAD⁺ pool ↑ ──► SIRT1 activity ↑ ──deacetylation──► PGC-1α↑ (mitochondrial biogenesis)
                                             ├─► FOXO↑ (antioxidant defense: MnSOD/Catalase)
                                             ├─► NF-κB↓ (anti-inflammatory: TNF-α/IL-6↓)
                                             └─► eNOS↑ (K496/K506 deacetylation → NO↑)
                                        ├─► competes with CD38/PARP consumption
                                        │   (CD38 rises with age and sequesters NAD⁺)
                                        └─► phenotype: mitochondrial function↑,
                                            vasodilation↑, metabolism↑
```

Tissue NAD+ declines by roughly 30–50% with age (markedly in muscle and brain, while the liver is relatively spared). The main causes are the combined effects of declining NAMPT activity, upregulated CD38, and PARP activation. The rationale for precursor supplementation is to **relieve a rate-limiting step**, but higher NAD+ is only an intermediate; the ultimate question is whether SIRT1 substrate function and clinical outcomes improve.

### 3.2 Details of Human RCT Evidence

| Study | Design | NAD+ increase | Main findings |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trammell 2016 (Nat Commun) | NR 100–1000 mg/day, single dose | 1000 mg raised blood NAD+ by about 60% | Dose-dependent; good safety profile |
| Martens 2018 (Nat Commun) | NR 1 g/day × 12 weeks (older adults) | About 60–90% increase | Improved blood NAD+ metabolite profile; limited improvement in functional endpoints |
| Airhart 2017 | NR 250–1000 mg/day | Dose-dependent | No clear anti-inflammatory or functional benefit observed |
| Yoshino 2021 (Science) | NMN 250 mg/day × 10 weeks (postmenopausal women with prediabetes) | Skeletal muscle NAD+ rose about 25% | **Improved muscle insulin sensitivity** (increased glycogen synthesis) |
| Igarashi 2022 | NMN 250 mg/day × 12 weeks (healthy older men) | Blood NAD+ increased | Improvement in physical measures such as grip strength and gait speed (small sample) |

**Consensus:** Oral NR/NMN can safely raise blood/tissue NAD+ by 1.5–2-fold in a dose-dependent manner. However, benefits for **functional endpoints** (insulin sensitivity, exercise performance, cognition) are inconsistent; positive results mostly come from small samples and short durations. NR’s absorption mechanism is clear (direct cellular entry via ENT transporters). The hypothesis that NMN is directly transported by Slc12a8 (Grozio 2019) has not been replicated; the mainstream view is “extracellular dephosphorylation → NR → cellular entry.”

### 3.3 The Central Controversy: Raising NAD+ ≠ Anti-Aging Benefit

Three unanswered issues: ① High CD38 expression in older tissues may “intercept” newly synthesized NAD+, such that a substantial increase in tissue levels may require combined CD38 inhibition (animal data; not validated in humans); ② the **“feeding cancer” hypothesis**—NAD+ may enhance PARP/SIRT1-dependent DNA repair and proliferation and, in theory, support tumors. There is currently no human causal evidence, but long-term trials are lacking; ③ high-dose nicotinamide inhibits SIRT1, while nicotinic acid (niacin) raises NAD+ but HPS2-THRIVE (2014) showed that its LDL reduction did not translate into fewer events and adverse effects increased—**different precursors are not interchangeable**. See 01-foundations/04-nad-sirtuin.md.

## 4. Resveratrol: The Debate over SIRT1 Activation

### 4.1 From “Direct Activation” to “PDE Inhibition”: A Paradigm Shift

In 2003, Howitz and colleagues reported that resveratrol directly activated SIRT1 in vitro (Nature); in 2006, Baur and colleagues showed that it improved health in high-fat-diet mice (Nature), igniting the “red wine anti-aging” boom. But **Park 2012 (Cell)** made a disruptive discovery: resveratrol’s AMPK activation did not occur through direct binding to SIRT1, but through **inhibition of cAMP phosphodiesterase PDE4 → cAMP↑ → Epac1 → CaMKKβ → AMPK**. The in-vitro concentration required to activate SIRT1 (about 50–100 µM) was also far higher than actual tissue concentrations after oral intake. Although subsequent work (e.g., Hubbard 2013) offered evidence for direct activation using fluorescent substrates, the mainstream consensus has shifted to “resveratrol is a weak, multi-target compound; direct SIRT1 activation remains doubtful.”

### 4.2 Bioavailability Is a Fundamental Weakness

Oral resveratrol bioavailability is **<1%** (Walle 2004). Within minutes after ingestion it is cleared through intestinal and hepatic glucuronidation/sulfation; free plasma concentrations are in the nanomolar range, 2–3 orders of magnitude below concentrations effective in vitro. Human RCTs (metabolic syndrome, NAFLD, vascular function) have mixed results: some show improved FMD and inflammatory markers; most are neutral. Doses range from 150 mg/day to 1 g/day. **Conclusion:** Moderate intake as a polyphenol in red wine is not an issue, but buying it as a “SIRT1 activator” is not evidence-based. Any anti-aging value is more likely to come from an overall polyphenol-rich dietary pattern than from a single compound.

## 5. Quercetin and Senolytics: The D+Q Combination

Quercetin is a flavonoid antioxidant: it inhibits NF-κB (anti-inflammatory), partially inhibits PDE5 to raise cGMP, and mildly upregulates eNOS. As a **stand-alone drug**, human evidence is weak (a small meta-analysis suggests a blood-pressure reduction of about 2–3 mmHg); bioavailability is <5%, and plasma concentrations from ordinary formulations are far below effective levels. It entered the anti-aging spotlight mainly through the **senolytic combination D+Q**:

- Hickson 2019 (EBioMedicine): dasatinib 100 mg plus quercetin 1000 mg for 3 consecutive days reduced p16INK4a-positive senescent cells in skin and fat biopsies from patients with diabetic kidney disease;
- Justice 2019 (EBioMedicine): senescence markers declined after D+Q in patients with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis;
- Wissler Gerades 2024: 14 days of D+Q in kidney-transplant recipients confirmed senescent-cell clearance and good tolerability.

**Note:** The active senolytic component in D+Q is dasatinib (a prescription drug); quercetin is an adjunct (it inhibits PI3K/Akt and helps dasatinib clear senescent adipocytes). Buying quercetin alone cannot reproduce this regimen. High-dose quercetin has been associated with isolated cases of kidney toxicity and CYP-mediated drug interactions; it should be used in the context of clinical trials. See the dedicated Senolytics article.

## 6. Curcumin: An Anti-Inflammatory Star with a Bioavailability Problem

Curcumin acts as an anti-inflammatory by inhibiting NF-κB, COX-2, and TNF-α. Meta-analyses in arthritis show modest relief of pain and stiffness (moderate effect size); findings for LDL and CRP in metabolic-syndrome studies are inconsistent. **The core problem is pharmacokinetics:** poor oral absorption, extensive first-pass metabolism, and a short half-life leave plasma levels of ordinary curcumin in the nanomolar range. A classic solution was reported by **Shoba 1998 (Planta Med): coadministration with piperine (a black pepper compound) increased curcumin bioavailability by about 2000%**—but piperine also inhibits CYP3A4/UGT and may increase exposure to concomitant medications. Modern formulations (the phospholipid complex Meriva, Theracurmin nanoparticles, BCM-95) use different approaches to improve absorption, each supported by human pharmacokinetic data.

**Assessment:** Curcumin is a classic case where “the mechanism is clear, but the formulation determines success.” Clinical doses are 500–2000 mg/day (standardized curcuminoids); choose a high-bioavailability formulation. People not taking medications may use piperine to boost absorption. Those taking medications (especially anticoagulants, antihypertensives, or immunosuppressants) should prefer a formulation without piperine. Evidence level L2 (surrogate endpoints); there is no hard-endpoint anti-aging evidence.

## 7. Omega-3 Fatty Acids (EPA/DHA)

### 7.1 Mechanisms and Cardiovascular Evidence

EPA/DHA reduce triglycerides (dose-dependent; 4 g/day can reduce TG by about 25–30%), alter membrane fluidity, reduce pro-inflammatory eicosanoids (PGE2, LTB4), and upregulate eNOS. **REDUCE-IT (Bhatt 2019, NEJM):** highly purified EPA (icosapent ethyl) 4 g/day in 8,179 high-risk patients with elevated TG; major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) were 17.2% versus 22.0% (HR 0.75, RRR 25%). This is a rare positive hard-endpoint trial in the supplement field. Two caveats are important: ① the control group received mineral-oil placebo, prompting debate that the “control was harmful” (criticism by Oliveira 2020); ② **the STRENGTH trial (Nicholls 2020, JAMA)** tested 4 g/day EPA+DHA against corn oil and was **negative**—suggesting benefits may come mainly from EPA rather than DHA, and that results are highly dependent on placebo selection.

### 7.2 Risks and Doses

- **Atrial fibrillation risk:** In REDUCE-IT, atrial fibrillation occurred in 5.3% of the EPA group versus 3.9% of controls. Meta-analyses confirm an increased risk with high-dose omega-3s (absolute increase about 1–3%).
- Increased bleeding tendency (>3 g/day); mercury/PCB contamination in fish-derived products (molecular distillation is required for purification).
- **VITAL trial (Manson 2019, NEJM):** fish oil 1 g/day did not reduce cardiovascular events overall, but myocardial infarction fell by 28% in the subgroup with low baseline fish intake (HR 0.72)—again supporting the logic of **“correcting a shortfall.”**

**Conclusion:** For people with high TG or high cardiovascular risk, 1–4 g/day (prescription-grade EPA or high-purity EPA+DHA) has the strongest evidence (L1). For healthy people, eating deep-sea fish twice a week is preferable to supplements.

## 8. Vitamin D and K2

Vitamin D regulates 1,000+ genes through VDR: skeletal calcium metabolism, immunity (the antimicrobial peptide cathelicidin), and endothelial function (eNOS upregulation). Deficiency is widespread globally (severe deficiency, 25(OH)D <30 nmol/L, can affect 30%+ in high-latitude regions). **VITAL (2019):** 2000 IU/day × 5 years, n=25,871; cancer and major cardiovascular endpoints were both negative. Meta-analyses show that mortality benefits from supplementation are **almost entirely concentrated among people deficient at baseline** (all-cause mortality fell by about 25% in those <30 nmol/L; no benefit in those with adequate levels). Toxicity: prolonged intake >10,000 IU/day can cause hypercalcemia. **Practical approach:** measure 25(OH)D first; for deficiency, supplement 800–2000 IU/day to reach 30–50 ng/mL, then maintain.

Vitamin K2 (MK-7) activates matrix GLA protein and osteocalcin, theoretically directing calcium into bone and preventing vascular calcification, and may act synergistically with D3. However, evidence that D3+K2 prevents CAC progression is still mainly observational (e.g., the Rotterdam cohort); RCTs are lacking. **Risk:** K2 antagonizes warfarin (a vitamin K-dependent coagulation-factor antagonist); people on anticoagulants should not use it.

## 9. Coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10)

### 9.1 Mechanism and Evidence

CoQ10 is an electron carrier between complexes I/II and III of the mitochondrial electron-transport chain and a fat-soluble antioxidant (it regenerates vitamin E). **Q-SYMBIO (Mortensen 2014, JACC Heart Fail):** 420 patients with moderate-to-severe heart failure received CoQ10 100 mg three times daily for 2 years; major adverse cardiovascular events were 15% versus 26% (HR 0.50, p=0.003)—a positive hard-endpoint result, although the sample was small and larger confirmation is needed. Positive findings have also been reported for blood pressure and migraine prevention (200–300 mg/day; moderate evidence).

### 9.2 Statin Depletion: The Clearest “Correcting a Shortfall” Indication

Statins inhibit HMG-CoA reductase and block the mevalonate pathway—**the same pathway supplies precursors for CoQ10 synthesis**. Thus, long-term statin use lowers plasma CoQ10 by about 20–40%. RCT results for CoQ10 supplementation (200–400 mg/day) in patients with myopathy/myalgia are mixed (a Cochrane review did not confirm a benefit for muscle pain), but evidence of benefit is more substantial in **cardiomyopathy and heart-failure patients**. Absorption depends on bile and is improved when taken with a fatty meal; fat-soluble formulations (the ubiquinol form) have better bioavailability.

## 10. Carnosine and Taurine

**Taurine—the 2023 Science headline study:** Singh and colleagues found that taurine declines with age (consistently in mice, monkeys, and humans); chronic taurine deficiency was identified as one driver of aging. Supplementation increased median lifespan by about 10–12% in mice, with benefits also in worms and yeast, and improved bone density, muscle function, and immunity. **Human evidence is still at an early stage:** a 6-month supplementation RCT in older adults confirmed safety, while functional outcomes are still accumulating; observational data suggest an association between blood taurine levels and metabolic health. Doses of 1–3 g/day have a good safety record (FDA GRAS), and the cost is low—making taurine a **high-value “animal longevity candidate.”**

**Carnosine:** a β-alanyl-histidine dipeptide that clears AGEs (anti-glycation), chelates metals, and buffers pH. Animal studies and small human trials show possible improvement in cognition and physical performance in older adults, but RCTs are small and long-term data are lacking. Supplementation with β-alanine (a carnosine precursor) can raise carnosine levels; a common side effect is skin paresthesia (tingling).

## 11. Urolithin A and Spermidine: Mitophagy and Autophagy Axes

**Urolithin A (UA):** gut microbes produce it by metabolizing ellagic acid found in pomegranates and berries. **Ryu 2016 (Nature Medicine):** UA induces mitophagy, extends worm lifespan by about 45%, and improves muscle function in older mice. **Andreux 2019 (Nature Metabolism):** human RCTs—500/1000 mg/day × 4 weeks—raised mitophagy markers in muscle and lowered plasma acylcarnitines and ceramides (indirect evidence of improved mitochondrial function), with good safety. A subsequent 12-month RCT in older adults confirmed long-term tolerability and suggested a trend toward improved muscle strength. **Key limitation:** gut microbiota in about 30–40% of people cannot convert ellagic acid into UA (“non-responders”). Direct UA supplementation bypasses this bottleneck, making it a classic example for teaching about a “microbiome-dependent nutrient.”

**Spermidine:** a natural polyamine that induces **autophagy** (inhibits the EP300 acetyltransferase → upregulates autophagy-related genes). **Eisenberg 2016 (Nature Medicine):** oral spermidine extended mouse lifespan by about 10% and improved cardiac function. **Kiechl 2018 (Am J Clin Nutr):** people in the highest quintile of dietary spermidine intake (mainly from wheat germ, soy, and mushrooms) had significantly lower all-cause mortality (HR about 0.6), an observational finding. Human RCTs (wheat-germ extract 1–6 mg/day) show trends toward improved cognition and immune markers, but samples are small. Autophagy induction shares a rationale with mTOR inhibition and may complement fasting (see 04-drugs/06-mtor).

## 12. Lipoic Acid, Pycnogenol, and Other Antioxidants

**α-Lipoic acid (ALA):** an endogenous mitochondrial cofactor (α-KGDH/PDH). External supplementation can increase intracellular reduced ALA and regenerate glutathione and vitamins C/E. **Liu 2002 (PNAS):** combined ALA + acetyl-L-carnitine restored mitochondrial function and improved spatial memory in aged rats—a classic animal finding, but human RCT evidence is sparse. Use of 600 mg/day for diabetic neuropathy (α-lipoic acid is an approved indication in Germany, supported by the ALADIN series) is one of the few human records of benefit. **Pycnogenol (French maritime pine bark extract):** a mixture of procyanidins. Small RCTs report improved FMD, reduced platelet aggregation, and improvement in symptoms of venous insufficiency (the Belcaro series); evidence is weak L2 and trial quality varies. **General caution:** high-dose antioxidants (vitamin E ≥400 IU/day, β-carotene) failed to prevent cancer in large trials such as SELECT/ATBC; β-carotene even increased lung-cancer risk in smokers. The simplistic equation “antioxidant = anti-aging” has been disproven. Moderate fluctuations in redox signaling (hormesis) may be more beneficial than constant scavenging.

## 13. Trace Elements: Magnesium, Zinc, Selenium

| Element | Mechanism | Evidence | Dose and risks |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Magnesium** | Cofactor for 300+ enzymes; antagonizes calcium channels in vascular smooth muscle (relaxation); eNOS/NOS cofactor | Meta-analysis (Zhang 2016, Hypertension): about 370 mg/day lowered systolic BP by about 2.0 mmHg and diastolic BP by 1.8 mmHg; low magnesium strongly associated with cardiovascular mortality | Magnesium glycinate/citrate providing 200–400 mg/day elemental magnesium; risk of hypermagnesemia in renal impairment |
| **Zinc** | Cofactor for 300+ enzymes; immunity and SOD cofactor | AREDS2: 80 mg zinc + antioxidants slowed AMD progression by about 25% (L1, in older people with age-related macular degeneration) | Long-term intake >40 mg/day causes copper deficiency (anemia/neuropathy); increased urinary-tract infections in women |
| **Selenium** | Selenoproteins (GPx, thioredoxin reductase) | SELECT 2011: 200 µg/day did not prevent prostate cancer and increased diabetes risk; strong evidence that supplementation prevents Keshan disease in selenium-deficient regions | Dietary selenium is adequate in most regions of China; **routine supplementation is not recommended**. In deficient areas: 50–100 µg/day |

The principle for trace elements is **correct deficiencies, not maximize intake**. The U-shaped curve is clearest for selenium and zinc; risks from supraphysiologic supplementation can be comparable to those from deficiency.

## 14. Connection to the NO Pathway: Arginine / Citrulline / Nitrate

There are three direct supplement-to-NO pathways, all corresponding to 01-foundations/03-no-basics.md:

```
Pathway 1 (substrate): L-arginine → eNOS → NO (endothelium)
                      L-citrulline ──(conversion in kidneys)──► L-arginine
                      (bypass route, more efficient at raising plasma levels)
Pathway 2 (nitrate–nitrite): dietary NO₃⁻ ──oral microbiota──► NO₂⁻
                             ──(deoxyhemoglobin/xanthine oxidase)──► NO
Pathway 3 (NAD+–eNOS coupling): NMN/NR → NAD⁺ → SIRT1 → eNOS
                                deacetylation (K496/K506) → NO↑
```

- **L-arginine:** extensive first-pass degradation by intestinal arginase after oral intake (about 40–60% lost); 4–24 g/day may be required for a blood-pressure effect (meta-analysis: systolic BP about −5 mmHg). **L-citrulline 3 g/day raises plasma arginine more effectively than an equal dose of arginine** (Schwedhelm 2008), making it the smarter substrate strategy.
- **Dietary nitrate:** beetroot juice (about 6.4 mmol NO3−) lowers blood pressure by about 10/8 mmHg 2.5–3 hours after ingestion (Webb 2008, Hypertension); in athletes, it improves oxygen-use efficiency by about 16% (Bailey 2009)—**evidence for food-derived NO precursors is stronger than for most “supplements.”** Note: mouthwash kills oral bacteria that reduce nitrate and therefore blocks the pathway. Concerns about nitrosamine formation primarily apply to high-nitrate processed meats; nitrate from vegetables has a good safety record.
- **Supplements vs. food:** The nitrate/polyphenol example shows that the “food matrix” (matrix, microbiota, synergistic compounds) is often superior to purified isolates. This is the resource’s basic position on supplements: **food first, supplements second, and supplement only what is missing.**

## 15. Combination Strategies: What to Prioritize Based on Evidence

Based on the assessment above, here is a “supplement pyramid” (an individualized ranking, not suitable for everyone):

| Tier | Product | Appropriate for | Suggested dose | Rationale |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| T1: Established | Vitamin D | 25(OH)D <30 ng/mL (test first) | 800–2000 IU/day | Strongest evidence for correcting a shortfall |
| T1: Established | Fish oil/EPA | High TG, high cardiovascular risk, low fish intake | EPA 2–4 g/day | REDUCE-IT/VITAL |
| T1: Established | Magnesium | Inadequate intake/hypertension/constipation | 200–400 mg/day | Blood-pressure and metabolic effects; wide safety window |
| T2: Strong association | NMN/NR | Age 50+, seeking NAD+ replenishment | NR 300–1000 mg/day or NMN 250–1000 mg/day | Good mechanism and safety; functional endpoints remain uncertain |
| T2: Strong association | CoQ10 | Long-term statin use, heart failure | 200–400 mg/day (with a fatty meal) | Q-SYMBIO + depletion mechanism |
| T2: Strong association | Taurine | Almost everyone | 1–3 g/day | 2023 Science + very safe and inexpensive |
| T3: Exploratory | Urolithin A / spermidine / curcumin (high bioavailability) / quercetin (formulation-dependent) | Experimental self-investment | Follow product label | Attractive mechanisms; human endpoints unproven |

**Implementation principles:** ① Add only one product at a time; after 3–6 months, assess whether to continue using measurable indicators (blood pressure, lipids, 25(OH)D, NAD+, symptoms). ② With a limited budget, prioritize T1 over T2, and T2 over T3. ③ Check for drug interactions (warfarin–K2/fish oil; statin–CoQ10 as a complementary intervention; piperine–CYP3A4 substrates). ④ Avoid “all-in-one” proprietary blends.

### 15.1 New Developments in Supplement Research, 2025–2026

Recent trends and updates worth watching in the supplement field:

- **A shift in NAD⁺ precursor studies toward “functional endpoints”:** Human NMN/NR trials are moving from “raising NAD⁺ levels” toward functional outcomes—walking speed, grip strength, insulin sensitivity, and cognitive measures. RCTs are being published, but results are generally “directionally positive, small in effect size, and based on limited samples.” The consensus that **“raising NAD⁺ is not the same as anti-aging benefit” is becoming stronger**; large, multi-year trials are still needed (see the debate in Section 3.3).
- **Taurine enters a “phenomenon-level” validation phase:** The 2023 *Science* longevity study prompted extensive follow-up. Human RCTs (6–12 months, with functional endpoints) are being launched, while observational data continue to support an association between blood taurine and metabolic health. **If confirmed in large RCTs, taurine could rank among the best supplements across the three dimensions of “dose, cost, and safety.”**
- **The “responder” question for urolithin A:** A 12-month RCT in older adults confirmed long-term safety, while the advantage of direct UA supplementation for “non-responders” (people whose microbiota cannot produce UA) became clearer—**“microbiome-tailored supplementation” is emerging as a new paradigm** (test the microbiome → select a supplement).
- **AI and personalized supplementation:** Digital tools offering “supplement prescriptions” based on blood biomarkers (25(OH)D, magnesium, lipids, inflammation) are emerging, but most are unvalidated. **“Test-driven supplementation” is moving from concept to product, while the evidence base remains weak** (see open question 4 in Section 16).
- **Signs of tighter regulation:** Enforcement against false claims about “anti-aging supplements” is increasing in the U.S. and Europe (FDA warning letters, EU EFSA restrictions on health claims). **Consumer education that “mechanistic narratives ≠ clinical evidence” is an industry focus**, consistent with this resource’s “evidence pyramid” (see Section 2).

**Overall assessment:** The field is moving **from “more is better” to “test-driven, outcome-validated”**. There has been no new “star molecule”-level breakthrough, but personalization and evidence-based practice are steadily advancing. This resource’s position remains unchanged: **supplements are for “correcting shortfalls,” not for leading the anti-aging effort**. The mainstays remain exercise, sleep, diet, and metabolic health (see intervention priorities in [01-daily-checklist](01-daily-checklist.md)).

## 16. Open Questions and Controversies

1. **The endpoint paradox of NAD+ replenishment:** Raising NAD+ is certain, but whether it translates into lifespan/healthspan benefits requires large, multi-year trials; the “feeding cancer” hypothesis and CD38 interception issue remain unresolved.
2. **Lessons from the resveratrol paradigm shift:** The central mechanistic claim for a “star molecule” taken by thousands was rewritten in 2012. The reliability of “mechanistic narratives” in the supplement field should remain under scrutiny.
3. **Standardizing senolytic doses and regimens:** The three-day D+Q regimen is pulse-based; the safety of repeated long-term administration (including damage to normal tissues) is unknown.
4. **The personalization gap:** Comparative studies of blood-level-guided dosing (25(OH)D, magnesium, NAD+) versus one-size-fits-all doses are almost nonexistent. Microbiome differences (UA non-responders, abundance of nitrate-reducing bacteria) clearly invalidate the assumption that “the same dose produces the same effect.”
5. **Regulation and quality:** Under the DSHEA framework, adulteration and dose discrepancies are common; third-party testing is the only line of defense. Research funding for supplement–drug interactions is seriously inadequate.
