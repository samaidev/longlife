# Nutrition & Fasting: Evidence for Dietary Patterns and Dietary Restriction as Aging Interventions

> **Core thesis:** Nutrition is the first layer of anti-aging intervention—**what, how much, and when we eat** jointly determine the net output of nutrient-sensing networks (AMPK/mTOR/insulin–IGF-1), thereby regulating autophagy, mitochondrial quality, inflammation, and epigenetics. Caloric restriction (CR) repeatedly extends lifespan across evolutionary distance, from yeast to primates. Intermittent fasting (IF) partly reproduces CR benefits through “metabolic switching” coupled to circadian rhythms. Mediterranean and plant-based diets lower cardiovascular and all-cause mortality as dietary patterns rather than single nutrients. Protein intake presents a longevity paradox: high protein stimulates mTOR, whereas low protein suppresses IGF-1. This chapter reviews mechanisms, evidence grades, clinical details, and intersections with NO/NAD+ longevity networks (see 01-foundations/03-no-basics.md and 02-vascular/03-metabolic-aging.md).

## I. Nutrition fundamentals: macronutrients, micronutrients, and energy balance

### 1.1 Three nutrient-sensing pathways: how cells “read” the diet

Dietary information is translated into the activation or suppression of three core signaling pathways:

```text
[High intake]                         [Low intake/fasting]
   │                                       │
   ▼                                       ▼
Glucose ↑ → insulin ↑ → PI3K/Akt       AMP/ATP ratio ↑ → AMPK ↑
   │                                       ├─→ phosphorylates Raptor → mTORC1 inhibition
   ▼                                       ├─→ phosphorylates ULK1 → autophagy initiation
mTORC1 activation (amino acids + growth factors) ├─→ phosphorylates ACC → fatty-acid oxidation ↑
   ├─→ S6K1 → protein synthesis ↑              └─→ eNOS-Ser1177 phosphorylation → NO ↑
   ├─→ 4E-BP1 → enhanced translation             IGF-1 ↓ → FOXO nuclear translocation
   └─→ autophagy inhibition (ULK1 inactivation)  └─→ SOD/catalase, antioxidant defense ↑; autophagy ↑
```

**mTORC1 is the central nutrient–longevity hub:** Amino acids (especially leucine) recruit mTORC1 to lysosomal membranes through Rag GTPases; growth factors activate it through PI3K/Akt→TSC2→Rheb. mTORC1 activation promotes anabolism (protein, lipid, and nucleotide synthesis) and suppresses autophagy. Excessive activation is associated with cellular senescence, cancer, and insulin resistance. Conversely, mTORC1 inhibition (rapamycin, fasting, protein restriction) extends lifespan in many animals. In the Interventions Testing Program (ITP), rapamycin is so far the only compound shown to significantly extend lifespan in all three mouse genetic backgrounds tested (median lifespan increase ~10–18%, greater in females); mechanistically it partly mimics nutrient restriction (see relevant chapters in 04-drugs/).

**AMPK is an energy-deficit sensor:** Fasting/exercise raises the AMP/ATP ratio; AMP binds the AMPKγ subunit, and LKB1 phosphorylation activates AMPKα-Thr172. Activated AMPK phosphorylates Raptor to inhibit mTORC1, phosphorylates ULK1 to initiate autophagy, phosphorylates PGC-1α to promote mitochondrial biogenesis, and phosphorylates eNOS-Ser1177 to increase NO. AMPK and mTORC1 form an antagonistic accelerator/brake pair; a key goal of nutritional intervention is to shift their balance toward AMPK.

**Insulin/IGF-1 axis:** Persistent stimulation by high glucose and protein raises insulin and IGF-1, inhibiting FOXO transcription factors through PI3K/Akt. FOXO3a is a key longevity transcription factor (FOXO3 polymorphisms are associated with longevity in several centenarian cohorts). Its activation increases antioxidant enzymes (SOD2, catalase) and autophagy genes and suppresses inflammation.

### 1.2 Macronutrient composition and metabolic consequences

| Macronutrient | Energy (kcal/g) | Consequences of high intake | Role during restriction |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Carbohydrate | 4 | Glucose↑→insulin↑→lipogenesis; refined carbohydrate promotes inflammation | After ~12 h fasting, glycogen depletion→fat mobilization |
| Protein | 4 | Amino acids→mTORC1 activation (leucine is strongest) | Methionine/leucine restriction→FGF21↑ |
| Fat | 9 | Excess→lipotoxicity (ceramides, DAG→insulin resistance) | Ketones (β-hydroxybutyrate) act as signaling molecules |

**Key concept—“energy density vs nutrient density”:** Anti-aging nutrition emphasizes nutrient density (micronutrients and phytochemicals per calorie). At equal calories, ultra-processed foods (UPF) and whole foods have very different effects on insulin, inflammation, and microbiota. Cohort studies using the NOVA classification find that each 10% increase in UPF intake is associated with roughly 10–15% higher all-cause mortality; proposed mechanisms include glycemic fluctuation, microbiome changes, and advanced glycation end-products (AGEs).

### 1.3 Micronutrients: underappreciated aging regulators

- **NAD+ precursors:** Nicotinamide (Nam), nicotinamide riboside (NR), and nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) produce NAD+ through the salvage pathway, whose rate-limiting enzyme is NAMPT (see Section XII).
- **Magnesium:** A cofactor for over 300 enzymes, participating in DNA repair and telomere maintenance. US NHANES data suggest about half of adults have inadequate intake; low magnesium is associated with higher inflammatory markers (CRP).
- **Vitamin D:** Deficiency is common with age and is associated with sarcopenia and immunosenescence. Supplementation benefits have generally been limited in RCTs (VITAL did not show major cardiovascular/cancer benefit).
- **Dietary nitrate:** Nitrate in beetroot and spinach follows the oral-microbiome-dependent nitrate→nitrite→NO pathway, providing an eNOS-independent bypass (see 02-vascular/02-dietary-nitrate.md).
- **Polyphenols:** Curcumin, quercetin, EGCG catechins, and anthocyanins have antioxidant effects, activate AMPK/SIRT1, and modulate the microbiome (see the “five colors” section of 06-tcm/04-food-medicine.md).

**Principle:** Obtain micronutrients primarily from food (dietary patterns outperform supplements; most supplement RCTs are negative, and beta-carotene supplements even increased lung-cancer risk in smokers). Supplement selectively only when testing confirms a deficiency.

## II. Mediterranean diet: PREDIMED and cardiovascular benefit

### 2.1 Definition and components

The Mediterranean diet (MedDiet) uses olive oil as its main fat, with abundant vegetables, fruit, and legumes; whole grains; moderate fish and poultry; little red meat and dairy; and moderate wine. Its anti-aging effects arise from multiple components acting together: monounsaturated oleic acid lowers LDL, omega-3 fatty acids (EPA/DHA) are anti-inflammatory, polyphenols are antioxidants, fiber is prebiotic, and AGE exposure is low.

### 2.2 PREDIMED: a landmark in primary cardiovascular prevention

PREDIMED (*Prevención con Dieta Mediterránea*) is among the largest RCTs of dietary patterns: 7,447 Spanish adults at high cardiovascular risk (ages 55–80) were randomized to MedDiet plus extra-virgin olive oil (EVOO, 50 mL/day), MedDiet plus mixed nuts (30 g/day), or a low-fat control diet. Interim analysis (median follow-up 4.8 years) found:

- Major composite endpoint (MI, stroke, cardiovascular death): **30% lower** with MedDiet+EVOO vs control (HR 0.70, 95% CI 0.54–0.92), and **28% lower** with MedDiet+nuts (HR 0.72, 95% CI 0.54–0.96); the trial stopped early.
- Stroke risk fell more markedly (EVOO HR 0.67; nuts HR 0.54).
- A 2022 PREDIMED follow-up analysis (median follow-up 8.1 years) confirmed that some benefit persisted after the intervention; participants with higher **SIRT1 expression** benefited more, suggesting partial SIRT1 mediation. Mechanistic studies indicate that hydroxytyrosol, an olive-oil polyphenol, can directly activate SIRT1/PGC-1α.
- Post-hoc analyses also reported lower atrial-fibrillation risk (~20–38%), lower breast-cancer risk in the nut group (HR 0.59), and improved cognitive-decline trajectories in a cognitive subgroup, especially in the EVOO group.

### 2.3 Mechanisms: anti-inflammation, antioxidation, and endothelial protection

```text
Olive-oil polyphenols (hydroxytyrosol) → SIRT1↑ / NF-κB↓ → anti-inflammatory
Omega-3 (EPA/DHA) → altered eicosanoid profile → pro-resolving mediators (resolvins)↑ → anti-inflammatory
Dietary fiber → microbial fermentation → SCFAs (butyrate)↑ → GPR41/43 → anti-inflammatory, gut barrier↑
Vegetable nitrate → NO bypass → endothelial function↑, blood pressure↓
Polyphenols → inhibit LDL oxidation → block an early step in atherosclerosis
```

- Randomized crossover trials show 1–2% improvement in brachial-artery flow-mediated dilation (FMD) after MedDiet intervention, consistent with improved endothelial function.
- CRP, IL-6, and IL-1β consistently decline in MedDiet interventions; oxidized LDL decreases.

**Evidence grade: established (human RCTs).** Reduction in cardiovascular events with Mediterranean dietary patterns is among the strongest causal findings in nutritional epidemiology and was adopted as a recommended pattern by the 2015 US Dietary Guidelines Advisory Committee.

## III. Anti-inflammatory diets: DASH and the Dietary Inflammatory Index

### 3.1 DASH diet

DASH (Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension) emphasizes fruit and vegetables, low-fat dairy, whole grains, sodium restriction (2,300→1,500 mg/day), and reduced red meat. In the DASH trial (459 people with prehypertension/stage-1 hypertension; eight-week crossover RCT), systolic pressure fell **5.5 mmHg** vs control (11.4 mmHg in the hypertensive subgroup), an effect approaching that of a single antihypertensive drug. Long-term cohorts, including the Nurses’ Health Study, associate DASH adherence with lower cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes risk.

### 3.2 Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII) and aging

The Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII) scores 45 foods/nutrients by pro- or anti-inflammatory weighting. Prospective cohorts, including PREDIMED participants, consistently show that higher (more pro-inflammatory) DII is associated with higher CRP and IL-6 and greater cardiovascular-event, cancer, and all-cause mortality risks. High DII is also associated with accelerated epigenetic age (Horvath clock), though this remains observational and sample sizes are moderate.

**Core anti-inflammatory food list:** dark berries (anthocyanins), turmeric/ginger (curcumin/gingerols), omega-3-rich fish, olive oil, green tea (EGCG), and cruciferous vegetables (sulforaphane activates Nrf2→HO-1 for anti-inflammatory/antioxidant effects). Avoid refined sugar, trans fats, UPFs, and excessive red meat and alcohol.

### 3.3 Connection to NO

Anti-inflammatory diets reduce NF-κB-driven inflammation and abnormal induction of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS). During inflammation, iNOS produces abundant NO that reacts with superoxide to form peroxynitrite (ONOO−), creating the “NO paradox”: eNOS-derived NO protects vessels, whereas excessive iNOS-derived NO can cause oxidative injury (see 01-foundations/03-no-basics.md). Anti-inflammatory diets may improve NO bioavailability by restoring eNOS/iNOS balance and suppressing NOX oxidases (e.g., through quercetin and other polyphenols).

## IV. Protein and longevity: the high- vs low-protein paradox

### 4.1 Amino-acid sensing by mTOR: protein’s “double edge”

Protein is the only essential macronutrient that strongly activates mTORC1; leucine and arginine are potent signals for lysosomal mTORC1 recruitment. This creates a central paradox:

- **Short-/middle-aged perspective:** Protein insufficiency (<0.8 g/kg/day) increases sarcopenia, frailty, fracture, and infection risks. Older adults have anabolic resistance and may require a higher intake threshold (1.2–1.6 g/kg/day) to maintain muscle protein synthesis.
- **Longevity perspective:** Animal studies consistently show that low-protein diets, especially low-methionine diets, extend lifespan and reduce IGF-1 and cancer risk. An NHANES cohort analysis (Levine 2014) associated high protein intake (>1.2 g/kg/day) in people aged 50–65 with higher all-cause and cancer mortality (HR about 1.75 and 4.33, respectively; mechanistic interpretation remains uncertain), while this association reversed after age 65.

**Possible reconciliation of the “protein paradox”:** age-dependent protein requirements, differences in protein source (plant vs animal), and total energy intake. Plant proteins (legumes) have a lower branched-chain amino-acid ratio and come with fiber/polyphenols; their net mTOR stimulation may be lower than that of an equal amount of animal protein, one possible mechanism for plant-based dietary longevity benefits.

### 4.2 Animal evidence for protein restriction (PR) and methionine restriction (MR)

- **Methionine restriction:** In rats/mice, reducing methionine intake to 20–30% of normal extends lifespan by 20–40%, independently of caloric intake (animals eat freely). Proposed mechanisms include FGF21 elevation, increased H₂S generation through CSE, mTORC1 inhibition, and mitochondrial ROS adaptation (mitohormesis).
- **Protein restriction and IGF-1:** Low-protein diets reduce growth-hormone/IGF-1-axis activity; human mechanistic studies report an approximately 25% IGF-1 decrease after seven days of low protein. Low IGF-1 is associated with longevity in multiple model organisms.
- **Amino-acid imbalance:** Restricting leucine or BCAAs alone mimics some low-protein lifespan benefits in flies/mice, suggesting amino-acid quality (BCAA ratio) may matter more than total protein.

### 4.3 Practical guidance: stratify by timing and dose

| Population | Suggested protein (g/kg/day) | Rationale |
|---|---:|---|
| Healthy adults under 65 | 1.0–1.2 | Balance muscle maintenance and mTOR/IGF-1 signaling |
| Over 65 / sarcopenia risk | 1.2–1.6 (across 3–4 meals) | Address anabolic resistance and prevent frailty |
| Aggressive anti-aging strategy | Periodic fluctuation between 1.6 and 0.8 | “Protein cycling” hypothesis (weak evidence) |

**Conclusion:** Protein should not simply be minimized; aim for “enough, not excessive; prioritize plant sources; coordinate with training and meal timing.”

## V. Plant-based eating and longevity: Blue Zones and legumes

### 5.1 Shared dietary features of the Blue Zones

The Buettner team’s summary of five long-lived regions (Okinawa, Japan; Sardinia, Italy; Nicoya, Costa Rica; Ikaria, Greece; and Loma Linda, USA) includes:

- **95–100% plant-based** diets, with beans, lentils, chickpeas, and soy as core protein sources. Soy foods (tofu, miso) traditionally provided nearly half the protein in older Okinawans’ diets.
- At least three servings of vegetables and fruit daily, a handful of nuts, and whole grains as staples.
- Meat fewer than five times a month, small amounts of fish, and almost no UPFs.
- Eating until about 80% full (Okinawan “hara hachi bu”), essentially a mild form of CR.
- One or two alcoholic drinks daily in some areas (Sardinian/Ikaria wine, Cannonau rich in polyphenols). **Note:** the J-shaped alcohol–cancer relationship is disputed; alcohol is not recommended here.

### 5.2 Evidence for legumes

- Okinawan cohorts: tofu intake is inversely associated with cardiovascular mortality, though traditional-diet confounding is substantial.
- Pooled analysis of six global cohorts (PURE 2017, 130,000 people): legumes were inversely associated with all-cause mortality; four or more servings weekly corresponded to ~22% lower mortality (HR 0.78).
- Mechanisms: low BCAA proportion in legume protein→weaker mTOR stimulation; fiber→SCFAs; soy isoflavones→weak estrogen-like/antioxidant effects and microbiome modulation; folate→lower homocysteine→endothelial protection.

### 5.3 Vegetarian diets and all-cause mortality

EPIC-Oxford (~50,000 Britons) and Adventist Health Study-2 (~96,000 Americans) report lower all-cause mortality, ischemic heart disease, type 2 diabetes, and some cancer risks among vegetarians/vegans than non-vegetarians (vegetarian ischemic-heart-disease risk ~20–25% lower). However, vegetarians may be deficient in vitamin B12, iron, calcium, and omega-3. B12 deficiency raises homocysteine and can damage endothelium—**plant-based diets require micronutrient planning**.

## VI. Caloric restriction (CR): lifespan evidence from yeast to primates

### 6.1 Model-organism evidence: evolutionary conservation

| Species | Typical lifespan extension | Note |
|---|---:|---|
| Yeast (*S. cerevisiae*) | 30–40% | Early systematic evidence predating McCay’s 1935 work |
| Nematode (*C. elegans*) | 30–50% | Depends on daf-16/FOXO and sir-2.1/SIRT1 homologues |
| Fruit fly | 20–40% | Involves AMPK and SIRT1 homologues |
| Mouse/rat | 20–50% (large strain variation) | Effects vary by sex and strain |
| Rhesus macaque | See below (controversial) | Wisconsin vs NIA results differ |

CR extends lifespan across the evolutionary span from yeast to primates, making it one of the most robustly supported anti-aging interventions in animals. The magnitude depends on strain, sex, age at onset, and degree (10–40%); not all strains benefit (in some inbred mice CR shortens life, reportedly in association with immune stress).

### 6.2 Rhesus-monkey trials: the “great divergence” between Wisconsin and NIA

The apparently conflicting conclusions of two rhesus-macaque CR trials can be explained by study differences:

| Feature | Wisconsin (UW–Madison, began 1989) | NIA (NIH, began 1987) |
|---|---|---|
| CR amount | 30% | 30% |
| Control diet | Ad libitum (some animals obese) | Quantitatively fed; controls relatively lean |
| Starting age | Adults (7–14 years) | Various ages, including young and old |
| Result | Significant reduction in age-related mortality (HR ~0.62); lower cancer, cardiovascular disease, and diabetes incidence | No significant all-cause mortality difference (controls were also relatively lean) |
| Interpretation | Large benefit relative to standard American-style ad-libitum feeding | Limited added benefit relative to a healthy, portion-controlled diet |

**Consensus:** CR protects macaques from obesity-related disease and some age-related mortality. Part of the benefit comes from preventing overconsumption: net CR effect = anti-obesity + independent metabolic adaptation (AMPK/autophagy/ketones). For humans, this indicates that total-energy control and healthy dietary composition cannot be separated.

### 6.3 CALERIE: the landmark human RCT of healthy-adult CR

CALERIE (Comprehensive Assessment of Long-term Effects of Reducing Intake of Energy) is the only long-term randomized human CR trial to date: 218 healthy adults with normal to mildly overweight BMI (22–28), aged 21–50, randomized to a 25% CR target or ad-libitum control for two years.

Results (Ravussin 2015; Redman 2018):

- Achieved CR was about **12%** (below the 25% target, but excellent for a long-term RCT). Weight fell about **7.5–8 kg** (~9–10%), approximately 71% of which was fat.
- **Oxidative stress declined:** 8-isoprostane F2α (8-iso-PGF2α, lipid-peroxidation marker) fell about **21%** in the CR group vs control, suggesting reduced oxidative damage.
- Resting metabolic rate (RMR) fell about **5–10% more than predicted by weight loss** (“metabolic adaptation”/energy-conservation phenotype)—potentially a longevity-related energy-saving mechanism, but it may also hinder long-term weight maintenance.
- Insulin sensitivity (HOMA-IR and hyperinsulinemic clamp) improved significantly; circulating T3 decreased and nocturnal core temperature fell ~0.2°C, resembling long-lived animal phenotypes.
- Systemic inflammatory markers (TNF-α, CRP) decreased; circadian transcriptomes were remodeled.
- **Aging biomarkers:** thymic function was preserved and telomere attrition was not accelerated. Post-hoc analyses suggested a 2–3% slower rate of DNA-methylation aging clocks (PhenoAge/GrimAge), requiring confirmation in larger samples.
- At two-year follow-up, about 90% of weight loss was maintained, suggesting CR can be sustainable.

**Significance of CALERIE 2:** It first showed that healthy, non-obese adults can sustain moderate CR long term and exhibit multidimensional improvements in oxidative stress, inflammation, and metabolic adaptation. It supports mechanisms for human CR benefits, but two years cannot establish a lifespan effect.

### 6.4 CR dose-response and adverse effects

- **Dose:** 10–30% restriction can be effective; 20–30% may have clearer effects but lower adherence. Above 30% in humans risks malnutrition, sarcopenia, reduced sex hormones (lower testosterone), lower bone density, menstrual disruption, cold intolerance, and mood problems.
- CR should be paired with adequate protein (to prevent sarcopenia), micronutrient sufficiency, and resistance training.
- **Fasting vs CR:** CR continuously limits total intake; IF limits the eating window but does not necessarily reduce total intake. Their metabolic adaptations partly overlap (AMPK/ketones), but IF preserves satiety and social meals within the eating window and may be easier to adhere to (next section).

## VII. Intermittent fasting (IF): 5:2, 16:8, alternate-day fasting, and circadian rhythm

### 7.1 Types of IF

| Protocol | Structure | Adherence | Evidence strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| TRE 16:8 | 8-hour daily eating window (e.g., 12:00–20:00) | High | Moderate–high (many human RCTs) |
| 5:2 | Two very-low-calorie days/week (500–600 kcal), five normal days | Moderate | Moderate (similar to daily CR) |
| Alternate-day fasting (ADF) | Alternating fasting/eating days (fast day 0–500 kcal) | Low–moderate | Moderate (weight/metabolic improvements) |
| Modified ADF | 25% of energy on fasting days | Moderate | Moderate |
| 24-hour fast (Eat-Stop-Eat) | One or two continuous 24-hour fasts/week | Low | Low–moderate |

### 7.2 Metabolic switching: molecular basis of IF

After 10–12 hours of fasting, liver glycogen is depleted, fat mobilization accelerates, and hepatic ketogenesis (β-hydroxybutyrate, BHB, and acetoacetate) rises. By 16–18 hours, significant ketosis may occur. This **metabolic switch** from glucose to ketones is central to IF’s proposed benefits. BHB:

- inhibits histone deacetylases HDAC1/3 (epigenetic regulation);
- activates FOXO3a and Nrf2 antioxidant transcription;
- inhibits the NLRP3 inflammasome;
- acts as a ligand for GPR109A (HCAR2);
- may be a “cleaner” fuel than glucose, generating less ROS per ATP (mechanistic evidence).

### 7.3 Circadian rhythm: the “chrononutrition” basis for TRE

Nearly all mammalian tissues have clocks driven by CLOCK/BMAL1. **Meal timing is a stronger synchronizer of biological clocks than meal content**:

```text
Time-restricted eating (TRE) → synchronizes hypothalamic SCN master clock and liver/gut peripheral clocks
   ├─→ Liver: corrects gluconeogenic-enzyme (e.g., PEPCK) phase → stable fasting glucose
   ├─→ Adipose tissue: aligns lipolysis rhythm with eating window → fat oxidation ↑
   ├─→ Gut: microbial rhythms (butyrate producers fluctuate with meal timing) → barrier/immunity
   └─→ Muscle: aligns glucose uptake rhythm with exercise/feeding → insulin sensitivity ↑
```

**Cost of late eating:** Insulin sensitivity is at its circadian nadir at night, so the same meal causes a higher postprandial glucose curve. Shift-worker cohorts (circadian inversion plus night eating) show higher metabolic-syndrome, obesity, and cancer risks. A core TRE mechanism is placing meals within the circadian period of greater metabolic efficiency.

### 7.4 Summary of human evidence

- **Weight/body fat:** Many 8–16-week 16:8 TRE RCTs report 3–5% weight loss (~2–4 kg), similar to daily CR; some report less muscle loss, potentially because protein intake is concentrated within the eating window. ADF weight loss is often ~5–8%.
- **Insulin sensitivity:** TRE RCTs (e.g., Sutton 2019, eight-week early TRE) report ~25% improvement measured by clamp, even without weight change; glucose AUC decreases.
- **Blood pressure:** TRE studies report systolic reductions of ~5–10 mmHg in some trials; 5:2 trials (e.g., Mattson series) show improvements comparable to CR.
- **Inflammation/oxidative stress:** ADF studies report CRP, TNF-α, and 8-iso-PGF2α reductions (~20–30% in mechanistic studies) and lower oxidized LDL.
- **Lipids:** Most studies show a small LDL decrease or no change, lower triglycerides, and variable HDL; overall effects are weaker than those of Mediterranean diets.
- **Cognition:** Animal evidence is strong (improved hippocampal plasticity, BDNF↑); early human studies suggest memory/executive-function improvement but have small samples.
- **Controversial analysis:** A 2022 American Heart Association meeting abstract (NHANES, ~20,000 adults) reported an association between an eight-hour eating window and higher cardiovascular mortality. It was observational, did not adjust for all confounding (e.g., shift work/comorbidity), and was not a complete peer-reviewed publication; it cannot overturn RCT evidence. It does, however, suggest that both timing and food content matter.

### 7.5 Sex differences: IF benefits may be attenuated in women

- Animal studies: female mice show weaker lifespan benefits from IF/CR than males, possibly involving reproductive axes and estrogen–leptin interactions.
- Human observations: some women experience menstrual irregularities, lower thyroid hormone (T3), higher cortisol, and anxiety during IF—possibly reflecting greater sensitivity to energy deficit because reproductive function is prioritized.
- Practical suggestion: women may start with gentler 12:12 or 14:10 windows; 5:2 may be better tolerated than ADF. Adjust immediately if menstruation or mood worsens.

## VIII. Molecular overview of fasting

### 8.1 Upstream: energy sensing and signal integration

```text
Fasting/CR
   ├─→ AMP/ATP↑ → AMPK↑ → Raptor phosphorylation → mTORC1↓ → S6K1↓ → protein synthesis↓
   │                         └→ ULK1 phosphorylation → autophagy initiation
   ├─→ NAD+/NADH ratio↑ → SIRT1↑ → PGC-1α deacetylation → mitochondrial biogenesis↑
   │                         └→ FoxO deacetylation → antioxidant/autophagy genes↑
   ├─→ insulin↓/glucagon↑ → lipolysis↑ → ketones (BHB)↑ → HDAC inhibition/FOXO3a↑
   ├─→ amino acids↓ → GCN2/eIF2α → ATF4 → FGF21↑ (liver secretion)
   └─→ IGF-1↓ → PI3K/Akt↓ → FOXO nuclear translocation↑
```

### 8.2 Midstream: autophagy and mitochondrial quality control

- **Autophagy:** Combined mTORC1 inhibition and AMPK activation turn on the ULK1 complex and autophagic flux—clearing damaged mitochondria (mitophagy), misfolded proteins, and senescence-associated molecular debris. Autophagic flux rises substantially after 24 hours of fasting. In animals, knockout of key autophagy genes (Atg5/Atg7) removes much of CR’s metabolic benefit.
- **Mitochondrial biogenesis:** SIRT1 deacetylates PGC-1α and AMPK phosphorylates it; together they drive NRF1/NRF2→TFAM→mitochondrial DNA replication and synthesis of oxidative-phosphorylation subunits. CR animals have more mitochondria and less ROS per mitochondrion (“fewer, better” phenotype).
- **Mitohormesis:** Mild mitochondrial stress early in CR may transiently raise ROS and adaptively upregulate antioxidant defenses via Nrf2/FOXO—an example of hormesis, or a beneficial response to a low-dose stressor.

### 8.3 Downstream phenotypes

| Phenotype | Mechanism | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Insulin sensitivity↑ | Fat oxidation↑→intramyocellular lipids↓→less PKCθ-mediated serine phosphorylation/inhibition of IRS-1 | CALERIE clamp; TRE RCTs |
| Inflammation↓ | NF-κB inhibition; BHB inhibits NLRP3; increased clearance of senescent-cell SASP | Multiple human IF studies |
| BP↓/endothelium↑ | eNOS phosphorylation↑, NO bioavailability↑ (see Section XI) | TRE/ADF trials |
| Neuroprotection | BDNF↑, BHB fuel, autophagic clearance of Aβ/α-synuclein aggregates | Strong animal; preliminary human |
| Anti-tumor | IGF-1/mTOR↓, immune surveillance (NK cells)↑, glycolysis restricted | Animal and observational evidence |
| Epigenetic remodeling | BHB-mediated HDAC inhibition, SIRT1 deacetylation, slower DNA-methylation clocks | Mechanistic studies + CALERIE post-hoc analysis |

### 8.4 SIRT1/NAD+ and FGF21: two hub molecules

- **How NAD+ may rise:** Fasting upregulates NAMPT (a SIRT1 transcriptional positive-feedback loop: SIRT1→Nampt promoter→NAD+ synthesis↑); CD38, an NAD+-consuming enzyme, may decline; a higher NAD+/NADH ratio itself enhances SIRT1 activity. Activated SIRT1 deacetylates eNOS (Lys496/506) to increase NO, PGC-1α to promote mitochondria, and FoxO to promote antioxidant defenses.
- **FGF21:** During fasting/protein restriction, hepatic ATF4 increases FGF21 secretion. FGF21 acts on hypothalamus/adipose tissue to drive ketogenesis, improve insulin sensitivity, and induce adaptive thermogenesis. FGF21-transgenic mice have been reported to live ~30% longer, making it a key “nutrient restriction→endocrine anti-aging signal.”

## IX. Ketogenic diet (KD): mechanisms, evidence, and long-term controversies

### 9.1 Definition and metabolic state

A ketogenic diet typically provides <50 g carbohydrate/day (60–75% of energy from fat), maintaining ketosis (blood BHB 0.5–3 mM). Its classic use is drug-resistant epilepsy (since the 1920s); modern uses include weight loss, type 2 diabetes, and metabolic syndrome.

### 9.2 Weight-loss and metabolic evidence

- **Weight loss:** Meta-analyses find modest short-term (6–12 month) advantage over low-fat diets (~1–2 kg extra), but the difference disappears after 12 months. The advantage is mainly appetite suppression (ketones may suppress ghrelin/increase satiety) and protein-related satiety, not a “metabolic miracle.”
- **Type 2 diabetes:** The two-year Virta Health cohort (262 people with T2DM) reported that 53% achieved HbA1c in the non-diabetic range and reduced medication, but it was a single-arm, nonrandomized study. RCT evidence suggests HbA1c improvement comparable to Mediterranean diets.
- **Neuroprotection:** BHB can serve as alternative brain fuel. Evidence is strong for childhood epilepsy, preliminary-positive for Alzheimer’s (some small RCTs report cognitive improvement), and preliminary for Parkinson’s disease.

### 9.3 Longevity perspective and long-term safety debate

- **Potential benefits:** KD is essentially carbohydrate restriction→insulin↓→indirect mTORC1 inhibition + AMPK activation + BHB signaling, sharing some fasting pathways. Some call it “fasting simulation without hunger.”
- **Concerns:** (1) Animal studies: short-term metabolic improvement, but after >1 year some mouse strains develop fatty liver, worse insulin resistance, or inflammation—“ketogenic paradox,” potentially involving lipotoxicity from excess fat. (2) Human observational studies associate long-term low-carbohydrate/high-fat diets with higher all-cause mortality (J-/U-shaped associations in several large cohort meta-analyses; causality uncertain). (3) LDL cholesterol may rise substantially in “hyper-responders,” with uncertain atherosclerotic risk. (4) Kidney stones, reduced bone density, and nutrient deficiencies are concerns.
- **Conclusion:** KD is a **therapeutic tool** (epilepsy, refractory obesity, rapid glycemic lowering), not a universal longevity diet. Long-term use requires lipid, renal-function, and bone-density monitoring. Periodic/pulsed ketogenic diets (e.g., 3–5 days per month) are under study at the mechanistic stage.

## X. Gut microbiota and diet: the fiber→SCFA axis

### 10.1 Evidence for the microbiome–aging axis

- Centenarian microbiomes: greater diversity, enrichment of butyrate producers (e.g., *Faecalibacterium prausnitzii*, *Roseburia*), fewer pathogens, and positive associations between some strains (e.g., *Akkermansia muciniphila*) and healthspan. Evidence is primarily from Italian/Japanese centenarian cohorts and remains mechanistic/observational.
- Aging is accompanied by lower microbiome diversity and more pro-inflammatory Proteobacteria. Dysbiosis is associated with sarcopenia, frailty, and inflammaging.
- Animal fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT): transfer of young-mouse microbiota to old mice improves gut barrier, immunity, and cognition; human evidence is limited.

### 10.2 Fiber→SCFA mechanism

```text
Dietary fiber (resistant starch/fructooligosaccharides/inulin/β-glucan)
   │ fermented by colonic microbiota
   ▼
SCFAs: butyrate (~60%) · propionate (~20%) · acetate (~20%)
   ├─→ Butyrate: principal fuel for colonocytes; HDAC inhibitor (epigenetic anti-inflammation); activates GPR109A
   ├─→ Propionate: gluconeogenic substrate in liver; GPR41/43 → GLP-1/PYY → appetite↓, insulin sensitivity↑
   ├─→ Acetate: substrate for peripheral lipid synthesis; crosses BBB → appetite regulation
   └─→ Butyrate → gut barrier (tight-junction proteins↑) → circulating endotoxin (LPS)↓ → systemic inflammation↓
```

**Intake:** Recommended adult fiber intake is 25–38 g/day (Chinese dietary guidelines: 25–30 g); average actual intake is about 11–14 g—a large gap. Each additional 10 g/day is associated with roughly 10% lower all-cause mortality (PURE 2019, 135,000 participants; likely related to cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and cancer).

### 10.3 Prebiotics, probiotics, and postbiotics

- **Prebiotics** (inulin, FOS, resistant starch, polyphenols): good evidence; obtain through foods such as onion, garlic, banana, oats, legumes, and chicory.
- **Probiotics** (Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus): specific strains have RCT evidence for IBS, antibiotic-associated diarrhea, and immune modulation. Anti-aging evidence is weak and highly strain-specific; “probiotics extend life” is often marketing.
- **Postbiotics/butyrate supplements:** Sodium butyrate and tributyrin improve metabolism and gut barrier in animals; human data are limited.
- **Personalization:** microbiome composition varies greatly with diet, birth mode, and antibiotic history. One-size-fits-all probiotic plans lack scientific basis; the most robust intervention remains “feeding the microbiome” with diverse, high-fiber plant foods.

## XI. Dietary restriction and NO: fasting→eNOS→vascular health

### 11.1 Molecular routes by which fasting may increase NO

```text
Fasting/CR/IF
   ├─→ AMPK↑ → eNOS-Ser1177 phosphorylation → NO↑
   ├─→ SIRT1↑ (NAD+-dependent) → eNOS-Lys496/506 deacetylation → NO↑
   ├─→ insulin↓ → improved endothelial insulin resistance → eNOS signaling restored
   ├─→ oxidative stress↓ (ROS↓) → less NO inactivation → NO bioavailability↑
   ├─→ BHB → ROS inhibition, eNOS upregulation, vasodilation (direct effects)
   └─→ microbiome shift → nitrate-reducing bacteria↑ → endogenous nitrate→NO bypass↑ (mechanistic hypothesis)
```

**BHB–NO synergy:** β-hydroxybutyrate may increase NO bioavailability by (1) inhibiting mitochondrial ROS and reducing O₂−-mediated NO inactivation; (2) increasing eNOS expression/phosphorylation; and (3) suppressing NF-κB and endothelial inflammation through GPR109A. Fasting may therefore be a non-pharmacological NO-enhancing strategy.

### 11.2 Human evidence

- Eight-week TRE trials: systolic BP falls ~5–10 mmHg, alongside improved endothelial function (higher FMD).
- ADF (~3 months): improved FMD and reduced pulse-wave velocity (arterial stiffness).
- CALERIE: blood pressure, lipids, and inflammation improved in the CR group, consistent with endothelial protection.
- Mechanistic fasting studies can directly measure increased eNOS phosphorylation and NO metabolites (nitrate/nitrite) in animals; human evidence relies mainly on functional measures (FMD, BP).

### 11.3 The “double-edged” boundary of NO and restriction

Fasting is proposed to increase **low-dose, intermittent, eNOS-derived NO** (physiological signaling). The concern is sustained, high-dose **iNOS-derived NO**, an oxidative-stress source that generates ONOO−. Fasting’s antioxidant/anti-inflammatory properties may suppress iNOS: the net “eNOS up, iNOS down” effect is a proposed vascular-protection mechanism (see the NO paradox in 01-foundations/03-no-basics.md).

## XII. Links to the NAD+ network

```text
Fasting → NAMPT↑ → nicotinamide→NMN→NAD+ synthesis↑ (rate-limiting salvage pathway)
       + CD38↓ (less NAD+ consumption) + NAD+/NADH ratio↑
   ▼
NAD+↑ → SIRT1 activation
   ├─→ eNOS deacetylation → NO↑ (connection to Section XI)
   ├─→ PGC-1α → mitochondrial biogenesis
   ├─→ FoxO → antioxidant defense/autophagy
   └─→ NF-κB-subunit deacetylation → inflammation↓
```

- **NAMPT response to fasting:** After 24–48 h fasting in mice, NAMPT expression and NAD+ rise in multiple tissues. In human fasting studies, profiles of NAD+ metabolites (nicotinamide-related metabolites) change with fasting duration.
- **SIRT1 causality in CR:** SIRT1-overexpressing mice show CR-like metabolic phenotypes but not longer lifespan in an independent 2008 study. Brain-specific SIRT1 knockout removes some CR metabolic benefits—SIRT1 may be necessary but not sufficient for CR effects.
- **NAD+ precursors vs fasting:** NR/NMN raise NAD+ but do not fully mimic CR (they do not necessarily activate AMPK or produce ketones). Human evidence for additive benefit from “fasting + NR/NMN” is lacking. Long-term NAD+ supplementation safety, including possible tumor-microenvironment growth concerns, remains under evaluation (see relevant 04-drugs chapters).
- **SIRT3:** Increased NAD+ also activates mitochondrial SIRT3, which deacetylates mitochondrial proteins (e.g., SOD2 and complex-I subunits) and reduces mitochondrial ROS—another link between NAD+ and mitohormesis.

## XIII. Intervention recommendations and actionable plans

### 13.1 Individualization principles

1. **Baseline assessment:** weight/body fat, HOMA-IR, lipids, blood pressure, inflammatory markers (CRP), thyroid function, vitamin D/ferritin, renal function, and bone density when at risk.
2. **Priority:** first improve dietary structure, then meal timing, and only afterward consider supplements.
3. **Contraindications:** IF/CR is unsuitable during pregnancy/breastfeeding, in type 1 diabetes (insulin risk), history of eating disorders, underweight (BMI<18.5), advanced kidney disease, or cancer cachexia. People taking medication for type 2 diabetes need medical supervision and possible dose adjustment.

### 13.2 Stepwise plan

| Level | Intervention | Target group | Expected effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Foundation | Mediterranean/anti-inflammatory pattern + 25–30 g fiber + 150 min exercise/week | Everyone | Cardiovascular risk↓, inflammation↓ |
| Advanced | Mild CR (10–15%) or habit of stopping at 80% satiety | Overweight/metabolic syndrome | Weight↓, insulin sensitivity↑ |
| Advanced | TRE 16:8 (or start 14:10), avoid late-night window | Those able to adhere | Metabolic switching, circadian alignment |
| Higher-intensity | 5:2 or periodic 24-h fast once weekly; protein 1.2–1.6 g/kg divided across meals | Metabolically healthy, motivated adults | Deeper autophagy, FGF21 |
| Caution | Methionine restriction/periodic ketogenic diet | Research-oriented populations | Mechanistic exploration; immature evidence |

### 13.3 Circadian-alignment points

- Keep the eating window in daytime when possible (e.g., 10:00–18:00), aligned with the circadian period of metabolic efficiency.
- Do not skip breakfast automatically; consider an earlier dinner. Early TRE may outperform late TRE metabolically in some studies, potentially due to melatonin–insulin-sensitivity rhythms.
- Unsweetened coffee/tea is generally acceptable during fasting and usually does not disrupt the major metabolic-switch signals.
- Distribute protein across 2–3 meals within the window (25–40 g each) and combine with resistance exercise to prevent sarcopenia.

## XIV. Open questions and controversies

1. **Can CR extend human lifespan?** CALERIE showed two-year mechanistic improvements, but a lifespan-endpoint trial is impractical and would take decades. Human claims will rely on surrogate endpoints (epigenetic clocks, metabolic health) and indirect evidence such as Mendelian randomization. Clocks suggest a 2–3% slowing, but their causal relationship with lifespan remains debated.
2. **Is CR “metabolic adaptation” good or bad?** RMR fell 5–10% beyond what weight loss predicted. If this reflects longevity-related energy conservation, it may be beneficial; if it leads to weight regain and cold intolerance, it may be harmful. Long-term consequences are unclear.
3. **Sex differences and hormonal costs of IF:** Women may be more sensitive to fasting due to reproductive-axis suppression. The debate over whether IF is ineffective or harmful for women requires age- and menopause-stratified RCTs.
4. **Quantifying the protein paradox:** There are no 20–30-year human prospective data to resolve animal low-protein longevity vs protein needs to preserve muscle in older adults. Independent effects of plant vs animal protein are not fully separated.
5. **TRE window duration:** A 2022 observational analysis suggested windows shorter than eight hours might associate with cardiovascular mortality. Confounding (shift work, smoking, comorbidity) cannot be excluded, but the finding cautions against extreme TRE.
6. **Fasting and tumor immunity:** In animals, fasting/ketones may enhance some immunotherapies but could suppress other immune responses. Fasting recommendations for cancer patients must be individualized and medically supervised.
7. **Microbiome causality:** Are microbial changes mediators of dietary benefit or accompanying changes? Negative human FMT trials suggest microbiome-targeted interventions may be overestimated.
8. **Ketogenic paradox:** Short-term metabolic benefits vs long-term animal/cohort warning signals require human RCTs longer than two years.
