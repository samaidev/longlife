# Daily Anti-Aging Checklist

> Core idea: The "dose" of anti-aging is determined by **execution**, not knowledge. No matter how precise the targets (mTOR, AMPK, eNOS), if they cannot be translated into day-after-day action, the molecular-level advantages amount to zero. This article translates scientific evidence into a daily checklist that ordinary people can tick off item by item: prioritizing interventions by evidence strength (sleep > exercise > diet > stress management > social connection > supplements), providing a three-phase action framework, quantified targets, execution psychology strategies, and a review mechanism. Each item is annotated with evidence level and mechanistic linkage, connecting to the NO/NAD⁺ pathway mainline (see 01-foundations/03-no-basics.md and 02-vascular/01-no-cardiovascular.md).

## Core Concept

The Daily Anti-Aging Checklist is a protocol that translates validated anti-aging interventions into repeatable, quantifiable, and monitorable daily behaviors. It is not an additive "do a little more" list, but a **subtractive list ordered by evidence strength**: first secure the three cornerstones of sleep, exercise, and diet, then address stress, social connection, cognition, and environment, and only then consider supplements and frontier technologies. The underlying logic is that the multiple hallmarks of aging are systemically coupled—exercise simultaneously activates AMPK, maintains mitophagy, and promotes eNOS phosphorylation; sleep simultaneously drives the glymphatic system to clear β-amyloid and restores NAD⁺ rhythmicity; social connection regulates inflammatory gene expression through the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis. A single intervention often hits multiple targets, so **execution priority = breadth of intervention coverage × evidence strength ÷ execution cost**. For the vast majority of people, sleep is the highest priority—broadest coverage, lowest cost, strongest evidence.

## 1. Checklist Design Principles

### 1.1 Evidence First

Every checklist item must answer three questions: Which aging hallmark does it act on (López-Otín 2023 twelve hallmarks)? What is the evidence level (Grade A RCT / Grade B cohort / Grade C animal and mechanism)? What is the expected effect size? Interventions with insufficient evidence (such as most "antioxidant" supplements) do not enter the core checklist, only the "cautious follow-up" tier. This principle prevents "checklist inflation"—every addition dilutes execution attention.

### 1.2 Measurable

Unmeasurable goals cannot be sustained or reviewed. Rewrite "exercise more" as "150 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic exercise per week (brisk walking where you can talk but not sing) + 2 strength training sessions (8 exercises × 3 sets each)"; rewrite "sleep well" as "fall asleep by 23:00, 7.5 hours, morning alertness self-rating ≥7/10." Quantified anchors make weekly review possible.

### 1.3 Sustainable

Adherence determines long-term effects. Behavioral science shows: **habit stability matters more than single-session intensity**—five 30-minute brisk walks per week (150 minutes) yield better long-term cardiovascular benefits than one 150-minute concentrated session, because the latter has a higher dropout rate. Design principle: start with the "minimum viable dose" (e.g., 10 minutes per day), then gradually increase after meeting the target; use habit stacking to attach new behaviors to existing ones (see Section 9).

### 1.4 Individualized

The same dose has different effects on people with different baselines. Individuals with baseline blood pressure of 130 mmHg may reduce it by 4–10 mmHg with dietary nitrate supplementation, while those with normal blood pressure show much smaller reductions; protein requirements increase with age (anabolic resistance); those with low HRV baseline benefit more from breathing training. The checklist provides a "baseline self-assessment → tiered targets" mechanism, rather than a one-size-fits-all approach.

## 2. Intervention Priority Ranking: Why Sleep Comes First

| Priority | Intervention | Number of Aging Hallmarks Covered | Evidence Level | Execution Cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Sleep | 6+ (epigenetics, proteostasis, mitochondria, neurodegeneration, inflammation, autophagy) | A (population + mechanism) | Lowest (free) |
| 2 | Exercise | 8+ (telomeres, epigenetics, proteostasis, mitochondria, cellular senescence, stem cells, inflammation, microbiome) | A (RCT meta-analysis) | Low |
| 3 | Diet | 5+ (nutrient sensing, proteostasis, mitochondria, inflammation, microbiome) | A–B | Moderate |
| 4 | Stress management | 3–4 (inflammation, epigenetics, telomeres) | B (cohort + small RCT) | Low |
| 5 | Social connection | 3 (inflammation, neurodegeneration, immunity) | B (large-scale cohort) | Low |
| 6 | Supplements | 1–2 | C–B | High (costly) |

**Ranking rationale**: 24 hours of sleep deprivation can reduce insulin sensitivity by approximately 20–30% in healthy young adults (Benedict 2012); chronic sleep of <6 hours increases all-cause mortality risk by approximately 12% (meta-analysis, Cappuccio 2010). Among supplements, with the exception of vitamin D (supplement if deficient) and Omega-3 (food first), most RCTs have failed to demonstrate benefits on longevity endpoints in healthy individuals. **Cost-benefit ratio is the ultimate arbiter of ranking**: free, strongly-evidenced behavioral interventions always rank ahead of costly, weakly-evidenced molecular interventions.

## 3. Daily Action Framework: Three Phases

```
Morning (circadian entrainment + NO activation)
  Wake at a fixed time (±30 minutes) → open curtains for 10 minutes of daylight (cortisol rhythm anchoring)
  → Drink 300–500 ml water → 5–10 minutes of light activity before breakfast (walk/stretch)
  → (Optional) 250 ml beetroot juice or dark leafy greens (dietary nitrate)

Daytime (metabolism + muscle + cognition)
  Stand up and move for 2 minutes every 45–60 minutes (sedentary breaks, NEAT↑)
  Lunch: vegetables ≥2 fists + protein 1 palm (see Section 6)
  Afternoon: 20–30 minutes brisk walk OR 40 minutes strength training (per weekly plan)
  → Avoid caffeine after 3 PM (sleep protection)

Evening (parasympathetic + detoxification)
  Dinner: light meal, finish eating 3 hours before bedtime (time-restricted eating window)
  → 1 hour before bed: warm light + no blue-light screens + 10 minutes breathing/meditation
  → Bedroom: dark (blackout curtains), cool (18–20°C), quiet
  → Fixed bedtime (around 23:00) → 7–9 hours of sleep
```

The physiological logic of the three-phase design: morning behaviors anchor the **master circadian clock** (SCN synchronized by light exposure), daytime behaviors maximize the **metabolic and muscle stimulation window** (post-meal insulin-sensitive period, daytime cortisol peak), and evening behaviors drive **parasympathetic dominance and glymphatic system detoxification** (during sleep, brain interstitial space expands by approximately 60%, and β-amyloid clearance rate increases approximately 2-fold, Xie 2013).

## 4. Sleep: The First-Priority Intervention

### 4.1 Mechanistic Linkage

```
Fixed schedule → circadian rhythm stability (Per/Cry clock gene expression phase consistency)
  → Pineal melatonin nocturnal peak normalization → SCN→hypothalamus→sympathetic output rhythm normalization
  → Deep sleep (SWS) proportion↑
      ├─→ Glymphatic system detoxification: β-amyloid, tau clearance↑
      ├─→ Growth hormone pulse↑ (during deep sleep) → tissue repair
      ├─→ NAD⁺/SIRT1 rhythm restoration: NAMPT circadian oscillation → SIRT1 target gene normal expression
      ├─→ Glucose metabolism: sleep deprivation → insulin sensitivity↓ (24h sleep loss can reduce by 20–30%)
      └─→ Immunity: NK cell activity↑ (4h sleep group NK activity decreased ~50% vs 8h group)
```

### 4.2 Quantified Targets and Evidence

| Indicator | Target | Evidence Level |
|---|---|---|
| Duration | 7–9 hours (adults); <6h all-cause mortality risk ↑12% (meta-analysis of 1.3 million) | A |
| Regularity | Sleep/wake time fluctuation <1 hour; social jetlag >2h associated with metabolic syndrome | B |
| Sleep efficiency | ≥85% (proportion of actual sleep in time in bed) | B |
| Deep sleep/REM | Deep sleep 13–23%, REM 20–25% (can be roughly estimated with wearable devices) | C (device accuracy to be verified) |

### 4.3 Sleep Hygiene Action Items

1. **Fixed schedule**: Weekend vs. weekday wake time difference ≤1 hour—catch-up sleep only partially compensates and will drift the circadian phase.
2. **Morning light**: Within 30 minutes of waking, get 10–15 minutes of outdoor light (effective even on cloudy days), suppresses melatonin, anchors cortisol awakening response.
3. **Blue light management**: Enable device night mode 1–2 hours before bed; screen blue light (460 nm peak) suppresses melatonin secretion with an effect size of approximately 2 hours phase delay (Chang 2015). Preferred physical solution: no phone for one hour before bed.
4. **Temperature**: Bedroom 18–20°C (core body temperature drop of 0.5–1°C is the sleep onset signal).
5. **Caffeine**: Avoid after 3 PM; caffeine half-life is approximately 5–6 hours, with large individual variation (CYP1A2 genotype).
6. **Alcohol**: Pre-sleep alcohol disrupts deep sleep and REM architecture, even if subjectively "sleep-inducing"—ALDH2 slow metabolizers (common in East Asia) are at higher risk.

## 5. Exercise: The Strongest NO Pathway Activator + All-Hallmark Intervention

### 5.1 Mechanistic Linkage

```
Muscle contraction → shear stress (blood flow friction on vascular endothelium)
  ├─→ PECAM-1/VEGFR2/VE-cadherin mechanosensory complex → PI3K→Akt→eNOS-Ser1177 phosphorylation → NO↑
  ├─→ Contracting muscle releases IL-6 ("myokine") → anti-inflammatory (inhibits TNF-α)
  ├─→ AMPK↑ (energy stress) → mitochondrial biogenesis (PGC-1α) → mitophagy
  └─→ Skeletal muscle glucose uptake↑ (GLUT4 translocation, insulin-independent)
Strength training → mechanical tension → mTOR (transient activation) → muscle protein synthesis↑ → sarcopenia delay
```

### 5.2 Prescription (WHO 2020 Guidelines + Longevity Research Supplements)

| Module | Dose | Evidence Level |
|---|---|---|
| Moderate-intensity aerobic | ≥150 minutes/week (e.g., brisk walking 5 days × 30 minutes) | A: all-cause mortality risk ↓~31% (meta-analysis) |
| High-intensity aerobic/HIIE | 75 minutes/week or 2 sessions × 15–20 minutes | A: higher cardiorespiratory fitness improvement efficiency; HIIE improves FMD up to 8.25% |
| Strength training | ≥2 sessions/week, 8–10 exercises × 2–3 sets × 8–12 reps | A: muscle mass and bone density; muscle loss 1–2%/year after age 40, accelerating after 60 |
| Balance/flexibility | 2–3 sessions/week (tai chi/yoga/single-leg stand) | B: fall risk (leading cause of injury death in those 65+) |
| NEAT (non-exercise activity) | 8,000–10,000 steps/day; stand up for 2 minutes every 45 minutes | B: sedentary time and all-cause mortality J-shaped relationship (>10h/day significantly elevated) |

**Key details**: ① 8,000–10,000 steps is the inflection point zone for all-cause mortality risk reduction (plateau after ~8,000 steps, Paluch 2022, ~110,000-person cohort); ② Sedentary breaks (stand up for 2 minutes every 30–60 minutes) can independently reduce blood glucose peaks and arterial stiffness risk, regardless of total exercise volume; ③ Intensity self-assessment: moderate intensity = can talk but not sing (RPE 12–14), high intensity = cannot speak complete sentences.

## 6. Diet: Anti-Inflammatory + Protein + Time-Restricted Eating

### 6.1 Three Pillars

**① Food Pattern: Mediterranean/Anti-Inflammatory Diet (Strongest Evidence)**

PREDIMED trial (7,447 participants, median follow-up 4.8 years): Mediterranean diet + extra virgin olive oil group had major cardiovascular event risk ↓30% vs. control (HR 0.70). Mechanism: olive oil monounsaturated fatty acids + polyphenols (hydroxytyrosol) → inhibit NF-κB → inflammation↓; fish Omega-3 → pro-resolving mediator (resolvin) synthesis↑; dietary nitrate → NO↑. Execution points: vegetables ≥2 fists per meal (rainbow colors), 2 servings fruit, whole grains replacing refined carbohydrates, ≥2 servings deep-sea fish per week (salmon/sardines), olive oil replacing refined oils, 30g nuts/day.

**② Protein: 1.2–1.6 g/kg/day**

60 kg adult = 72–96 g/day (approximately 3–4 palms of protein). Rationale: older adults have anabolic resistance, requiring higher protein thresholds to trigger mTOR-mediated muscle synthesis; protein intake is positively correlated with muscle mass in older adults (Grade B cohort). Distribution: 25–30 g per meal (~1 palm), breakfast is often neglected and is the biggest gap. No evidence of harm at 1.6 g/kg for those with normal kidney function; CKD patients should follow medical advice.

**③ Time-Restricted Eating (TRE): 16:8 as the Mainstream Option**

Compress the eating window to 8–10 hours (e.g., 10:00–18:00). Mechanism: extended fasting period → insulin↓ → AMPK↑ → NAD⁺/SIRT1↑ → autophagy↑. Evidence: human RCTs show weight↓, insulin sensitivity↑, blood pressure↓; but **food quality within the eating window remains the determining factor**—16:8 + junk food will not reverse metabolic damage. Note: women and low-body-weight individuals need monitoring (insufficient evidence on TRE interactions with female hormonal axes and menstrual cycles); those with eating disorder history should avoid.

### 6.2 Diet Execution Checklist

- Each meal: half plate vegetables + 1/4 protein + 1/4 whole grains/legumes
- Daily: 1–2 servings dark leafy greens (spinach/arugula/kale—also dietary nitrate sources)
- Weekly: deep-sea fish 2 times; legumes 3 times; limit red meat, processed meat (WHO Group 1 carcinogen)
- Eliminate/limit: sugar-sweetened beverages (associated with all-cause mortality ↑11% per daily serving, meta-analysis), ultra-processed foods
- Eating window 8–10 hours; no eating 3 hours before bedtime

## 7. Stress Management: Vagus Nerve + HRV

### 7.1 Mechanism

Chronic stress → sustained HPA axis activation → cortisol↑ → glucocorticoid receptor-mediated inflammatory gene upregulation, telomerase activity↓ (Epel 2004: chronically stressed women had shorter leukocyte telomeres and lower telomerase activity); sustained sympathetic dominance → HRV↓. Conversely: slow abdominal breathing (~6 breaths/min) → pulmonary stretch receptors → vagal tone↑ → HRV↑ → parasympathetic dominance → inflammation↓, NO bioavailability↑ (acetylcholine via vagal stimulation of endothelial M3 receptors can promote eNOS phosphorylation—"cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway").

### 7.2 Action Items

| Method | Dose | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| 4-7-8 or 6 breaths/min breathing | 2 times/day × 5–10 minutes | B: acute HRV↑; long-term effects supported by small RCTs |
| Mindfulness meditation (MBSR) | 10–20 minutes/day | B: cortisol↓, inflammatory markers↓, telomerase activity↑ (8 weeks, small-sample RCT) |
| HRV biofeedback | 1 time/day 10–20 minutes, breathing with device rhythm | B: effective for asthma/anxiety populations; evidence weak for whether "training HRV itself" translates to longevity endpoints in healthy people |
| Evening walk | 20 minutes, low intensity | B: natural "stress offloading" + daylight + NEAT triple benefit |

**Honest boundary**: HRV is a **monitoring indicator, not a training target**—studies show limited benefit from "training HRV to improve HRV"; a more reliable path is to improve autonomic balance through upstream behaviors like sleep, exercise, and breathing practice, with HRV as a downstream readout (see Section 10 monitoring).

## 8. Social, Cognitive, and Environmental Factors

### 8.1 Social Connection: Findings from the Longest Longevity Study

The Harvard Study of Adult Development (begun in 1938, tracking 724 individuals and their descendants for nearly 85 years) core conclusion: **relationship satisfaction at age 50 is a stronger predictor of physical health at age 80 than cholesterol levels**. Mechanistic research (Grade C—mechanistic speculation): social isolation → chronic stress → inflammation↑, accelerated telomere shortening; loneliness is associated with all-cause mortality risk ↑26% (Holt-Lunstad 2015, meta-analysis of 3.4 million). Action items: ≥1 real (non-virtual) social activity per week; regular contact with family/friends; pet ownership (dog owners have higher step counts and lower loneliness, Grade B). Execution cost is nearly zero, yet evidence reaches Grade B—exceptional cost-effectiveness.

### 8.2 Cognitive Stimulation

Cognitive reserve delays the clinical onset of dementia (Grade B). Most effective forms: **learning new skills** (new language/instrument/dance—requires active processing and coordination, superior to passive crossword puzzles) and **dual-task training** (walking while doing arithmetic/memorizing vocabulary—challenges prefrontal-hippocampal networks). Execution: 3 times/week × 20–30 minutes of "deliberate practice" type cognitive activity; dance simultaneously satisfies social + exercise + cognitive triple stimulation, making it the most cost-effective single activity. Note: brain training APP commercial claims are often exaggerated—evidence for transfer to daily functioning is weak (to be verified; most independent reviews reach conservative conclusions).

### 8.3 Environmental Design

- **Blue light**: Warm light environment (below 2700K) for one hour before bed; no white LED direct light in bedroom.
- **Temperature**: Bedroom 18–20°C (promotes deep sleep); sauna (Finnish-style 80–100°C, 2–4 times/week × 15 minutes) associated with cardiovascular event risk ↓ (Grade B, heat shock protein HSP70 mechanism, dose-response).
- **Sedentary environment**: Standing desks/standing meetings; screen timed reminders to stand up.
- **Social environment**: Neighborhood walkability is positively correlated with physical activity in older adults—the environment passively shapes behavior, more reliable than willpower.

## 9. Execution Strategies: Behavioral Science Toolkit

No matter how scientifically designed the checklist is, if it cannot be executed, it equals zero. Four behavioral science levers:

**① Habit Stacking**: Anchor new behaviors to existing stable behaviors. "After brushing teeth → take fish oil"; "after morning light → brisk walk 10 minutes." Formula: **After [existing habit], I will [new habit]**. New habits initially bound to cues are more reliable than those bound to time.

**② Environment Design**: Changing the environment is 10 times easier than changing willpower. Put sneakers by the door, move phone charger out of bedroom, wash vegetables and place on most visible fridge shelf, stash snacks in back of cabinet. Fogg Behavior Model: Behavior = Motivation × Ability × Prompt—lowering the "ability" threshold (making the correct behavior easier) is the most controllable variable for ordinary people.

**③ 2-Minute Rule**: Shrink any new habit to completable within 2 minutes—"brisk walk 30 minutes daily" becomes "put on sneakers and walk out the door"; "meditate 15 minutes daily" becomes "sit on the meditation cushion." Startup cost is the biggest execution barrier; start first, then extend duration.

**④ Accountability**: Commit to others (partner/friend/community check-in) + record (see Section 10). Research shows: habits with clear commitment and peer supervision have significantly higher adherence rates than solo execution. Weekly review (Section 11) is the core ritual of self-accountability.

## 10. Monitoring: Calibrating Execution with Data

### 10.1 Home Self-Measurement (Free or Low-Cost)

| Indicator | Frequency | Target Range | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weight | 1 time/week (morning, fasting) | Fluctuation <2 kg; trend more important than single point | Scale + trend line |
| Waist circumference | 1 time/month | Men <90 cm, women <85 cm (Asian standards) | Central obesity is an independent metabolic risk indicator |
| Blood pressure | 2–3 times/week | <130/85 mmHg (home measurement) | Measure after morning urination, seated 5 minutes |
| Resting heart rate | Daily morning | 60–80 bpm (decreases with cardiorespiratory fitness) | Long-term upward trend warrants attention |
| HRV | Daily morning | Individualized baseline ±20% (look at trends, not single days) | Downward trend = insufficient recovery/overload signal |
| Sleep | Continuous (wearable) | Duration 7–9h, efficiency ≥85% | Device absolute accuracy limited, trends usable |
| Steps | Continuous | 8,000–10,000 | Sedentary breaks more granular than total steps |

### 10.2 Annual Medical Examination

- Fasting blood glucose + HbA1c (<5.7% normal, 5.7–6.4% prediabetes)
- Lipid panel (LDL-C target stratified by cardiovascular risk, not one-size-fits-all)
- Liver and kidney function, vitamin D (25-OH-D, <30 nmol/L deficient, high deficiency rate in East Asian populations)
- Thyroid function (TSH), homocysteine (>15 µmol/L associated with cardiovascular risk↑)
- Age 50+: bone density (DXA), colorectal cancer screening (FIT/colonoscopy), fundus examination
- Ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (office blood pressure may be "white coat" elevated)

**Monitoring philosophy**: Indicators are for **calibrating execution** (which module is falling behind), not for creating anxiety. Daily fluctuations in single indicators are mostly noise; 4–12 week trends are meaningful.

## 11. Weekly / Monthly / Quarterly Review Mechanism

| Period | Review Content | Decision Rules |
|---|---|---|
| Weekly | Sleep duration/regularity, exercise completion rate (150 min + 2 strength sessions), eating window, social frequency | Modules with <70% completion: reduce to minimum viable dose, don't abandon |
| Monthly | Weight/waist trend, blood pressure average, monthly HRV vs. previous month, sleep efficiency | A module not improving for 4 consecutive weeks → change execution method (switch time slot/form), don't switch the goal |
| Quarterly | Blood marker comparison, exercise capacity (e.g., 6-minute walk/estimated VO₂max), subjective energy/mood scores | Core indicators worsening → return to Section 2 priorities, fix sleep first; all stable → upgrade one tier (e.g., tighten time-restricted eating or add supplements) |

**Psychological points of review**: Recording itself is an intervention (self-monitoring effect)—written goals have significantly higher completion rates than mental goals; during review, look only at trends and completion rates, don't self-blame for single-day failures ("never miss twice" principle: occasional lapses are allowed, but two consecutive days of lapse are not).

## 12. Common Misconceptions

1. **Over-supplementation**: Multiple RCTs of high-dose "antioxidant" supplementation (vitamins C/E, β-carotene) in healthy individuals show no benefit, and some (e.g., β-carotene in smokers) are harmful—**oxidative stress is a signal, not a pure enemy**; excessive scavenging blunts Nrf2/AMPK adaptive signaling. Supplements always rank after behavioral interventions in the checklist.
2. **Extreme diets**: Ketogenic/pure vegan/very-low-calorie diets without medical indication carry high risks of nutritional deficiency and dropout; the strongest longevity evidence remains "healthy pattern + moderation" (Mediterranean-style), not any extreme label.
3. **Neglecting fundamentals**: Staying up late while swallowing NMN is the least cost-effective combination—NAD⁺ precursors cannot compensate for the circadian disruption and metabolic damage caused by sleep deprivation; fix sleep first, then discuss NAD⁺ enhancement.
4. **Digital superstition**: Wearable sleep scores/HRV scores ≠ health outcomes; use trends to calibrate behavior, don't chase daily perfect scores.
5. **Treating supplements as "insurance"**: NMN/NAD⁺ precursors show improvement in some metabolic markers in human RCTs (muscle NAD⁺, insulin sensitivity), but no evidence yet indicates they extend lifespan endpoints in healthy people—positioned as "optional experimental tools," not "buy and live longer."
6. **Ignoring strength training**: Doing only aerobic exercise is a common imbalance—muscle and bone loss accelerates irreversibly after age 40; strength training is the only direct countermeasure and is independently associated with all-cause mortality risk ↓ (meta-analysis, Grade B–A).

## 13. Connection to the NO / NAD⁺ Longevity Network

Nearly every item on the daily action checklist directly or indirectly acts on the two mainlines of NO and NAD⁺:

**Daily actions that promote NO production**:
- **Exercise**: Shear stress → eNOS-Ser1177 phosphorylation → NO↑ (strongest activator, see 02-vascular/01-no-cardiovascular.md for full chain)
- **Dietary nitrate**: Dark leafy greens/beetroot → oral bacteria reduce to nitrite → gastric acid/hypoxic environment reduces to NO (non-enzymatic bypass, eNOS-independent; **do not use chlorhexidine mouthwash**, which kills oral nitrate-reducing bacteria and can significantly reduce circulating nitrite within 6 hours)
- **Deep breathing**: Slow breathing → vagus nerve → endothelial M3 receptors → eNOS activation ("cholinergic-endothelial" coupling)
- **Sleep**: Rhythm restoration → endothelial function repair (24h sleep deprivation can reduce FMD by ~2–3%)

**Daily actions that promote NAD⁺**:
- Time-restricted eating/caloric restriction → AMPK↑ → NAMPT↑ → NAD⁺ synthesis↑ → SIRT1 activation
- Exercise → energy stress → NAD⁺/NADH ratio change + SIRT1 activation
- Sleep → NAMPT circadian rhythm normalization (NAD⁺ concentration oscillates with circadian rhythm)

**Positive feedback loop**: NO ↔ SIRT1 mutually activate—SIRT1 deacetylates and activates eNOS, and NO enhances SIRT1 activity; the "exercise-diet-sleep" combination formed by daily execution is essentially continuously feeding this positive feedback loop (see 04-drugs/05-molecular-mech.md and 07-interventions/03-nutrition.md).

## 14. Open Questions and Controversies

1. **Long-term safety of time-restricted eating**: Data on muscle mass maintenance, female endocrine effects, and eating disorder induction risk for 16:8 beyond 12 months remain insufficient (especially in East Asian populations).
2. **Exercise dose upper limit**: The effects of very high-volume exercise (>10 hours/week) on the heart (atrial fibrillation risk) and joints are controversial; J-shaped/U-shaped relationships to be determined.
3. **Whether HRV training is worthwhile**: Undisputed as a monitoring indicator, but evidence is weak as a training target itself—the benefit distribution between "training plasticity" and "monitoring upstream behaviors" is unclear.
4. **Protein intake and mTOR**: The muscle benefits of 1.6 g/kg protein in older adults vs. concerns about potential pro-aging effects of mTOR activation lack human endpoint data; current consensus leans toward "muscle protection first."
5. **Individual genotype differences**: FTO (obesity), APOE (lipid metabolism/AD risk), CYP1A2 (caffeine) genotypes cause significant effect differences for the same checklist in different individuals—"precision checklist" is still in its infancy.
6. **Biological age quantification of lifestyle effects**: Epigenetic clocks (Horvath 2013) show that lifestyle changes (diet, exercise, weight loss) can slow epigenetic age, but effect sizes, reversibility, and consistency with hard endpoints (lifespan) are still being validated—"biological age reversal of X years" marketing claims warrant caution (see relevant chapters in 01-foundations).
