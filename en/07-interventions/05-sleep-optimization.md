# Sleep Optimization and Longevity

> Core idea: Sleep is the one physiological state that simultaneously drives clearance of brain waste, growth-hormone pulses, restoration of NAD+/SIRT1 rhythms, stronger immune surveillance, and maintenance of metabolic homeostasis. It is a “free, comprehensive, and best-supported” anti-aging intervention, with higher priority than any supplement (see intervention priorities in 07-interventions/01-daily-checklist.md). Both the NO and NAD+ pathways—the main threads of this resource—oscillate over the sleep–wake cycle. Sleep therefore forms the “rhythmic foundation” of the longevity network.

## Core Concepts

Sleep is not passive “shutdown,” but an evolutionarily conserved active repair program: multicellular organisms broadly use periodic quiescence to enable glial waste clearance and the integration of synaptic homeostasis and immune memory. In 2013, Nedergaard’s group (Xie et al., *Science*) showed that during sleep, especially deep slow-wave sleep, the glymphatic system expands brain interstitial space by about 60%. Cerebrospinal fluid then flows through perivascular AQP4 pathways, increasing clearance of metabolic waste such as β-amyloid (Aβ) by about twofold compared with wakefulness. Sleep has consequently been called “the brain’s detoxification time.” From an aging perspective, sleep acts on at least seven of López-Otín’s 12 hallmarks of aging (2023): loss of proteostasis (clearance of Aβ/tau), mitochondrial dysfunction (NAD+ rhythms and autophagy), epigenetic alterations (clock-gene networks), neurodegeneration, chronic inflammation (NF-κB/IL-6), altered intercellular communication (HPA axis/sympathetic activity), and disabled macroautophagy. Epidemiologically, sleep duration and all-cause mortality follow a U-shaped curve, with a nadir at 7–8 hours; short sleep (<6 hours) is an independent risk factor associated with about 12% higher all-cause mortality (Cappuccio 2010, meta-analysis of about 1.3 million people). Understanding sleep’s molecular mechanisms is a prerequisite for upgrading it from a “lifestyle habit” to an “anti-aging prescription.”

## I. Sleep Physiology: Molecular Foundations of Architecture and Rhythms

### 1.1 Sleep Architecture: NREM–REM Cycles and the 90-Minute Rhythm

Sleep consists of alternating non-rapid eye movement (NREM) and rapid eye movement (REM) sleep. NREM is divided by depth into N1 (sleep onset), N2 (light sleep, with 12–15 Hz sleep spindles and K-complexes), and N3 (deep/slow-wave sleep, SWS, with high-amplitude 0.5–4 Hz delta waves). During REM, EEG activity approaches that of wakefulness, but skeletal muscle tone disappears (REM atonia) and rapid eye movements occur. Adults experience 4–6 cycles of about 90 minutes over a night: N3 deep sleep predominates during the first half (“high sleep pressure”), while the proportion of REM gradually increases in the second half. The core regulator is the **two-process model** (Borbély 1982): the phase relationship between Process S (sleep pressure, which accumulates approximately linearly with time awake and is associated with adenosine concentration) and Process C (circadian rhythm, driven by the suprachiasmatic nucleus, SCN) determines sleep timing and the proportion of deep sleep. Slow-wave activity (SWA) is an electrophysiological readout of Process S and a quantitative measure of sleep homeostasis.

```
More time awake → adenosine accumulates → A1/A2A receptors
    ├─→ reduced thalamocortical depolarization blockade → SWA↑ (Process S)
    └─→ N3 deep-sleep proportion↑ → SWA dissipates (homeostatic recovery)
Circadian rhythm (Process C): SCN master clock ~24.2 h → nighttime melatonin peak /
    nighttime core-body-temperature nadir
Coordination of both phases → sleep–wake gate
    (flip-flop switch: mutual inhibition between VLPO and orexin neurons)
```

Deep sleep has irreplaceable restorative functions: **the growth-hormone (GH) pulse reaches its daily peak during the first slow-wave period after sleep onset** (Takahashi 1968, *J Clin Invest*), driving tissue repair and protein synthesis; the slow-wave period is also the window of highest glymphatic clearance efficiency.

### 1.2 Circadian Rhythm: SCN, Melatonin, Core Temperature, and Light

The suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) is the mammalian master clock, with a self-sustained period of about 24.2 hours (Czeisler 1999, *Science*), and must be calibrated daily by zeitgebers. Light is the strongest zeitgeber: melanopsin-sensitive intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells (ipRGCs; peak sensitivity to 460-nm blue light) → retinohypothalamic tract → SCN → paraventricular nucleus–superior cervical ganglion → pineal gland, where melatonin synthesis is suppressed. Melatonin is the “darkness signal”: nighttime plasma concentrations rise 5–10-fold compared with daytime; via MT1/MT2 receptors, melatonin feeds back to regulate SCN phase. Its onset (dim-light melatonin onset, DLMO) is the gold standard for circadian phase.

At the molecular level, circadian rhythms are maintained by transcription–translation negative-feedback loops:

```
CLOCK:BMAL1 heterodimer → activates Per1/2/3 and Cry1/2 transcription
    → PER:CRY proteins enter the nucleus and inhibit CLOCK:BMAL1
    (approximately 24-hour oscillation)
    ├─→ drives NAMPT transcription → circadian oscillation of NAD+ synthesis
    │   (Ramsey 2009; Nakahata 2009, Science)
    ├─→ NAD+ → rhythmic SIRT1 activity → deacetylation of BMAL1/PER2
    │   feeds back to regulate the clock (Nakahata 2008, Cell)
    └─→ output genes: melatonin-synthesis enzyme AANAT, cortisol axis, metabolic enzymes
```

**Core body temperature** is another key sleep signal: 1–2 hours before bedtime, peripheral vasodilation lowers core temperature by about 0.5–1°C. The rate of temperature decline, rather than its absolute value, triggers sleep onset; a cool bedroom (18–20°C) promotes this process by widening the skin-to-core temperature gradient. Circadian disruption (shift work, social jet lag) fundamentally represents uncoupling between the CLOCK-gene network and peripheral timing cues such as light, meals, and temperature, flattening NAMPT–NAD+ oscillations.

### 1.3 Three Major Restorative Functions of Deep Sleep

**① Glymphatic clearance:** Iliff 2012 (*Sci Transl Med*) proposed a lymphatic-like pathway in which CSF flows along periarterial spaces, is driven through the interstitium by AQP4 water channels in astrocyte endfeet, and exits along perivenous spaces. Xie 2013 (*Science*) showed that during sleep, particularly slow-wave sleep, extracellular space expands by about 60% and Aβ clearance rises about twofold; the mechanism is associated with lower norepinephrine levels and reduced resistance in the interstitial space. Efficiency of this pathway declines 40–60% with age (Kress 2014, *Ann Neurol*), associated with impaired AQP4 polarization and weaker arterial pulsation. This provides a direct pathway for “poor sleep → Aβ accumulation → AD” (see 03-immunity/06-neuro-immune-axis.md).

**② Synaptic homeostasis and memory consolidation:** The synaptic homeostasis hypothesis (Tononi & Cirelli 2006) proposes that learning strengthens synapses overall during wakefulness and increases metabolic cost, while slow-wave sleep prunes redundant connections through “synaptic downscaling,” restoring room for plasticity. Another route for memory consolidation is **hippocampal replay**: sequences of hippocampal neurons encoded during wakefulness are rapidly replayed during sleep (Wilson & McNaughton 1994, *Science*). Coupling of slow waves and spindles transfers episodic memories to long-term cortical storage (reviewed in Rasch & Born 2013, *Physiol Rev*). Sleep deprivation directly impairs hippocampus-dependent episodic memory and synaptic plasticity (LTP).

**③ Immune and metabolic repair:** The sleep–wake cycle drives rhythmic mobilization of immune cells: NK-cell activity, naive T-cell counts, and IL-12 peak at night; deep sleep is an “immune-surveillance window.” Metabolically, after two nights of sleep deprivation, leptin falls by about 18%, ghrelin rises by about 28%, hunger increases by about 24%, and preference shifts toward high-calorie foods (Spiegel 1999, *Lancet*). A single night of four hours’ sleep can reduce insulin sensitivity by 19–25% (Donga 2010, *JCEM*).

```
Deep sleep (SWS) ─┬─→ glymphatic clearance↑ (Aβ/tau removal) → proteostasis
                  ├─→ GH pulse↑ → tissue repair
                  ├─→ synaptic downscaling + hippocampal replay → memory consolidation
                  ├─→ restoration of NAD+/SIRT1 rhythms (NAMPT) → mitophagy
                  ├─→ NK/naive T-cell mobilization↑ → immune surveillance
                  └─→ leptin↑/ghrelin↓/insulin sensitivity↑ → metabolic homeostasis
```

## II. Clinical and Epidemiological Evidence on Sleep and Aging

### 2.1 Sleep Duration and All-Cause Mortality: The U-Shaped Curve

Cappuccio 2010 (*Sleep*, meta-analysis of 1.3 million people) found that short sleep (<6 h) was associated with an all-cause mortality risk ratio of RR≈1.12, while long sleep (>9 h) was associated with RR≈1.30. Kripke 2002 (*Arch Gen Psychiatry*, cohort of 1.1 million) confirmed that seven hours was associated with the lowest mortality. The increased risk on the long-sleep side is often confounded by comorbidities, depression, sleep apnea, and other factors; it should not be interpreted in reverse as “sleeping more is harmful.” A sleep-restriction experiment (Dinges 1997, *Sleep*: 4–5 hours for 14 consecutive days) demonstrated cumulative declines in cognition and alertness, while subjective sleepiness lagged behind objective performance deterioration—“feeling fine” does not rule out sleep debt.

### 2.2 Sleep and Alzheimer’s Disease

- **Aβ:** Spira 2013 (*JAMA Neurol*) found that self-reported insufficient sleep was associated with increased Aβ deposition in community-dwelling older adults. Ju 2013 (*JAMA Neurol*) found that sleep fragmentation was associated with lower CSF Aβ42 (a surrogate marker of cortical deposition). Lucey 2018 (*Ann Neurol*) found that a single night of sleep deprivation raised CSF Aβ42 by about 30%.
- **Tau:** Holth 2019 (*Science*) showed that the sleep–wake cycle directly regulates tau: sleep deprivation approximately doubled brain interstitial tau in mice, and human CSF tau also rose with time awake; enhancing slow waves lowered tau. This suggests that the extracellular tau pool is controlled both by release driven by neuronal activity during wakefulness and by clearance during sleep.
- **Age-related loss of slow waves:** Mander 2013 (*Nat Neurosci*) found that prefrontal atrophy in older adults → reduced NREM slow-wave activity → impaired hippocampus-dependent memory consolidation, forming a mediating chain of “age → loss of deep sleep → cognitive decline.” The proportion of deep sleep in people aged 60–70 is about 50–80% lower than in young adults (both slow-wave amplitude and density decline).
- **Cohorts:** Lim 2013 (*Sleep*) found that sleep fragmentation was independently associated with AD incidence and rate of cognitive decline in older adults.

### 2.3 Sleep and Cardiovascular Health: OSA and the Endothelium

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a pivotal disorder on the sleep–cardiovascular axis: intermittent hypoxia → repeated arousals → sympathetic activation and oxidative stress. Peppard 2000 (*NEJM*, Wisconsin cohort) found a dose-dependent relationship between the apnea–hypopnea index (AHI) and four-year hypertension risk; risk in those with AHI≥15 was about 2–3 times that in people without OSA. Gami 2007 (*JACC*) found that OSA was independently associated with incident atrial fibrillation. At the endothelial level, Jelic 2008 (*Circulation*) demonstrated reduced vascular endothelial eNOS expression and impaired repair capacity in OSA patients; four weeks of CPAP treatment partially restored these measures. CPAP lowers blood pressure by about 2–2.5 mmHg (meta-analysis, Fava 2014)—less than antihypertensive drugs, but it also corrects hypoxia. Sleep deprivation itself, even without OSA, damages vascular endothelium: 24 hours of sleep deprivation significantly reduces flow-mediated dilation (FMD) and increases systolic blood pressure (Dettoni 2012, *Am J Hypertens*).

### 2.4 Sleep, Metabolism, and Immunity

- **Metabolism:** Short sleep is associated with a type 2 diabetes risk ratio of RR≈1.3–1.5 (meta-analyses). Mechanisms include reduced insulin sensitivity, increased nighttime sympathetic activation and cortisol, and appetite amplification from leptin/ghrelin imbalance (see 1.3③).
- **Immunity:** Cohen 2009 (*Arch Intern Med*) found that people sleeping ≤5 hours were about 4.5 times as likely to catch rhinovirus as those sleeping 7–8 hours. Prather 2012 (*Sleep*) found that after hepatitis B vaccination, people sleeping <6 hours were about 11.5 times as likely as adequate sleepers to lack protective antibody levels at six months. Irwin 2006 (*Arch Intern Med*) found that sleep restriction elevated morning IL-6, TNF-α, and NF-κB pathway activity, providing molecular evidence for “insufficient sleep → chronic low-grade inflammation.” Irwin 1994 (*FASEB J*) found that a single night of partial sleep deprivation reduced NK-cell cytotoxicity by about 30–40%.

### 2.5 Insomnia Treatment: CBT-I First-Line, Medication Risks

Chronic insomnia affects about 10% of adults, rising to over 30% with age. **Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is the best-supported first-line treatment.** Its core components are stimulus control (go to bed only when sleepy), sleep restriction (compress time in bed to improve efficiency), and cognitive restructuring (eliminate catastrophic beliefs such as “I must get enough sleep”). Morin 2009 (*JAMA*) found CBT-I’s short-term efficacy comparable to zolpidem and significantly superior to medication alone over the long term (6–24 months). A meta-analysis by Trauer 2015 (*Ann Intern Med*) found that CBT-I shortened sleep-onset latency by about 20 minutes and improved sleep efficiency by about 8–10%, without medication adverse effects. Medications require caution: benzodiazepines and Z-drugs have limited effects on total sleep (shortening sleep-onset time by about 5–10 minutes), but significantly increase falls, hip fractures, and cognitive impairment in older adults (Glass 2005, *BMJ* meta-analysis), and may lead to tolerance and dependence. A matched cohort by Kripke 2012 (*BMJ Open*) suggested an association between prescription sleeping pills and mortality (causality is disputed; see open questions). Melatonin has limited efficacy for primary insomnia (shortens sleep-onset latency by about 7–12 minutes, Buscemi 2005 meta-analysis), but is effective for delayed sleep–wake phase disorder (DSWPD) and jet-lag adjustment; low doses (0.3–3 mg) have a good safety profile. Newer orexin receptor antagonists (suvorexant, daridorexant) do not alter sleep architecture and have lower dependence potential, representing a direction of progress in pharmacotherapy.

### 2.6 Sleep and Epigenetic Age: Insufficient Sleep Accelerates the “Biological Clock”

The connection between sleep and aging clocks is a recent focus in anti-aging research:

- **Sleep deprivation changes DNA methylation:** Short-term total sleep deprivation (40 hours) can alter blood DNA methylation profiles (some sites involve immune and stress pathways), suggesting that “acute sleep debt” leaves epigenetic traces. Multiple cross-sectional studies have reported a trend toward accelerated epigenetic age in people with chronic short sleep (moderate effect size, consistent direction).
- **Coupling of clock genes and methylation:** CLOCK:BMAL1 directly drives transcription of metabolic genes such as NAMPT, while the activity of DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs) and TET enzymes is itself regulated by circadian rhythms. **Circadian oscillation is the “metronome” that maintains the epigenome**; circadian disruption may accelerate epigenetic aging through methylation drift (see the epigenetic-clock mechanism in [02-aging-clocks](../01-foundations/02-aging-clocks.md)).
- **Sleep interventions and epigenetic age (preliminary evidence):** Some studies of CPAP treatment in OSA patients have observed slowing of epigenetic age. Studies of epigenetic-clock changes after CBT-I improves sleep in insomnia patients are ongoing. The direct evidence chain for “better sleep = epigenetic anti-aging” is not yet complete, although the mechanistic pathway is clear.

**Clinical significance:** For individuals, regular sleep is the least expensive means of “maintaining epigenetic age”—requiring no supplements or devices, only a stable rhythm (regular schedule + morning light + screen limits). This elevates sleep from a “healthy habit” to an “epigenetic anti-aging prescription” (echoing the discussion of NAD+ rhythms in Section 4).

## IV. Connections to the NO / NAD+ / Longevity Network (A Distinctive Perspective of This Resource)

**Sleep and NO (nitric oxide):** Sleep is the “daily repair window for the endothelial–NO system.” Sleep restriction activates the sympathetic and renin–angiotensin systems and increases oxidative stress, causing superoxide radicals to oxidize and consume NO into peroxynitrite (ONOO−), while eNOS phosphorylation declines. Even 24 hours of sleep deprivation significantly impairs FMD (Dettoni 2012). Intermittent hypoxia and reoxygenation in OSA are a stronger source of NO damage: every hypoxic event triggers a ROS burst → NO scavenging → downregulation of endothelial eNOS expression (Jelic 2008), forming the chain “snoring → hypoxia → NO↓ → hypertension/atrial fibrillation.” Some of CPAP’s blood-pressure effect is achieved by restoring NO bioavailability. Melatonin also interacts with NO: as a direct free-radical scavenger, it reduces ONOO− formation; mechanistic studies show it can upregulate eNOS phosphorylation and inhibit eNOS uncoupling, providing vascular protection (see the NO double-edged-sword framework in 01-foundations/03-no-basics.md). Conversely, NO also participates in sleep regulation—brain NO promotes slow-wave activity through the sGC/cGMP pathway, forming bidirectional regulation.

**Sleep and NAD+/SIRT1:** This is the most distinctive connection in this resource. NAMPT is the rate-limiting enzyme in NAD+ salvage synthesis, and its transcription is directly driven by CLOCK:BMAL1, causing NAD+ concentrations to oscillate circadianly (Ramsey 2009; two concurrent papers by Nakahata 2009, *Science*). NAD+ in turn feeds back to close the clock loop by SIRT1-mediated deacetylation of BMAL1/PER2 (Nakahata 2008, *Cell*). Thus **sleep/circadian disruption = flattened NAMPT oscillations → lower NAD+ peak → impaired SIRT1 activity → reduced mitochondrial biogenesis (PGC-1α) and autophagy**. This complements the NAD+ precursor strategy discussed in 04-drugs/01-rentosertib.md: supplementing NMN/NR without repairing circadian rhythms is like adding water to a leaking bucket. This mechanism underlies the high prevalence of metabolic syndrome in shift workers. Sleep optimization is therefore “endogenous NAD+ rhythm management”; zero-cost circadian intervention takes priority over exogenous NAD+ supplementation.

**Sleep, mitochondria, and muscle:** Mitochondrial quality control (mitophagy) peaks at night—autophagic flux rises during sleep and damaged mitochondria are cleared (partly driven by NAD+/SIRT1 rhythms). A single night of sleep deprivation reduces skeletal-muscle mitochondrial protein synthesis and increases oxidative stress. Deep-sleep GH pulses (see 1.3) drive muscle protein synthesis and tissue repair—**“muscle growth occurs mainly during deep sleep” is a physiological prerequisite for strength-training gains**. This explains the biological basis of “sleep well, train better” for resistance-training populations: sleep-deprived people have significantly impaired gains in muscle mass and strength. In addition, insufficient sleep raises nighttime cortisol, which suppresses muscle synthesis, promotes breakdown, and worsens insulin resistance. Sleep, muscle, and metabolism form a tightly regulated triangle (echoing the recovery discussion in [02-exercise](02-exercise.md)).

**Sleep, chronic inflammation, and immunosenescence:** Sleep is the immune system’s “rhythmic anchor.” Sleep deprivation drives chronic inflammation through three pathways: ① activation of the sympathetic–adrenal axis, disruption of catecholamine and cortisol rhythms, and increased NF-κB activity (Irwin 2006); ② impaired nighttime mobilization of NK cells and naive T cells, reducing immune surveillance (Irwin 1994); ③ reduced glymphatic clearance, allowing inflammatory mediators to accumulate in the brain. Long-term consequences include accelerated “immunosenescence”—reduced thymic output, an aging T-cell repertoire, and increased inflammaging (see [04-immunosenescence](../03-immunity/04-immunosenescence.md)). **Sleep–inflammation–aging form a positive-feedback loop:** poor sleep → more inflammation → worse deep sleep → more inflammation. Breaking this cycle (CBT-I, a fixed schedule, OSA treatment) is one of the most cost-effective anti-inflammatory interventions, complementary to drug-based anti-inflammatory approaches such as senolytics ([03-senolytics](../04-drugs/03-senolytics.md)), in a “lifestyle + medication” strategy.

## V. Intervention Directions and Actionable Recommendations

An actionable protocol ranked by evidence strength (consistent with the three-stage framework in 01-daily-checklist.md):

1. **Fixed schedule (Evidence A):** Keep the wake-time anchor within ±30 minutes; do not shift by more than one hour on weekends. Social jet lag >2 hours is associated with metabolic syndrome (Wittmann 2006; Parsons 2015). Catch-up sleep only partly restores cognition and can shift circadian phase.
2. **Morning light (Evidence A):** Get 10–30 minutes of outdoor light within 30 minutes of waking (about 1,000–5,000 lux on an overcast day can still be effective). This suppresses melatonin and anchors the cortisol awakening response (CAR). Evening 460-nm blue light (screens) delays melatonin onset by about two hours (Chang 2015, *PNAS*); avoid screens for the hour before bed.
3. **Temperature (Evidence A–B):** Keep the bedroom at 18–20°C. Take a warm bath or foot bath at 38–40°C for 10–20 minutes, 1–2 hours before bed. Passive heating first raises core temperature, followed by a rebound decline; this widens the skin-to-core temperature gradient and shortens sleep-onset latency (meta-analysis, Haghayegh 2019, *Sleep Med Rev*).
4. **Caffeine (Evidence A):** Avoid after 2 p.m. Its half-life is 5–6 hours (range 1.5–9.5 hours between individuals, with large CYP1A2 fast/slow-metabolizer differences). It antagonizes adenosine A1/A2A receptors, blocking the accumulating Process-S signal.
5. **Alcohol (Evidence A–B):** Although drinking before bed may shorten sleep-onset latency, it reduces slow-wave and REM sleep, increases awakenings in the second half of the night, and increases apnea events. Risks are greater for slow ALDH2 metabolizers (common in East Asia).
6. **Naps (Evidence B):** Keep naps to 20–30 minutes and before 3 p.m. Long naps (>60 minutes) are associated with higher cardiovascular risk and all-cause mortality (Yamada 2015, *Sleep* meta-analysis), possibly reflecting sleep debt or nighttime fragmentation.
7. **For insomnia (Evidence A):** Prioritize CBT-I (including digital CBT-I). Use medication only if CBT-I fails or for short-term treatment of an acute phase; reserve melatonin for DSWPD/jet lag.
8. **Screen for OSA (Evidence A):** People who snore and have morning headaches and daytime sleepiness should be screened with STOP-BANG. CPAP is first-line for confirmed OSA; oral appliances may be considered for mild-to-moderate cases. Weight loss provides dose-dependent benefits for OSA.
9. **Wearable monitoring (Evidence C–B):** Consumer devices have moderate accuracy for sleep staging compared with PSG, but are clearly useful for tracking long-term trends (regularity, sleep efficiency, HRV) as feedback for self-optimization.

### 5.1 Why Sleep Ranks High in the “Anti-Aging Priorities”

There are three reasons for placing sleep in the top tier of anti-aging interventions (alongside exercise and above most supplements): **① Breadth:** a single intervention improves brain function (glymphatic clearance, memory), vascular function (NO restoration), metabolism (insulin sensitivity), immunity (NK/T cells), and epigenetics (circadian–methylation coupling), touching nearly all hallmarks of aging; **② Evidence strength:** harms from insufficient sleep (metabolic, immune, cognitive, vascular) are supported by rigorous human experiments (Donga 2010, Irwin 2006, Dettoni 2012, etc.), not merely observational inference; **③ Zero cost and low barrier:** no prescription, equipment, or supplements are needed—only behavioral changes (fixed schedule, morning light, screen limits, caffeine limits). **The drawback is behavioral adherence:** unlike medication, sleep optimization does not work “passively”; it requires sustained self-discipline. This is the essential difference from supplements and drugs, and why [01-daily-checklist](01-daily-checklist.md) places sleep first among the “three foundation layers.” For people with insomnia or OSA, medical interventions (CBT-I, CPAP) take priority over any supplement; for the general population, protecting circadian regularity protects the foundation of anti-aging.

## VI. Open Questions and Controversies

1. **Can “catch-up sleep” reverse sleep debt?** Dinges 1997 showed that cumulative effects of chronic restriction were only partly reversed during recovery (deep sleep rebounded first, cognition recovered later). Whether weekend catch-up sleep can offset weekday losses still lacks high-quality RCTs.
2. **Individual differences in optimal duration:** Carriers of short-sleep genes such as DEC2/PROSER2 and ADRB1 can function normally on six hours, suggesting that a universal “7–8 hours” recommendation ignores genetic heterogeneity. Biological criteria for determining an individual’s optimal duration (e.g., alertness after waking, number of nighttime awakenings) have not been standardized.
3. **Proportion of deep sleep vs. total duration:** Deep sleep is crucial for glymphatic clearance and GH pulses, but whether interventions that extend deep sleep (such as slow-wave-enhancing acoustic stimulation) translate into long-term health benefits remains uncertain.
4. **Causal direction of the long-sleep side of the U-shaped curve:** Elevated risk associated with long sleep may mainly mark confounding from comorbidities, depression, or OSA; designs such as Mendelian randomization are needed to clarify causality.
5. **Association between sleeping pills and mortality:** The association in Kripke 2012 may involve reverse causation (insomnia itself is associated with mortality). Randomized evidence is lacking, so clinical decisions still favor “short-term use at the lowest effective dose.”
6. **Independent effects of sleep hygiene:** Sleep-hygiene education alone is less effective than CBT-I (review by Irish 2015), suggesting that knowledge must be paired with behavioral techniques (stimulus control, sleep restriction) to work.
