#02 Dietary Nitrates and NO

> Core point of view: Dietary nitrate is the **non-enzymatic bypass** for human NO synthesis-it does not rely on eNOS or oxygen partial pressure, but is more active in ischemia, hypoxia and acidic environments. One serving of beetroot juice significantly increases plasma nitrite and lowers blood pressure within 2–3 hours. This makes it the most actionable nutritional intervention for "decreased NO bioavailability" (see article 01).

## 0. History and Conceptual Framework

The physiological significance of dietary nitrate has experienced a reversal of the concept of "poison → drug". In the mid-20th century, nitrate/nitrite were only regarded as food additives and potential carcinogens (nitrosamines); in 1994, Benjamin et al. discovered that NO can be produced non-enzymatically in the stomach. In the late 1990s, the Lundberg team proposed the "nitrate-nitrite-NO pathway" hypothesis. Since the 2000s, a large number of RCTs have confirmed its antihypertensive and exercise-enhancing effects. Today this pathway is considered the second pillar of NO homeostasis in the body, complementary to the eNOS pathway:

```text
                        ┌─ Enzymatic pathway: L-arginine → eNOS → NO (requires O₂, requires BH4)
Two pathways of human NO production ─┤
                        └─ Non-enzymatic bypass: NO₃⁻ → NO₂⁻ → NO (more active in hypoxia/acidosis)
```

The former is driven by shear stress, hormones, drugs (statins, etc.), and the latter is driven by diet and oral flora. The two share the downstream sGC/cGMP signal (see Section 1 of Part 01), so the target of dietary intervention is essentially "to provide more NO precursors to the shared downstream".

**Historical node review**: In the early 1900s, nitrate was only used as a food preservative; around 1945, epidemiology focused on the association between nitrate and infants and young children with methemoglobinemia and gastric cancer, and it was once listed as a major environmental pollutant. The turning point occurred in the 1990s: Benjamin proposed in "Nature" in 1994 that non-enzymatic reduction of nitrite in the gastric acid environment can generate NO. In 1998, Lundberg's team confirmed that this reaction actually occurs in the human stomach and has physiological concentrations. In 2006, Larsen et al. used placebo control for the first time in the "New England Journal of Medicine" to prove that dietary nitrate can significantly reduce diastolic blood pressure in healthy adults. In the following more than ten years, the complete chain of evidence from mechanism to clinical practice was gradually closed. Understanding this reversal of concepts is crucial to grasping the grading of subsequent evidence - many early "nitrates are harmful" conclusions came from industrial nitrates or nitrite exposure in processed meat, and cannot be simply extrapolated to nitrates from vegetable sources.

## 1. Nitrate–nitrite–NO pathway: human circulation beyond the intestinal tract

The classic L-arginine/eNOS pathway relies on oxygen and cofactors, and its efficiency plummets during hypoxia. The dietary nitrate pathways are exactly complementary:

```text
Dietary nitrate NO3⁻ (vegetables/beetroot)
   │  Intestinal absorption (small intestine, nearly complete absorption, bioavailability ~100%)
   ▼
Plasma NO3⁻ → salivary glands (parotid/submandibular) active concentration
   │  Salivary NO3⁻ concentration reaches 10-20x plasma levels
   ▼
Oral microbiota reduction: NO3⁻ → NO2⁻  (nitrate reductase, key step!)
   │  Main species: Rothia (R. mucilaginosa / R. dentocariosa),
   │            Neisseria, Veillonella, Actinomyces
   ▼
Swallowing → gastric acid: NO2⁻ + H⁺ → HNO2 → NO↑  (non-enzymatic reduction, gastric NO can reach ppm levels)
   │
   ▼
Tissue nitrite reductases entering circulation (activated in hypoxia/low pH):
   ├─ Deoxyhemoglobin (deoxyHb)  → NO + methemoglobin (nitrite reductase activity)
   ├─ Xanthine oxidase (XO)       → NO (also produces O2⁻)
   ├─ Deoxymyoglobin (cardiac/skeletal muscle)
   ├─ Aldehyde oxidase / cytochrome P450 / eNOS (can also reduce nitrite in hypoxia)
   └─ Mitochondrial electron transport chain complex III
   ▼
NO → sGC → cGMP → vasodilation (mechanism in Article 01)
```

**The key link is the oral cavity**: Salivary glands concentrate plasma nitrate 10–20 times into saliva, and oral commensal bacteria (mainly Roseburia and Neisseria) reduce it to nitrite. **After killing oral flora with mouthwash or antibiotics, the increase in plasma nitrite and the blood pressure-lowering effect are almost completely eliminated** - this is the most famous causal evidence of this pathway. Gastric acid further non-enzymatically converts nitrite into NO (high concentrations of NO in the stomach also have defensive significance in killing pathogenic bacteria).

### 1.1 The dynamics of pathways and the deep significance of "intestinal-salivary circulation"

The term "enterosalivary circulation" illustrates the circulatory nature of this pathway: nitrate is not used once, but shuttles repeatedly between blood-saliva-stomach-blood. Specifically, ingested nitrate is almost completely absorbed into the plasma in the proximal small intestine, and is then actively concentrated into saliva by the salivary glands through the nitrate/iodide transporter (sialin, SLC17A1). The concentration in saliva can reach 10–20 times that of plasma. After nitrate in saliva is reduced to nitrite by oral flora, it is swallowed and enters the stomach, where it is partially non-enzymatically converted to NO in acidic gastric juice. The remaining nitrite and residual nitrate are then absorbed back into the circulation by the small intestine. Thus, a single ingestion of nitrate can produce a low-level nitrite spike in the body that lasts for several hours—explaining why the blood pressure effects can persist for 3–6 hours, well beyond the circulatory half-life of nitrite itself, which is approximately 20–30 minutes.

**Kinematic characteristics of the pathway**: ① Nitrate is almost completely absorbed in the proximal small intestine (bioavailability is close to 100%), and the unabsorbed part enters the colon and is utilized by intestinal flora; ② Plasma nitrite peaks about 2–3 hours after a single ingestion, and the circulating half-life of nitrite is only about 20–30 minutes, so **dose-dependent continuous supplementation (rather than a single large dose)** can maintain homeostasis; ③This pathway is not affected by eNOS Inhibitor (L-NAME) affects and can work independently of the enzymatic pathway - this explains why dietary nitrate can still effectively supply NO in the elderly and cardiovascular disease populations with impaired eNOS function (see Section 6 of Article 01).

### 1.2 Functional complementation with the eNOS pathway: the meaning of "more active under hypoxia"

The key difference between the two pathways is not only "enzymatic vs non-enzymatic", but also **oxygen partial pressure sensitivity**. eNOS catalyzes the oxidation of L-arginine requires molecular oxygen as a substrate, so its efficiency drops sharply when blood oxygen decreases; on the contrary, the key reduction steps in the dietary nitrate bypass—the reduction of nitrite by deoxyhemoglobin, deoxymyoglobin, and xanthine oxidase—are actually activated under low oxygen partial pressure. This forms an exquisite physiological complement: when the body is hypoxic, the supply of eNOS pathway decreases, while the supply of nitrate bypass pathway increases, and the two together maintain the relative stability of tissue NO supply.

This feature is of great significance for sports and ischemic scenarios. The sudden drop in local oxygen partial pressure during high-intensity contraction of skeletal muscles is the window for the nitrate bypass pathway to work (see Section 3.2 for details). In animal experiments, nitrate supplementation significantly reduced infarct size in the ischemia-reperfusion injury model, and the mechanism was related to enhanced hypoxia reduction. In other words, dietary nitrate is not a simple "NO substitute", but a "hypoxia compensator" - it preferentially releases NO where NO is most scarce and needed most.

## 2. Beetroot: a natural carrier of nitrates

| Ingredients | Quantity (per 100 g raw beetroot) | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Nitrate | About 110–250 mg (typically around 150 mg) | Varies greatly due to soil fertilization and variety; organic cultivation is usually higher |
| Betalain | About 40–100 mg (mainly betanin) | Water-soluble red pigment with antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities |
| Dietary fiber | About 2–3 g | Pectin-based, helpful for intestinal flora (some strains also contain nitrate reductase) |
| Folate/Potassium/Manganese | Folic acid approximately 80–110 µg | Provides approximately 20% of daily requirements per 100 g |

**Practical conversion**: 250 mL of commercial beetroot juice concentrate (such as Beet It) contains approximately 400–500 mg of nitrate; a medium-sized beetroot (approximately 150 g) contains approximately 200–300 mg. Plasma nitrite peaks at 2–3 hours after ingestion, and blood pressure effects persist for 3–6 hours or longer.

**Why beetroot is used as a research vehicle**: Beetroot is widely used in RCTs because of its high nitrate content and its standardizability - commercially available concentrated juices can consistently deliver set doses from batch to batch (e.g. Beet It contains 6.4 mmol nitrate per 70 mL), allowing for double-blind placebo-controlled designs (the placebo uses the same juice with the nitrates removed). In contrast, the nitrate content of whole vegetables fluctuates greatly due to soil, variety, and season, making it difficult to accurately control the amount. It is important to emphasize, however, that the effects in beetroot juice were primarily driven by nitrates - nitrate-removed beetroot juice (nitrate-depleted version) produced no antihypertensive or locomotor effects in most trials, providing contrasting evidence that nitrate is the active ingredient. The independent contribution of minor components such as betalains and polyphenols is uncertain, but if any, it is minor.

## 3. Clinical Evidence

### 3.1 Blood pressure

- Multiple randomized controlled trials have shown that 250 mL of beetroot juice (containing approximately 400–500 mg nitrates) reduced systolic blood pressure by **4–10 mmHg** and diastolic blood pressure by 2–5 mmHg 2.5–3 hours after ingestion, with effects lasting 3–6 hours after ingestion.
- Groundbreaking study: Larsen 2006 (NEJM 355:2792-2793) Ingesting sodium nitrate (equivalent to approximately 500 mg of nitrate daily) in healthy volunteers for 3 days resulted in a significant reduction in diastolic blood pressure of approximately 3.7 mmHg - the first placebo-controlled trial to demonstrate that dietary nitrate lowers blood pressure in healthy individuals.
- Direct evidence of entero-salivary circulation: Webb 2008 (Hypertension 51:784-790) Ingestion of 500 mL of beetroot juice in healthy volunteers reduced systolic blood pressure by approximately 10.4 mmHg and diastolic blood pressure by approximately 8 2.5–3 hours later. mmHg, and the increase in plasma nitrite is significantly related to the decrease in blood pressure - this study is the first to link the antihypertensive effect of beetroot juice with intestinal-salivary circulation and restoration of oral flora.
- Sustained blood pressure reduction in hypertensive patients: Kapil 2015 (Hypertension 65:320-327) Phase II double-blind placebo-controlled trial, 68 hypertensive patients took 250 mL beetroot juice (approximately 6 mmol nitrate) daily for 4 weeks, the average office blood pressure decreased by **7.7/2.4 mmHg**, and the 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure decreased by **7.7/5.2 mmHg** - This is the strongest sustained evidence that nitrates can reduce blood pressure so far, and the magnitude is close to the single-drug effect of some first-line antihypertensive drugs.
- Meta-analysis (Siervo et al., Hypertension 2013): including 16 trials, nitrate supplementation reduced systolic blood pressure by an average of **4.4 mmHg** (95% CI 0.5–8.3).
- Mechanism: Nitrite is reduced to NO in hypoxic tissues, dilating resistance blood vessels; both normal blood pressure and pre-hypertensive people benefit, and the effect is more obvious in people with pre-hypertension.

### 3.2 Athletic Performance

- Meta-analysis shows that nitrate supplementation improves** endurance exercise performance (time trial/time to failure) by 10–15%** (typical effect 2–4% power output gain; some studies are higher).
- Foundational study on dose-response relationship: Wylie 2013 (J Appl Physiol 115:325-336) compared 70/140/280 mL concentrated beetroot juice (containing 4.2/8.4/16.8 mmol nitrate respectively) and found that medium and high doses significantly increased the power output of moderate-intensity exercise and improved time trial performance, and there was an obvious dose gradient.
- Mechanism: ① Reduce muscle oxygen consumption (mitochondrial respiration efficiency↑, P/O ratio optimization); ② Improve skeletal muscle blood flow and contraction coupling; ③ Improve calcium processing efficiency of fast muscle fibers.
- Vanhatalo 2010 (Am J Physiol Regul Integr Comp Physiol 299:R1121-31 and J Appl Physiol 109:135-148) confirmed that acute nitrate supplementation can improve muscle contraction efficiency and oxygen uptake kinetics during quadriceps stretching exercise, and blood pressure also decreases simultaneously - unifying the two major effects of "hypertension reduction" and "exercise enhancement" into the same physiological pathway.
- There are individual differences in the effect: those with low baseline NO levels (elderly people, people with cardiovascular disease) will benefit more; elite athletes with high baseline NO levels will have a weakened effect.

### 3.3 Dose Effect: Evidence from RCTs in 2026

| DOSAGE | MAIN EFFECTS |
|---|---|
| 200 mg Nitrate | No significant effect (below threshold) |
| **400 mg** | **Significant improvement in flow-mediated dilation (FMD)** (endothelial function) |
| **800 mg** | **Significant decrease in blood pressure** (vascular tone effect) |

This dose-response study (a randomized controlled trial published in 2026) suggests that endothelial function and blood pressure are sensitive to different doses—lower doses are needed to improve endothelial function (about 400 mg), and higher doses (about 800 mg) may be needed to reduce blood pressure. This has direct implications for supplementation strategies: **Whether the goal is "endothelial health" or "blood pressure reduction", the dosage strategy should be different**.

### 3.4 Elderly people

- The elderly have a decreased NO pathway (see Section 6 of Part 01) and are the group that benefit the most from nitrate supplementation: multiple studies have shown that the improvement in FMD and the decrease in blood pressure in elderly subjects are greater than those in young subjects.
- Improvements in cerebral blood flow, particularly in frontal lobe white matter, were also observed in small trials in older adults with mild cognitive impairment.
- Jackson 2018 (Am J Clin Nutr / Nutrition Reviews 76:348-371) Systematic review and meta-analysis pointed out that the evidence for nitrate/nitrite supplementation in improving vascular function (FMD, blood pressure) is strong, but the evidence for cognition and cerebral blood flow is still limited, and the study samples are generally small - you need to be cautious when interpreting "nitrate brain protection".

### 3.5 Population Epidemiology: Vegetable Nitrate and Cardiovascular Outcomes

In addition to clinical trials, observational cohort studies provide evidence that nitrates are associated with real-world cardiovascular outcomes:

- **Bondonno 2021 (Eur J Epidemiol 36:8)**: Based on 53,150 adults (52–60 years) free of baseline cardiovascular disease from the Danish Diet, Cancer and Health cohort, followed for a mean of approximately 23 years. The highest quintile of vegetable nitrate intake (median approximately 60 mg/day, approximately 1 cup of leafy greens) was associated with an approximately 26% lower risk of **peripheral artery disease** and a significantly lower risk of ischemic heart disease compared with the lowest quintile (approximately 23 mg/day); approximately 21.9% of the cardiovascular risk reduction was mediated by a decrease in systolic blood pressure. This magnitude (approximately 60 mg/day vegetable nitrate) is well below the dose commonly used in clinical trials (400–800 mg), suggesting that **daily vegetable intake may provide substantial cardiovascular benefits** without the need to pursue high-dose supplements.

### 3.6 Grading of evidence

| Evidence | Content | Level |
|---|---|---|
| Mouthwash blocking test | Antibacterial mouthwash eliminates plasma nitrite and antihypertensive effects | A (mechanism confirmation) |
| Blood pressure RCT meta-set | SBP reduction 4.4 mmHg (16 trials) | A |
| Continuous RCT in patients with hypertension | Kapil 2015: 4-week ambulatory blood pressure reduction of 7.7/5.2 mmHg | A |
| Sports Performance Collection | Time Trial Boost 10–15% | A |
| Population-based prospective cohort | Bondonno 2021: 26% risk reduction in peripheral artery disease | B (observational) |

| Cerebral blood flow study in the elderly | Small, limited sample | C |

## 4. Factors affecting the effect

| Factor | Mechanism | Effect on NO pathway |
|---|---|---|
| Antibacterial mouthwash | Kills oral nitrate-reducing bacteria (Rosella/Neisseria) | Plasma nitrite ↓ ~60%, antihypertensive effect disappears |
| Antibiotics | Broad-spectrum antibiotics disrupt oral/intestinal flora | Also impair nitrate reduction |
| Proton pump inhibitors/acid-suppressing drugs | Insufficient gastric acid → weakened non-enzymatic reduction (NO production in the stomach↓) | Weakened gastric reduction link (circulating nitrite has less effect) |
| Smoking | Tobacco burning produces CO that competes for binding to sGC heme; smokers' oral flora composition changes | NO sensitivity ↓, nitrate effect weakened |
| Chewing and saliva secretion | Full chewing promotes the concentration of salivary nitrate | Insufficient secretion will reduce the amount ↓ |
| Individual differences in oral flora | Rosebella abundance varies from person to person (related to age and diet) | An important source of large differences in individual responses |
| Caffeine/Polyphenols | Xanthine oxidase inhibitors (allopurinols) or tea polyphenols may interfere with nitrite reduction | Evidence is mixed, impact is small |

**Individual differences are the core practical difficulty**: The peak plasma nitrite produced by the same dose of nitrate in different individuals can differ several times, and the blood pressure effect is more obvious. A considerable part of this difference comes from the composition of oral flora - the abundance of nitrate-reducing bacteria such as Roseburia and Neisseria decreases with age and a high-sugar and low-fiber diet. Antibacterial mouthwash and even antibacterial ingredients in some toothpastes can weaken the reducing ability. Therefore, the answer to "Is eating beetroot juice effective?" is highly individualized: the effect will be significant in those with healthy flora, but the effect may be greatly reduced in those with compromised flora (elderly people, people who use mouthwash for a long time, users of broad-spectrum antibiotics).

## 5. Security

### 5.1 Nitrosamine risk (theoretical concern, actual low risk)

- Nitrite can form N-nitrosamines (carcinogens) under certain conditions (high temperature, acidity, coexistence with amines).
- **Key Analysis**: The main source of dietary nitrates is vegetables. Vegetables are rich in vitamin C and polyphenols, both of which **inhibit** the formation of nitrosamines. The risk of nitrosamines from nitrites in meat processing is different from that of nitrate vegetables. Epidemiological studies have not found that vegetable nitrate intake is associated with an increased risk of cancer, and some studies have even shown a reduced risk of digestive tract cancers.
- The World Health Organization's ADI (allowable daily intake) for nitrate is 3.7 mg/kg body weight (based on nitrate); approximately 260 mg/day for a 70 kg adult - **A single serving of beetroot juice (400–500 mg) is beyond the ADI**, but the ADI is mainly based on nitrite toxicity settings, and the applicability of nitrates from vegetable sources is controversial. Most toxicological and epidemiological evidence supports a positive benefit-risk ratio for vegetable nitrates.

**Old controversy about "high nitrate vegetables and gastric cancer"**: In the 1960s and 1970s, epidemiology reported a high incidence of gastric cancer in areas where high nitrate vegetables were consumed, and based on this, the "nitrate-nitrosamine-gastric cancer" hypothesis was proposed. However, follow-up studies failed to consistently confirm this link, and found that consuming vitamin C and polyphenols together blocked the formation of nitrosamines in the stomach. Modern large-scale cohorts (including Bondonno 2021) have instead shown that vegetable nitrates are associated with a reduced risk of digestive tract cancers. The core lesson of this controversy is: **Distinguish the source**—nitrites in processed meats/preserved foods and nitrates in vegetables are not equivalent in their nitrosamine risks. The WHO/FAO Joint Expert Committee (JECFA) also clearly pointed out in its assessment that there is insufficient evidence on nitrate exposure and carcinogenic risks from vegetables, and the setting of the ADI mainly serves the purpose of regulating drinking water and food additives.

### 5.2 Methemoglobinemia (clinically rare)

- Excessive nitrite can oxidize hemoglobin to methemoglobin (MetHb), losing its oxygen-carrying capacity.
- It will almost never occur with normal dietary intake in adults (acute, large-dose exposure is required, such as contaminated well water or accidental ingestion of industrial nitrites); infants and young children (<6 months) have low gastric acid and immature MetHb reductase activity and are susceptible groups - **Infants under 6 months of age should avoid vegetable juices with high nitrate content**.

### 5.3 Others

- Beetroot juice contains oxalic acid, so patients with kidney stones (calcium oxalate type) should be careful.
- Users of antihypertensive drugs may experience additive antihypertensive effects and should monitor their blood pressure.
- **Dose Toxicology**: Nitrate acute toxicity is extremely low (rat LD50 approximately 3–4 g/kg); clinical supplemental doses (hundreds of mg) are well below toxic doses. What really requires vigilance is acute nitrite poisoning (lethal dose in adults is about 2–4 g), but the amount of nitrite produced through the dietary route is limited by the reduction rate of oral flora and gastric acid consumption, making it almost impossible to reach poisoning levels. The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) reassessed in 2023 that nitrate intake from vegetable sources does not pose a health risk to the general population.

## 5.4 Precautions for special groups of people

| Crowd | Risk | Recommendations |
|---|---|---|
| Infants under 6 months old | Susceptible to methemoglobinemia (immature MetHb reductase, low gastric acid) | Avoid high nitrate vegetable juices/complementary foods |
| Pregnant women | Theoretically MetHb can cross the placenta, but vegetable nitrates have no evidence of adverse pregnancy outcomes | Maintain normal vegetable intake |
| People taking PDE5 inhibitors | Overlapping with NO donors → Risk of hypotension | Avoid large-dose supplementation on the same day |
| Chronic kidney disease/hyperkalemia | Beetroot is high in potassium (approximately 300 mg/100 g) | Limit and monitor blood potassium |
| Calcium oxalate kidney stones | Beetroot has high oxalic acid content | Limit intake and drink plenty of water |

| Arugula (arugula/rocket) | 250–450 (highest grade) | Cruciferous, contains glucosinolates |
| Spinach | 150–300 | Contains oxalic acid, which can be partially removed by blanching |
| Celery | 100–250 | Contains apigenin (flavonoid) |

| Lettuce | 100–200 | Everyday leafy vegetables |
| Carrots | 50–100 | Medium |
| Potatoes | 50–100 | Medium |

**Practical Tips**: No need to stick to a single "superfood" - 200–400 g of leafy green vegetables (arugula, spinach, lettuce, etc.) per day provides 300–1000 mg of nitrates, which is more than most clinically tested doses. The advantage of beetroot is that it is a standardized concentrated product (with controllable dosage and strong research evidence). **Prioritize incorporating nitrates into daily vegetable patterns** rather than relying on supplements: Bondonno 2021 showed that vegetable nitrates of approximately 60 mg/day were associated with cardiovascular benefits, well below clinical trial doses. If a specific goal is pursued (e.g., lowering blood pressure in patients with hypertension), higher doses are needed, in which case standardized beetroot juice is more practical.

## 7. Open questions

1. **Safety endpoints of long-term supplementation**: There are no large-scale hard endpoint (cardiovascular events/cancer) trials of nitrate supplementation for months to years, and long-term safety relies on inference.
2. **Plasticity of oral flora**: Can Roseburia and Neisseria be directionally enriched through probiotics/diet engineering, so that "nitrate reducing ability" becomes a trainable phenotype?
3. **Fine characterization of dose-effect**: The separation phenomenon of "400 mg tube endothelium/800 mg tube blood pressure" in the 2026 RCT needs to be independently replicated and its mechanism explored (whether it involves differences in NO sensitivity in different vascular beds).
4. **Interactions with drugs**: Are there additive benefits from combining nitrate supplementation with statins and ACE inhibitors? Is there a risk of hypotension when combined with a PDE5 inhibitor (sildenafil)?
5. **Individualization of Exercise Performance Effects**: How are "responders" predicted by baseline FMD/nitrite levels?
6. **Integration with routine treatment of hypertension**: Kapil 2015 showed that nitrate can reduce ambulatory blood pressure by 7.7/5.2 mmHg, which is close to some first-line antihypertensive drugs. However, whether it can be used as an independent treatment or as an auxiliary reduction treatment requires direct head-to-head comparison with standard antihypertensive drugs.
