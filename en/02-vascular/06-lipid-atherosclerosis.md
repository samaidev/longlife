# Lipid Metabolism and Atherosclerosis: The Complete Causal Chain from Oxidized LDL to Plaque Rupture

> **Key point:** Atherosclerosis is a progressive disease in which apolipoprotein B (ApoB)-containing lipoprotein particles are retained in the intima and oxidatively modified, then taken up without feedback inhibition by macrophages to form foam cells, alongside chronic inflammation, smooth-muscle proliferation, and fibrosis. LDL-C is a **causal** risk factor, established by both Mendelian randomization and randomized controlled trials. HDL-mediated reverse cholesterol transport (RCT) is an endogenous protective mechanism—but the “HDL concentration hypothesis” has been refuted by the failure of CETP inhibitors. This chapter reviews the lipoprotein-metabolism landscape, oxidative modification as an initiating event, foam-cell formation, plaque progression, inflammatory amplification, the evidence chain for lipid-lowering, the independent risk of Lp(a), and deeper connections with NO signaling and vascular aging.

## 1. Core Concepts

Atherosclerosis is a lipid-driven chronic inflammatory disease of the arterial intima that develops over decades: from fatty streaks in childhood, to fibrous plaques in middle age, to high-risk vulnerable plaques in older age. The initiating event is **retention of ApoB-containing LDL particles in the intima and their oxidative modification to oxLDL**. Monocytes then enter the intima and differentiate into macrophages. Through scavenger receptors (SR-A, CD36, LOX-1), they take up oxLDL without feedback inhibition and become **foam cells**, forming the lipid core. Reverse cholesterol transport (RCT), mediated by HDL, ABCA1/ABCG1, and SR-BI, returns peripheral cholesterol to the liver for removal and is an endogenous defense against this process. In relation to anti-aging, atherosclerosis is the most prevalent lethal phenotype among vascular diseases that accompany aging. Vascular aging (endothelial dysfunction, smooth-muscle-cell senescence, and matrix remodeling) directly accelerates plaque formation and rupture, making lipid control and restoration of NO signaling foundations of longevity interventions.

## 2. The Lipoprotein-Metabolism Landscape: Which Cholesterol Is Truly Atherogenic?

### 2.1 Lipoprotein Families

Lipoproteins consist of a **hydrophobic core (triglycerides and cholesteryl esters) plus an amphipathic surface (phospholipids, free cholesterol, and apolipoproteins)**. In order of increasing density:

| Lipoprotein | Main apolipoproteins | Core contents | Main function |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chylomicron (CM) | ApoB-48, ApoE | Triglycerides | Transports dietary fat from intestine to periphery |
| VLDL | ApoB-100, ApoE, ApoC | Triglycerides | Transports endogenous triglycerides from liver |
| IDL | ApoB-100, ApoE | Triglycerides/cholesterol | Intermediate in conversion of VLDL to LDL |
| LDL | ApoB-100 | Cholesteryl esters | Delivers cholesterol to peripheral tissues (major atherogenic particle) |
| HDL | ApoA-I | Cholesteryl esters | Reverse cholesterol transport (RCT) |
| Lp(a) | ApoB-100 + apolipoprotein(a) | Cholesteryl esters | Independent prothrombotic/proatherogenic particle |

**Key assessment:** Each LDL particle contains one ApoB-100 molecule, and ApoB is the “entry pass” that allows particles to enter the arterial wall and trigger inflammation. Thus, “non-HDL-C” (total cholesterol minus HDL-C) and ApoB concentration provide a more comprehensive measure of atherogenic burden than LDL-C alone: they include all ApoB-containing particles in LDL, IDL, VLDL, and Lp(a).

### 2.2 The Full Reverse Cholesterol Transport (RCT) Pathway

RCT is the body’s only clearance route for returning peripheral cholesterol—especially from arterial-wall macrophages—to the liver:

```text
Cholesterol accumulation in peripheral macrophages (foam cells)
   │
   │ Free cholesterol (FC) esterified by ACAT into cholesteryl esters (CE), stored in lipid droplets
   ▼
ABCA1 exports FC to lipid-poor ApoA-I (forming nascent pre-β-HDL)
   │   [ABCA1 mutation → Tangier disease, extremely low HDL, macrophage cholesterol deposition]
   ▼
LCAT (lecithin–cholesterol acyltransferase) esterifies FC to CE; spherical mature HDL grows
   │
   ├─→ Direct transfer of CE to hepatocytes via SR-BI (selective uptake, HDL pathway)
   ├─→ CETP transfers CE to LDL/VLDL in exchange for TG, then indirect clearance via LDL receptors
   └─→ Hepatic CE excreted from the body as bile acids/cholesterol (final clearance)
```

**Molecular details:** ABCA1 transcription is regulated by the nuclear receptor LXR (whose ligand is oxysterol). When cholesterol-loaded, RXR/LXR heterodimers upregulate ABCA1/ABCG1—macrophages’ first-line response to cholesterol overload. People with ABCA1 deficiency (Tangier disease) have virtually no HDL, orange-yellow tonsils, and premature atherosclerosis. **RCT “quality” matters more than HDL concentration**: anti-atherogenic capacity depends on the lipid-accepting ability of ApoA-I, expression of ABC transporters, and LCAT/CETP activity—not simply the HDL-C value.

### 2.3 Failure of the HDL-Concentration Hypothesis

Three large RCTs of CETP inhibitors failed in succession: torcetrapib (raised HDL by 60–70% but increased mortality), dalcetrapib (raised HDL by 30% without benefit), and evacetrapib (raised HDL by 130% without benefit). These trials showed that **“raising HDL-C alone protects the heart” is an illusion**. Possible reasons: (1) HDL-C measures cholesterol “cargo,” not transport flux; (2) although CETP inhibition raises HDL, it may reduce net RCT flux by blocking transfer of CE from HDL to liver/LDL; and (3) many people with high HDL have **dysfunctional HDL** (oxidized, glycated, or modified ApoA-I) with reduced anti-inflammatory/antioxidant capacity. Current consensus: **HDL-C is a risk-prediction measure, not a treatment target**. Research has shifted toward ApoA-I mimetic peptides (e.g., CSL112, to accelerate RCT) and measures of HDL functional quality (cholesterol efflux capacity, CEC).

## 3. Oxidative Modification of LDL and the Initiation of Atherosclerosis

### 3.1 The Response-to-Retention Hypothesis

LDL that enters the intima can normally be cleared through the endothelial barrier. However, when plasma LDL concentration is high, endothelial permeability is increased (more evident when NO is insufficient; see Section 8), or blood flow is slow at arterial branch points (prolonging LDL retention), LDL becomes **retained** on extracellular-matrix proteoglycans in the intima, especially heparan sulfate/chondroitin sulfate. Retained LDL is exposed to oxidative stress in the intimal microenvironment (reactive oxygen species released by activated endothelial cells, macrophages, and smooth muscle), and its lipids and ApoB proteins undergo oxidative modification:

```text
LDL ──(endothelial/macrophage/smooth-muscle ROS)──→ minimally modified LDL (mmLDL)
   │  Lipid peroxidation: polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) → lipid peroxides → reactive aldehydes (4-HNE, MDA)
   │  ApoB-100 lysine residues covalently modified by MDA/HNE → epitopes altered (no longer recognized by LDL receptor)
   ▼
Heavily oxidized oxLDL
   │  No longer recognized by LDL receptor (LDLR)
   │  Recognized by scavenger receptors (SR-A/CD36/LOX-1) ← key: scavenger receptors lack feedback inhibition
   ▼
Unrestrained uptake by macrophages → foam cells
```

**Core mechanistic anchor:** oxLDL is pathogenic because it is **both** a ligand (taken up by scavenger receptors) and a signal (activates inflammatory pathways in endothelial cells and macrophages). Binding of oxLDL to endothelial LOX-1 upregulates adhesion molecules and promotes endothelial apoptosis; uptake by macrophage CD36/SR-A drives foam-cell formation and activates the pro-inflammatory NF-κB cascade.

### 3.2 Scavenger Receptors and Foam Cells

| Receptor | Cellular distribution | Ligands | Features |
|---|---|---|---|
| SR-A (MSR1) | Macrophages | Modified LDL, polyanionic ligands | No feedback inhibition; uptake is effectively unlimited |
| CD36 | Macrophages/endothelium/platelets | oxLDL (oxidized-phospholipid epitopes), fatty acids | Involved in foam-cell formation and inflammation |
| LOX-1 (OLR1) | Endothelial cells/macrophages | oxLDL, AGEs | Mediates endothelial uptake/apoptosis; expression is reduced by shear stress |

**The “no-feedback” problem in foam-cell formation:** Normal macrophages take up cholesterol through LDL receptors (LDLR); when intracellular cholesterol rises, LDLR is downregulated (negative feedback). But expression of scavenger receptors SR-A/CD36 is not regulated by intracellular cholesterol. Thus oxLDL uptake is unrestrained, cholesteryl esters accumulate in lysosomes, lipid droplets form, and the cell swells into a foam cell. Foam-cell fate branches: (1) cholesterol export via ABCA1/ABCG1 (the protective RCT route); or (2) apoptosis/necrosis, releasing lipids and pro-inflammatory factors to form the lipid core (harmful).

### 3.3 The Progression Spectrum of Atherosclerotic Plaques

```text
Fatty streak (childhood–young adulthood; reversible)
   │  Focal accumulation of intimal foam cells; asymptomatic; may regress
   ▼
Fibrous plaque (middle age)
   │  Smooth-muscle-cell proliferation/migration into intima; collagen forms a fibrous cap over lipid core
   │  Cap thickness determines stability
   ▼
Vulnerable plaque (older age; high risk)
   │  Thin fibrous cap (<65 µm) + large necrotic core + many inflammatory cells (macrophages/M)
   │  Matrix metalloproteinases (MMP-2/9) degrade collagen → cap thins
   ▼
Plaque rupture → tissue-factor exposure → platelet aggregation → thrombus → myocardial infarction/stroke
```

**Quantitative anchor:** Autopsy and imaging studies show that approximately 60–70% of acute coronary events result from rupture of a **thin-cap fibroatheroma (TCFA)**. Most others are triggered by plaque erosion (endothelial denudation without rupture) or calcified nodules. Formation of the necrotic core depends on two factors: increased foam-cell apoptosis **and impaired efferocytosis**—normally, macrophages engulf apoptotic cells and clear debris, but plaque macrophages have impaired phagocytosis, so apoptotic cells remain and undergo secondary necrosis, releasing pro-inflammatory DAMPs and enlarging the necrotic core.

## 4. The Central Role of Inflammation in Atherosclerosis

### 4.1 The Bridge from Cholesterol to Inflammation: The NLRP3 Inflammasome

After cholesterol crystals (formed within foam cells and the necrotic core) are phagocytosed by macrophages, needle-shaped crystals can form in lysosomes and **rupture the lysosomal membrane**, releasing cathepsin B into the cytoplasm and activating the **NLRP3 inflammasome** (NACHT, LRR, and PYD domains-containing protein 3):

```text
Cholesterol crystals / oxLDL
   │
   ▼
Lysosomal membrane damage → cathepsin B release
   ▼
NLRP3 assembly (ASC adaptor + pro-caspase-1)
   ▼
Caspase-1 activation → cleavage of pro-IL-1β → mature IL-1β
   ▼
IL-1β binds IL-1R1 → NF-κB → IL-6, CRP, adhesion molecules upregulated
   │
   ▼
Amplification of systemic inflammatory cascade (source of elevated hsCRP)
```

**Evidence grades:** (1) Cholesterol crystals activate the NLRP3 inflammasome—Grade A (cell and genetic-mouse models; atherosclerosis is substantially reduced in NLRP3−/− mice); (2) IL-1β is a core cytokine in inflammatory amplification—Grade A (mechanistic evidence); (3) anti-IL-1β monoclonal antibody reduces cardiovascular events—CANTOS trial (see Section 6).

### 4.2 CANTOS: Clinical Validation of the Inflammation Hypothesis

The **CANTOS trial** (Canakinumab Anti-inflammatory Thrombosis Outcomes Study, 2017, Ridker et al., *NEJM*) was a landmark in atherosclerosis research. Among 10,061 patients with previous myocardial infarction and hsCRP≥2 mg/L, the anti-IL-1β monoclonal antibody canakinumab (150 mg every 3 months) **reduced major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE: nonfatal MI, nonfatal stroke, cardiovascular death) by 15% without lowering LDL-C** (HR 0.85, P=0.021), while lowering hsCRP in a dose-dependent manner. This “two birds with one stone” result demonstrated that (1) inflammation is a pathogenic pathway independent of lipids (anti-inflammatory treatment can be beneficial without lipid lowering); and (2) hsCRP is a modifiable readout of residual inflammatory risk. The cost was an **increase in serious and fatal infections**, highlighting the immune cost of broad IL-1β inhibition and the need for precision anti-inflammatory treatment.

## 6. Evidence Chain for Lipid-Lowering Treatment (Clinical and Experimental Details)

### 6.1 Statins: Establishing the LDL Hypothesis

Statins inhibit HMG-CoA reductase, upregulate hepatic LDL receptors, and lower LDL-C by approximately 30–50%. Landmark trials:

- **4S** (Scandinavian Simvastatin Survival Study, 1994, *Lancet*): 4,444 patients with coronary disease received simvastatin 20–40 mg/day and were followed for 5.4 years. All-cause mortality fell by 30% (RR 0.70, P=0.0003), establishing statins for secondary prevention.
- **CARE** (1996, *NEJM*): 4,159 patients with previous MI and a mean LDL of 139 mg/dL received pravastatin 40 mg; major coronary events fell by 24% over five years.
- **LIPID** (1998, *NEJM*): 9,014 patients with previous MI or unstable angina received pravastatin 40 mg; over 6.1 years, coronary death fell by 24% and all-cause mortality by 22%.
- **CTT meta-analysis** (Cholesterol Treatment Trialists’ Collaboration, 2010, *Lancet*): 26 trials and 170,000 participants. **Each 1 mmol/L (about 38.6 mg/dL) reduction in LDL lowers major vascular events by about 20–22%**—the strongest quantitative anchor for benefit from LDL lowering.

### 6.2 PCSK9 Inhibitors: Lowering LDL to New Levels

PCSK9 (proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9) is secreted by the liver and binds to LDL receptors, promoting their degradation. Inhibiting PCSK9 increases LDLR abundance on the liver surface and lowers LDL-C to very low levels.

- **FOURIER** (2017, *NEJM*, Sabatine et al.): Among 27,564 patients with established ASCVD, evolocumab added to statin therapy lowered LDL-C by a further 59% (median to 30 mg/dL, about 0.78 mmol/L). The primary composite endpoint (MI, stroke, coronary revascularization, or hospitalization for MI/stroke) fell by 15% (HR 0.85, P<0.001); no significant safety signal appeared during three years of follow-up.
- **ODYSSEY OUTCOMES** (2018, *NEJM*, Schwartz et al.): Among 18,924 patients with recent acute coronary syndrome, alirocumab reduced events by a further 15% and lowered LDL to approximately 53 mg/dL.
- **IMPROVE-IT** (2015, *NEJM*, Cannon et al.): Ezetimibe (inhibits intestinal NPC1L1-mediated absorption) added to statin therapy lowered LDL from 70 to 54 mg/dL in 18,144 ACS patients and reduced cardiovascular events by an additional 6.4% (HR 0.936). This showed that “lower LDL is better” remains true even in the 50–70 mg/dL range.

### 6.3 New Targets: Evinacumab and ApoC-III

- **Evinacumab:** An antibody to ANGPTL3 (angiopoietin-like protein 3), it lowers LDL-C through an LDLR-independent pathway. In **homozygous familial hypercholesterolemia (HoFH)**, where LDL receptors are nearly absent and conventional lipid-lowering therapies are ineffective, intravenous evinacumab 15 mg/kg every four weeks lowered LDL-C by a further approximately 47% (GENESYS trial, *NEJM* 2020), providing a last-resort option for patients with “no LDLR.”
- **ApoC-III inhibition:** ApoC-III inhibits lipoprotein lipase (LPL) and promotes VLDL production. The antisense oligonucleotide volanesorsen lowers triglycerides by approximately 70% in people with hypertriglyceridemia (COMPASS trial, 2019). ANGPTL3 and ApoC-III are emerging “non-LDL pathway” lipid-lowering targets.

### 6.4 Antithrombotic and Anti-inflammatory Secondary Prevention

- **COMPASS** (2017, *NEJM*): Among 27,395 patients with stable atherosclerotic disease, rivaroxaban 2.5 mg twice daily plus aspirin 100 mg reduced major vascular events by 24% versus aspirin alone (HR 0.76), but increased major bleeding.

### 6.5 New Developments in Lipid-Lowering Treatment, 2024–2026

Recent key developments focus on three directions: “lower LDL, stronger clearance, and targeting new lipids”:

- **Oral PCSK9 inhibition is emerging:** Following injectable PCSK9 monoclonal antibodies, oral small-molecule PCSK9 inhibitors (such as enlicitide/MK-0616) have shown an additional 50–60% LDL-C reduction in Phase III trials; oral dosing greatly improves accessibility. The shift of PCSK9 inhibition from injection to oral treatment is an important variable in clinical practice after 2025.
- **Inclisiran (long-acting siRNA):** PCSK9-targeted siRNA given by subcutaneous injection twice per year has been approved. The ORION program shows sustained LDL reductions of about 50%, with an adherence advantage—“two injections a year to lower LDL” changes the landscape of long-term management.
- **Bempedoic acid (ATP citrate lyase inhibitor):** CLEAR Outcomes (2023, *NEJM*) showed a 13% reduction in MACE among people intolerant of statins, without increasing new-onset diabetes risk (in contrast to the diabetes risk associated with statins). It is a new option to replace or supplement statins.
- **Lp(a)-targeted drugs entering hard-endpoint testing:** Pelacarsen (antisense oligonucleotide) and olpasiran (siRNA) both lower Lp(a) by 80–95%. Phase III hard-endpoint trials (Lp(a)-HORIZON, OCEAN(a)-Outcomes) are underway. **If positive, they will provide the first proof that “lowering Lp(a) itself” reduces events**, opening a new era in management of “residual lipid risk.”
- **Phase III results for the ApoA-I mimetic CSL112:** AEGIS-II (2024, *NEJM*) did not meet its primary endpoint (90-day composite events) in patients with acute MI, although a post hoc 180-day analysis showed reduced cardiovascular mortality. The success of the “HDL function enhancement” strategy remains unsettled and needs further validation.

**Trend assessment:** Lipid-lowering is moving from “LDL as the sole target” toward “comprehensive management of multiple lipids” (LDL + Lp(a) + TG + HDL function), while dosing is evolving from daily oral therapy to long-acting injections. From an anti-aging perspective, **lifelong maintenance of a low ApoB-particle burden is one of the most evidence-based medical interventions for cardiovascular aging** (echoing the NO–endothelial-protection framework in [01-no-cardiovascular](01-no-cardiovascular.md)).

## 7. Lp(a): The Overlooked Independent Risk Factor

Lp(a) consists of **an LDL-like particle (containing ApoB-100) covalently linked by a disulfide bond to apolipoprotein(a)**. It has two pathogenic mechanisms: (1) the atherogenicity of an ApoB-containing particle, similar to LDL; and (2) **high homology between apolipoprotein(a) and plasminogen**, allowing it to competitively inhibit plasminogen activation and promote PAI-1 expression, thereby suppressing fibrinolysis and promoting thrombosis. Lp(a) concentration is determined largely by the number of Kringle-IV-2 repeats in the LPA gene (which determines apo(a) particle size) and is barely affected by diet or lifestyle. It is a genuine **lifelong genetic risk**.

**Evidence grade:** Mendelian randomization and large GWAS consistently establish elevated Lp(a) as a **causal** risk factor for coronary artery disease and aortic stenosis. About 20% of the population has Lp(a)>50 mg/dL (approximately 125 nmol/L), associated with a roughly 1.5–2-fold elevation in risk, independent of and additive to LDL. **No Lp(a)-targeted drug is currently approved**—statins do not lower it. Antisense oligonucleotides against apo(a) (pelacarsen, Lp(a)-HORIZON trial) and siRNA (olpasiran, OCEAN(a)-DOSE) in development can lower Lp(a) by 80–95%; hard-endpoint results are awaited. Lp(a) is currently one of the most prominent unmet needs in residual lipid-risk management.

## 8. Connections to NO / NAD⁺ / Longevity Networks

### 8.1 Endothelial Dysfunction Is the Earliest Event in Atherosclerosis

The **first measurable phenotype of atherosclerosis is not lipid deposition, but endothelial dysfunction**—reduced NO bioavailability appears before fatty streaks in the intima. Links in the mechanism: NO deficiency → (1) increased endothelial permeability, allowing more LDL into the intima (NO maintains the VE-cadherin barrier); (2) increased VCAM-1/ICAM-1 adhesion molecules, promoting monocyte rolling, adhesion, and transendothelial migration; (3) dysregulated smooth-muscle proliferation/migration; and (4) accelerated LDL oxidation.

### 8.2 NO’s Anti-Atherosclerotic Mechanisms at Every Node

```text
LDL ──oxidation──→ oxLDL ──scavenger receptors──→ foam cells
   ▲                    │                              ▲
   │                    │                        inhibited by NO
   │                    ▼                              │
NO inhibits LDL     Endothelial activation/             │
oxidation            inflammation                      │
   │                    ▲                              │
   │              NO inhibits NF-κB                    │
   │              (reduces VCAM-1/ICAM-1/E-selectin)   │
   │                    │                              │
   └────flow shear stress → eNOS → NO ────────────────┘
            │
            ├─ High-shear regions (laminar flow): sustained eNOS activation,
            │  “atheroprotected” arterial regions
            └─ Low-shear regions (branch points): inadequate eNOS activation,
               predilection sites for lesions
```

**Quantification and grading:** (1) NO inhibits LDL oxidation (directly scavenges lipid-peroxidation radicals)—Grade B (mechanistic studies); (2) NO inhibits adhesion-molecule expression (by suppressing NF-κB nuclear translocation)—Grade A (lesion area is markedly greater in eNOS−/− mice); (3) NO/cGMP/PKG inhibits PDGF-driven smooth-muscle proliferation—Grade B; (4) **blood-flow shear stress is a natural “atheroprotective factor”**: high-shear laminar-flow regions (e.g., straight arterial segments) have sustained eNOS activation and fewer lesions, whereas low-shear regions at branch points/curves have inadequate eNOS activation and more lesions. This spatial distribution is the clearest visual evidence of NO’s anti-atherosclerotic role.

### 8.3 Vascular Aging: The Engine Accelerating Atherosclerosis

Aging accelerates atherosclerosis through several interacting routes, including decline in NO signaling:

| Aging mechanism | Connection to atherosclerosis/lipids |
|---|---|
| Endothelial-cell senescence | eNOS expression↓, NO bioavailability↓ → endothelial-barrier damage, lipid retention↑ |
| Vascular smooth-muscle-cell (VSMC) senescence | Phenotypic switch (contractile → synthetic/inflammatory), SASP secretion, abnormal collagen synthesis in fibrous cap |
| Macrophage senescence | Impaired efferocytosis, pro-inflammatory M1 polarization, inflammatory amplification |
| Oxidative stress (Nox↑, eNOS uncoupling) | oxLDL generation↑, NO quenching↑ |
| Mitochondrial dysfunction | ROS leakage↑, promoting LDL oxidation and endothelial injury |
| NAD⁺ decline | SIRT1 activity↓ → less eNOS deacetylation and more NF-κB activation |

**Important emerging insight: VSMC senescence is both a threat to plaque stability and a potential source of fibrous-cap cells.** Senescent VSMCs secrete MMPs that degrade collagen and thin the fibrous cap (promoting rupture), but they can also transdifferentiate into chondrocyte-/osteoblast-like cells and participate in calcification. SASP (senescence-associated secretory phenotype: IL-6, IL-8, MCP-1, MMPs) bridges cellular senescence and plaque inflammation. Senolytics (dasatinib + quercetin; see Section 04) are being explored as a new strategy for plaque stabilization.

### 8.4 The NAD⁺/SIRT1 Intersection

NAD⁺ levels decline with age → reduced SIRT1 activity → (1) less deacetylation of NF-κB p65, increasing NF-κB activation and inflammation; (2) less eNOS deacetylation, reducing eNOS activity; and (3) impaired regulation of LXR/ABCA1, weakening cholesterol efflux. Thus, **NAD⁺ supplementation (NMN/NR) might indirectly improve endothelial function and cholesterol efflux through SIRT1**, although evidence remains early (Grade B/C).

## 9. Intervention Directions and Actionable Recommendations

### 9.1 Tiered Interventions with Established Evidence

| Tier | Intervention | Target/mechanism | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| First-line | Statins | HMG-CoA reductase inhibition, LDLR upregulation | A (RCT meta-analyses) |
| First-line | Ezetimibe | NPC1L1 inhibition (intestinal cholesterol absorption) | A (IMPROVE-IT) |
| Second-line | PCSK9 inhibitors | Inhibit LDLR degradation | A (FOURIER/ODYSSEY) |
| Second-line | Anti-inflammatory treatment (canakinumab) | IL-1β blockade | A (CANTOS, but infection risk) |
| Lifestyle | Mediterranean diet/nuts | Reduces ApoB particles, improves endothelium | A/B |
| Lifestyle | Aerobic exercise | Shear stress↑ → eNOS↑, HDL function↑ | A (FMD improves 3–4%) |
| Lifestyle | Smoking cessation/alcohol moderation | Restores endothelial function and HDL function | A/B |

### 9.2 Strategic Priorities

1. **The “lower LDL is better” principle:** The evidence chain from 4S→CARE→LIPID→FOURIER→IMPROVE-IT supports progressively lower LDL targets. For very-high-risk patients, LDL<55 mg/dL (about 1.4 mmol/L), and even <1.0 mmol/L, has become a guideline target.
2. **From managing LDL-C to managing ApoB particle number:** In people with high TG, diabetes, or metabolic syndrome, ApoB/non-HDL-C reflects atherogenic particle burden more accurately than LDL-C.
3. **Residual inflammatory versus residual lipid risk:** Patients whose hsCRP and LDL are both low after treatment (LDL<70 mg/dL + hsCRP<2 mg/L) have the lowest event rates, suggesting that both pathways need management.
4. **New therapies:** Lp(a) targeting (pelacarsen/olpasiran), ANGPTL3 (evinacumab), ApoC-III inhibition, and ApoA-I mimetics (CSL112 to enhance RCT) are directions for the next 5–10 years.
5. **Anti-aging perspective:** Senolytics to clear senescent VSMCs/macrophages, NAD⁺ supplementation to restore SIRT1/eNOS, and exercise to maintain shear-stress signaling may become adjunct strategies for plaque stabilization beyond lipid management (all remain in the research stage).

## 10. Open Questions and Controversies

1. **Is HDL completely out of the picture?** Did CETP-inhibitor failure disprove raising HDL itself, or only raising HDL-C concentration? If ApoA-I mimetic CSL112 succeeds in Phase III, attention may shift from “concentration” back to “transport function.”
2. **Personalizing residual inflammatory risk:** How can inflammation be targeted precisely without increasing infection? The infection cost in CANTOS suggests selecting “inflammation-driven” subgroups (high hsCRP and no high-risk infection factors).
3. **How low can LDL go safely?** In FOURIER, median LDL was 30 mg/dL and some participants reached <10 mg/dL, without a significant increase in adverse events over three years. Longer follow-up is needed to verify long-term safety of extremely low LDL, especially for the nervous system and hormone synthesis.
4. **Can plaque regression be made reliably stable?** Intensive lipid lowering reduces plaque volume and lipid-core size, but it is unclear whether it reliably prevents conversion of a “stable plaque” to rupture; plaque erosion may be underestimated.
5. **Hard endpoints for Lp(a) targeting:** Whether Phase III results for pelacarsen (Lp(a)-HORIZON) and olpasiran translate into fewer events is a major future question.
6. **Effects of senescent-cell clearance on plaques:** Senolytics reduce SASP and improve efferocytosis, but their net effect on fibrous-cap thickness (potentially reducing collagen) could stabilize or destabilize plaques; this is a mechanistic controversy with effects in both directions.

### 10.1 An Integrated View of Lipid Metabolism and Aging: From “Lipid Management” to “Vascular Anti-Aging”

The relationship between lipid metabolism and aging extends far beyond “blood lipid levels” and can be integrated at three levels:

- **The lipid–inflammation–aging triangle:** LDL retention and oxidation (oxLDL) → macrophage foam-cell formation and senescence → SASP inflammation → more LDL oxidation and endothelial injury. This positive-feedback loop—“lipids drive inflammation, inflammation drives aging, aging amplifies lipid damage”—is the structural basis for atherosclerosis as a quintessential “disease of aging.” **Breaking the loop requires both lipid management (statins/PCSK9) and anti-inflammatory measures (lifestyle/CANTOS-like strategies)**; a single approach is unlikely to suffice (echoing the “dual-target” principle in Section 9.2).
- **The intersection of lipids and metabolic aging:** Visceral-fat accumulation (lipotoxicity) → insulin resistance → worsening lipid profile (high TG, more small dense LDL) → accelerated atherosclerosis. Lipid metabolism is the “conveyor belt” between metabolic aging in [03-metabolic-aging](03-metabolic-aging.md) and vascular aging. Weight loss—especially loss of visceral fat—has the strongest leverage for improving all three.
- **“Vascular age” as a window into aging:** Atherosclerotic burden (coronary artery calcium score, CAC; carotid IMT) quantifies “vascular age” and is among the strongest imaging markers for predicting all-cause mortality (10-year cardiovascular-event rates are very low with CAC=0). **Vascular-age assessments (CAC score, FMD, PWV) should be included in anti-aging monitoring**, complementing epigenetic clocks in [02-aging-clocks](../01-foundations/02-aging-clocks.md): imaging measures structure, while clocks measure molecular age.

**Core judgment:** Lipid management is the most mature medical tool for “cardiovascular anti-aging”—hard-endpoint evidence for statins/PCSK9 is stronger than for any anti-aging supplement. But moving from “disease prevention” to “anti-aging” requires embedding lipid management in an integrated intervention of the “lipid–inflammation–aging” triangle (exercise, weight loss, NO pathway, senolytics), rather than lowering lipids in isolation (an example in vascular medicine of the combination strategy in [09-immortality/02-breakthrough-candidates](../09-immortality/02-breakthrough-candidates.md)).
