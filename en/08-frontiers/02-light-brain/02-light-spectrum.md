## Core concepts

Spectrum engineering refers to engineering intervention that artificially designs the wavelength composition, intensity (illuminance lux), color temperature and timing (timing) of light based on the photobiological characteristics of different light wavelengths to regulate circadian rhythm, neuroendocrine and metabolic systems**. Its biological basis is the coexistence of "dual light pathways": on the one hand, red light/near infrared (600–1100nm) regulates cellular energy through mitochondrial CCO photodissociation of NO (see 01-photobiomodulation.md for details); on the other hand, visible light (especially blue light 460–480nm) triggers non-visual pathways through intrinsic photosensitive retinal ganglion cells (ipRGCs, containing melanopsin) → Suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) → pineal gland, drives circadian rhythm and melatonin secretion. Spectrum engineering elevates "light" from a passive environmental factor to a programmable anti-aging intervention dimension: **High-illuminance blue light in the morning anchors the rhythm phase, blue light during the day maintains alertness and metabolism, and avoids blue light at night to protect melatonin and NAD+ oscillations**. The connection with aging is that circadian rhythm disorder itself accelerates aging (the CLOCK gene network is decoupled from NAMPT/NAD+ oscillation), and the correct use of light is the material basis of "chrono-hygiene".

## 1. Basics of photobiology: three-level photoreceptor system from photons to genes

### 1.1 Three types of photoreceptors in the eye

The human retina is not a single photoreceptor system, but a collaboration of three types of photoreceptors:

| Photoreceptors | Phytochrome | Peak wavelength | Main functions | Pathways |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rod photoreceptor cells | Rhodopsin | ~498nm | Dark vision, low light intensity | Visual pathway → Visual cortex (imaging) |
| Cones | Opsins (S/M/L cones) | ~420/530/560nm | Photopic vision, color vision | Visual pathway → Visual cortex |
| **ipRGCs** | **Melanopsin** | **~480nm blue light** | **Non-visual: rhythm, pupils, alertness** | **Retinohypothalamic tract (RHT)→SCN** |

**Intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells (ipRGCs)** are the core target of spectrum engineering. This type of cell expresses melanopsin itself and can sense light without the need for rods/cones. It is the only retinal cell that directly connects to the non-visual pathway. Its light absorption peak is about **480nm (blue light area)**, which is "blue light sensitive", which is why **blue light has a much greater impact on circadian rhythm than red/green light of the same illumination**. ipRGCs receive input from rods/cones simultaneously and integrate the output to multiple brain regions.

### 1.2 Light → SCN → non-visual pathway of pineal gland (full chain)

```
Photon (blue light 460-480nm peak sensitive)
│
▼
ipRGCs (melanopsin, retina)
│
▼ Retinohypothalamic Tract (RHT)
│ Via optic nerve → optic chiasm → lateral hypothalamus
▼
Suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN, master circadian pacemaker)
│ Glutamate/NMDA receptor afferent, clock gene Per/Cry transcriptional reset
│ SCN output: autonomic nervous system + hormonal signals
▼
Paraventricular nucleus (PVN) → superior cervical ganglion (SCG, sympathetic preganglionic/postganglionic fibers)
│
▼
Pineal gland
│ α1/β1-adrenergic receptor activation → tryptophan → 5-HT → N-acetyltransferase (AANAT) → melatonin
▼
Nocturnal melatonin secretion (dark signal) → feedback regulation of SCN phase via MT1/MT2 receptors
```

The core of this pathway is the "dark signal" property of melatonin: melatonin is only secreted during the dark period (night), and light (especially blue light) inhibits AANAT activity and blocks melatonin synthesis through the above pathway. Therefore, melatonin is not only a sleep hormone, but also a central signaling molecule that couples "ambient light → circadian rhythm → endocrine".

### 1.3 Clock Gene Network: How to "wind up" light

Circadian rhythms within SCN neurons are driven by a **transcription-translation negative feedback loop (TTFL)**: CLOCK/BMAL1 heterodimer activates Per/Cry transcription → Per/Cry protein accumulation followed by negative feedback inhibits its own transcription, with a cycle of approximately 24 hours. **Light (glutamate via RHT → Ca²⁺/cAMP) acts on this loop in a critical step: activating CREB → inducing Per1/Per2 transcription**, allowing the endogenous cycle (approximately 24.2h, slightly longer than 24h) to be "calibrated" to 24h by daily light. This calibration is **phase resetting**, and its law is described by **Phase Response Curve (PRC)**: **The phase effect of light depends on the moment when the light occurs** - early morning light makes the rhythm advance (phase advance), nighttime (physiological dark period) light makes the rhythm delay (phase delay), and daytime light has the least impact.

## 2. Light and circadian rhythm: melatonin suppression, PRC and phase shift

### 2.1 Spectral sensitivity of melatonin suppression

Melatonin suppression is the most quantitatively studied photobiological effect, and its action spectrum** reveals the ipRGC/melanopsin mechanism:

- **Peak at blue light ~460nm**: Under the same photon number, the efficiency of 460nm blue light in suppressing melatonin at night is about 5–10 times that of 555nm green light (Brainard 2001, *J Neurosci*; Thapan 2001, *J Physiol*, two articles establishing the spectral sensitivity of non-visual pathways);
- The inhibition efficiency at short wavelength (<450nm) and long wavelength (>500nm) decreases significantly;
- **Age Difference**: The lens yellows and transmittance decreases with age. The retina of a 60-year-old receives about 50% less blue light than a 20-year-old. The melatonin suppression response of the elderly to phototherapy is weakened - this is one of the optical mechanisms of the advanced sleep rhythm phase in the elderly (early bedtime and early awakening).

### 2.2 Lighting timing, intensity and phase shift (executable data)

| Illumination Factor | Direction of Effect | Magnitude/Data |
|---|---|---|
| Early morning light (after the onset of the phase) | Phase advance (advance) | 30–60 minutes after rising early 1000–5000 lux can significantly advance DLMO |
| Night light (late phase) | Phase delay (delay) | **Screen blue light 1 hour before bed delays the onset of melatonin by about 2 hours (Chang 2015, *PNAS*)** |
| Light intensity threshold | Melatonin suppression | 50% suppression of melatonin requires ~200 lux blue light or ~800 lux white light; dose-response is S-shaped |
| Illumination duration | Dose accumulation | Brightness × duration (lux·h) accumulation, short-term high brightness and long-term low brightness parts are interchangeable |
| Red light at night | Minimal impact on melatonin | 620–700nm has a much lower melatonin inhibition efficiency than blue light and can be used at night |

**Key corollary**: It is not "light is harmful", but "**light at the wrong time is harmful**". Morning light is beneficial (anchoring phase, maintaining rhythm), while night light is detrimental (delaying rhythm, suppressing melatonin).

### 2.3 Practical significance of PRC

The engineering value of the phase response curve: ** Want to advance the rhythm (adapt to go to bed early and get up early, cross time zones to the east) → bright light in the morning + avoid light in the evening; want to delay the rhythm (shift work, travel west) → evening/light before going to bed**. Morning light therapy uses "morning bright light → phase advance" to correct the common "rhythm phase advance syndrome" (early bedtime and early awakening) in the elderly and the seasonal phase delay of winter depression.

## 3. Detailed explanation of the effects of each band of the visible spectrum

### 3.1 Blue light (460–495nm): double-edged sword—a wake-up hormone during the day and a rhythm disruptor at night

**Daytime Blue Light (Benefit)**:
- Via ipRGC→SCN→locus coeruleus/hypothalamic orexin system, it improves **alertness** and cognitive performance, suppresses melatonin, and maintains the morning peak of cortisol;
- When improving mood (anti-depressant) and reaction, this is the anti-depressant mechanism of "morning bath";
- Regulation of pupil size (pupillary light reflex dominated by ipRGC).

**Blue light (loss) at night**:
- Suppress melatonin and delay rhythm (see 2.2);
- Increase nighttime heart rate and sympathetic tone;
-Screen (LED backlight) blue light exposure is associated with reduced sleep quality (see Section 6).

**Blue light intensity and hazard classification**: Retinal phototoxicity mainly comes from **<450nm short-wave blue-violet light** (which can induce retinal pigment epithelial apoptosis, and long-term high illumination is related to the risk of age-related macular degeneration AMD), while **460–480nm rhythmic blue light** mainly affects the biological clock and has low retinal phototoxicity. The two need to be distinguished: OLED and blue-light filter glasses mainly filter 400-440nm blue-violet light, while the filtration of 480nm rhythmic blue light needs to be considered separately.

### 3.2 Green light (495–570nm): New evidence for analgesia and anxiety modulation

- **Analgesia**: Green light (520–530nm) irradiation shows pain relief in migraine and fibromyalgia models. The mechanism may be related to up-regulating the endogenous opioid/cannabinoid system and regulating the thalamus and cortical pain network (Martin et al., mechanism studies show that green light changes the activity of pain-related brain areas; evidence level: animals + small sample of humans);
- **Melatonin Effect**: Green light inhibits melatonin weaker than blue light but stronger than red light;
- Green light has shallow penetration and is mostly absorbed by hemoglobin. It is **not suitable as a wavelength for PBM deep in tissues** (see the Optical Window section of 01-photobiomodulation.md).

### 3.3 Red light (620–700nm): "Night-friendly" light for mitochondria + melatonin

- **mitochondrial PBM**: 660nm matches the CCO CuA center and is one of the main wavelengths of PBM (see 01-photobiomodulation.md for details);
- **Nighttime Availability**: Red light (especially >630nm) has low melatonin suppression efficiency and is a "rhythm-friendly" choice for night lighting;
- **Limitations of photosensitivity**: Red light induces "dark adaptation protection" of rod dark adaptation, but deep tissue penetration is better than blue-green light;
- Evidence shows that red light illumination at night has less impact on sleep structure and alertness the next day than blue/white light, making it a recommended direction for "night lighting transformation".

### 3.4 Ultraviolet light (UV): The trio of vitamin D, NO release and carcinogenesis

**Ultraviolet light (UV-A 315–400nm / UV-B 280–315nm)** is an underestimated and risky part of spectrum engineering:

**UV-B→Vitamin D**: UV-B irradiates skin 7-dehydrocholesterol→Vitamin D3. Vitamin D not only regulates calcium and phosphorus metabolism, but also participates in immune regulation, muscle function and cognition; vitamin D deficiency is associated with the risk of all-cause death, falls, fractures, and some cancers. **Synthesis Efficiency**: Approximately 10–30 minutes of face/arm exposure (depending on latitude, season, skin color) can synthesize hundreds to thousands of IU.

**UV-A→Skin NO→Prevent blood pressure (researched by the University of Sussex, UK)**:
- Richard Weller's team at the University of Sussex (2014, *J Invest Dermatol*; 2014, *Blood*) proved that: **UVA irradiation of the skin (about 30 minutes) significantly reduces systolic blood pressure (SBP average decrease of about 4.7–5.5 mmHg) and diastolic blood pressure**. The mechanism is that UVA photolysis of nitric oxide nitroso compounds (S-nitrosothiols, nitrite) stored in the skin → releases NO → vasodilation;
- This mechanism positions the skin as a **NO reservoir**: the skin stores large amounts of nitrite/nitrosothiols, which trigger the release of UVA into the circulation, producing systemic blood pressure effects;
- Consistent with epidemiological associations with sun exposure (higher hypertension/cardiovascular mortality at higher latitudes);
- **Key Trade-Off**: Antihypertensive benefits of NO release from UVA vs skin photoaging and carcinogenic risks of UV (risk of squamous cell carcinoma, melanoma increases with cumulative UV exposure). **Whether sunscreen blocks NO release is still controversial** - Some sunscreen formulas have incomplete UVA absorption spectrum, and research conclusions are mixed.

**Dose balance of UV carcinogenesis**: UV is a clear Class I carcinogen (skin cancer), but completely avoiding UV will lose the benefits of vitamin D and NO release. Engineering ideas: **Replace long-term sun exposure** with moderate UV exposure of 10–20 minutes/day (avoiding noon), or use UVA single-band sub-erythema dose for controlled irradiation, taking into account NO benefits and cancer control.

### 3.5 Infrared light (IR): Thermal effect and far-infrared blood vessel regulation

- **Near infrared (NIR-I 760–900nm)**: PBM optical window, mitochondrial mechanism (see 01-photobiomodulation.md for details), mainly non-thermal effects;
- **Mid/far infrared (IR-B 1.4–3µm / IR-C 3µm–1mm)**: Mainly **thermal effect** (such as far-infrared sauna/heating pad), promoting sweating, dilating blood vessels, and increasing blood flow; there is animal and preliminary human evidence that thermotherapy is associated with NO (heat-induced eNOS activation) and cardiovascular improvement;
- The role of infrared in spectrum engineering: near infrared is used for cell energy/deep regulation, and far infrared is used for thermal/vascular effects. The two have different mechanisms and dose spectra, and cannot be mixed.

## 4. Application of phototherapy (panorama of clinical application)

### 4.1 Phototherapy for Seasonal Affective Disorder (SAD)

- **Evidence Level**: Strong (Multiple meta-analyses support that morning light therapy is about 50–70% effective, which is equivalent to or better than some drugs).
- **Parameters**: 2500–10000 lux white light (or 500 lux blue light) for 30–60 minutes in the early morning, the mechanism includes "phase advance + melatonin suppression + serotonin/dopamine regulation".
- Key: **Requires sufficient illumination and correct timing** (within 1 hour after getting up), low light or wrong timing will be ineffective.

### 4.2 Antidepressant and morning light therapy

- Non-seasonal depression: The effectiveness of morning light therapy (5000–10000 lux 30–60 minutes after waking up) as an adjunct or alone is supported by meta-analyses, especially in patients with sleep disorder/late-morning depression;
- Mechanism: phase correction + melatonin suppression + hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis regulation + prefrontal/amygdala network neural regulation;
- Combination therapy: Combining phototherapy with SSRIs may enhance efficacy (some studies show the combination is better than either alone).

### 4.3 Blue light blocking glasses and light protection at night

- **Wearing amber/orange filters at night (filtering below 480nm)**: Research shows that it can partially retain melatonin at night and reduce sleep delays caused by electronic screens; **The effect varies from person to person**, and the benefits are more obvious for heavy screen users;
- Limitations: Blue-light filtering glasses are not beneficial to people with insufficient daytime exposure (they cannot replace morning light); and most "anti-blue light glasses" only filter 400–440nm blue-violet light, and are insufficient to filter rhythm-related 480nm blue light;
- A more fundamental solution: **Schedule level** - Reduce screens, lower screen brightness, switch to night mode, and use warm (red/amber) lighting 1–2 hours before bed.

### 4.4 Phototherapy and circadian rhythm disorders: shift work and jet lag

- **Shift work**: Listed by WHO as a "possible carcinogen" (2A) due to circadian rhythm disruption that disrupts immunity, metabolism, DNA repair and melatonin secretion. Intervention: **Bright light anchoring the target phase before/mid-shift + complete light blocking (eye mask/blackout curtain) while sleeping + pharmaceutical melatonin assistance (0.5–3mg, to be taken in a specific phase window)**;
- **Jet lag**: Travel eastward (delay rhythm adaptation) → bright light in the morning + avoid light in the evening; travel west → early adaptation to bright light in the evening/before bedtime; take melatonin according to bedtime in the target time zone;
- Evidence: A combined light + melatonin regimen is superior to a single intervention in a cross-time zone adapted RCT.

### 4.5 Transcranial PBM (tPBM) and cognition

Spectrum engineering applications beyond the non-visual pathway: near-infrared transcranial irradiation (see 01-photobiomodulation.md for details) increases cerebral blood flow/oxygenation, promotes BDNF, and has exploratory positive results for cognitive decline and depression in the elderly (Barrett 2013 double-blind RCT, etc.), but is limited by light attenuation (>99% cannot reach the hypothalamus) (see 04-fus.md for details).

## 5. Artificial Light and Health: Metabolism and Cancer Risk of Artificial Light at Night (ALAN)

### 5.1 ALAN and metabolism

- **Light at night (LAN) and obesity/diabetes**: Epidemiology and prospective cohorts show that bedroom nighttime lighting (including nightlights and curtain light) is positively related to increased BMI, metabolic syndrome, and diabetes risk;
- **Mechanism**: Nighttime light → melatonin suppression → decreased insulin sensitivity + appetite/feeding rhythm disorder (melatonin is involved in regulating insulin secretion and blood sugar rhythm); circadian rhythm gene (such as CLOCK) variation superimposes light exposure to amplify metabolic risks;
- Animal experiments: Continuous light exposure at night in mice induces weight gain and impaired glucose tolerance (obvious compared to the dark cycle), supporting causality.

### 5.2 ALAN and cancer risk

- **IARC Category 2A**: Classified as a possible carcinogen during shift work (including nighttime light exposure);
- **Melatonin Hypothesis**: Night light → decrease in melatonin → weakening of the anti-proliferative/antioxidative/anti-inflammatory effects of melatonin → increased risk of tumors;
- Breast Cancer Cohort Study: High nighttime light exposure was associated with a slightly increased risk of breast cancer (OR approximately 1.2–1.5, depending on cohort); similar for prostate cancer;
- **Evidence Grading**: Strong association/reasonable mechanism, causality needs to be strengthened (many confounding factors make it difficult to conduct human RCT).

### 5.3 LED blue light exposure and screen

- **LED backlight screen** (mobile phone/computer) has a high proportion of blue light and a cold color temperature (6500K+), and is the most common blue light source at night;
- Screen use before bed is associated with increased sleep onset latency, shorter sleep duration, and decreased alertness the next day (particularly significant in adolescent studies);
- **Color Temperature Engineering**: Night mode (low color temperature 2700–3000K) + reduced brightness significantly reduces rhythm disruption (although some studies suggest intensity rather than color temperature is more critical).

## 6. Light environment engineering: home lighting design, color temperature, dynamic lighting and light dose

### 6.1 Light dosimetry: illuminance × duration = light dose

- **Illumination (lux)**: The luminous flux density of the light source acting on the target surface. Artificial lighting is usually 100–500 lux indoors, 10,000–100,000 lux outdoors on a sunny day, and 1,000–5,000 lux on a cloudy day;
- **Light dose** ≈ illumination (lux) × time (hour), that is, lux·h. The effective dose of morning light therapy is about tens of thousands lux·h (such as 5000 lux × 2h = 10,000 lux·h);
- **Rhythm Effective Threshold**: The blue/white light threshold that suppresses melatonin is about 100–200 lux (pupil, age, spectral composition affect individual differences);
- **Project Points**: At home, it is necessary to distinguish between "functional illumination for reading and writing" (above 500 lux) and "rhythm-friendly illumination" (50-100 lux warm light at night is sufficient, avoid high-bright cold light).

### 6.2 Color temperature engineering (dynamic adjustment of color temperature)

| Time period | Recommended color temperature | Recommended illumination | Biological target |
|---|---|---|---|
| Morning (0–2h after waking up) | Cool white 5000–6500K | 1000–5000 lux | Phase advance, suppress melatonin, anchor wakefulness |
| Daytime | Neutral 4000–5000K | 500–1000 lux | Maintain alertness, support work |
| Evening (2 hours before bed) | Warm yellow 2700–3000K | <200 lux | Reduce blue light and allow melatonin to gradually rise |
| 1h before bed | Red/Amber <2500K | <50 lux | Maximize protection of melatonin |

**Dynamic lighting / human-centric lighting**: A lighting system (smart lighting) that automatically switches color temperature and illumination on a time axis to simulate natural daylight rhythms. Evidence: Improvements in sleep and mood have been reported in office/ward/retirement facility populations, but effect sizes are moderate and studies are heterogeneous.

### 6.3 Suggestions for implementation at home (executable)

1. **Morning light priority**: Go outside or by the window for 10-30 minutes within 30 minutes after getting up (still effective on cloudy days), which is closer to the "rhythm gold standard" than any lamp;
2. **Blue color reduction at night**: 1–2 hours before going to bed, switch the lamp to warm color + reduce the brightness + turn on the screen in night mode;
3. **Make the bedroom completely dark**: blackout curtains, blackout eye masks, remove charger LED and other small light sources;
4. **Special Tips for the Elderly**: Yellowing of the lens requires higher illumination or longer exposure to achieve the same rhythmic effect, and the dose of morning light therapy should be adjusted accordingly;
5. **Step by step**: Sudden changes in the lighting scheme may cause temporary discomfort. It is recommended to adjust it day by day.

## 7. Contact NO / NAD+ / longevity network

### 7.1 UVA→Skin NO→Blood Pressure↓: Light-blood pressure axis

- **R. Weller Group, University of Sussex (2014)**: UVA irradiation of forearm/back skin in healthy adults (approximately 30 minutes, 1–1.5 times minimum erythema volume) → mean decrease in systolic blood pressure of approximately **4.7–5.5 mmHg**, with concurrent decrease in diastolic blood pressure lasting several hours; increase in circulating NO-related markers (nitrite);
- Mechanism: UVA photolysis of skin surface nitrite (NO₂⁻) and S-nitrosothiols (RSNO) → releases NO → sGC/cGMP/PKG → vasodilation (homologous to the NO pathway of 01-photobiomodulation.md);
- **Epidemiological support**: High blood pressure and cardiovascular mortality are higher in high latitude (low UV) areas; UK Biobank and other data show that moderate sun exposure is associated with a reduced risk of cardiovascular events;
- This "light-skin-blood pressure" axis is directly connected to the classic NO longevity pathway (see 01-foundations/03-no-basics.md for details), which is a rare **non-visual pathway** health benefit in spectrum engineering.

### 7.2 Red light/NIR and NO release

- PBM red light/near infrared light dissociates NO, promotes eNOS, and suppresses iNOS via CCO (see 01-photobiomodulation.md for details), which is the regulation of NO at the mitochondrial level;
- Complementary to UVA skin NO release: UVA acts on skin NO pools (exogenous) and NIR acts on cellular mitochondrial CCO-bound NO (endogenous). The two have different mechanisms and target tissues, forming a dual channel of "light-NO".

### 7.3 Circadian rhythms and NAMPT/NAD+ oscillations

- **NAMPT (nicotinamide phosphoribosyltransferase)** is the rate-limiting enzyme of the NAD+ salvage synthesis pathway. Its expression is directly regulated by the CLOCK/BMAL1 clock gene. **NAD+ concentration exhibits diurnal oscillation** (high in the day and low in the night in tissues such as the liver);
- Light disturbance (shift work, nighttime light exposure) → CLOCK/BMAL1 rhythm decoupling → **NAMPT/NAD+ oscillation flattening** → SIRT1/SIRT3 activity decrease → Mitochondria are decoupled from the metabolic clock → accelerated aging (crossed with "mitochondrial dysfunction" and "intercellular communication alterations" in López-Otín 2023's twelve hallmarks of aging);
- **Positive feedback candidate for light rhythm → SIRT1**: Morning light-anchored rhythm → Maintains NAMPT/NAD+ oscillations → SIRT1 cycle activation → Promotes clock genes (SIRT1 deacetylates BMAL1/Per2) → Strengthens rhythm. **Maintaining normal light rhythm = a prerequisite for maintaining NAD+/SIRT1 longevity network**;
- Relationship with the supplement of NAD+ precursors (NMN/NR): **NMN increases the total amount of NAD+, while the light rhythm maintains the rhythm of NAD+**—the two complement each other positively; those with rhythm disorders who simply supplement NMN and ignore the light rhythm may not be able to fill the "oscillation base".

### 7.4 Light and endothelial function, cardiovascular clock

- There is a peripheral clock in the vascular endothelium, which is regulated by the main clock and hemodynamic/sympathetic signals;
- Morning peak cardiovascular events (rising blood pressure in the morning, high incidence of myocardial infarction and stroke) are partly due to the coupling of endothelial clock and sympathetic activation; morning light synchronization can regulate this rhythm and maintain vascular health.

## 8. Intervention directions and executable suggestions

1. **Morning light therapy routine**: 10–30 minutes (1000–5000 lux) outdoors/by the window within 30 minutes after getting up, which is better than any artificial light and is the "first priority" of rhythmic hygiene;
2. **Light-NO dual channel**: Moderate sun exposure during the day (10–20 minutes, avoid strong UV at noon) + near-infrared/red light PBM at night (warm light for relaxation before bed). The former activates the skin NO store to reduce blood pressure, and the latter supports mitochondrial energy - forming a "light-blood pressure-energy" dual channel; UV dosage needs to be controlled and exposure to the sun must be avoided;
3. **Dynamic lighting transformation at home**: smart lights automatically switch according to the 6.2 color temperature table; the bedroom is completely dark + night mode;
4. **Shift workers/Jet lag**: Combination of light + melatonin (see 4.4), the timing of light is more important than the illumination;
5. **Dose increase for the elderly**: Due to yellowing of the lens, the illumination/duration of morning light therapy needs to be increased by 50–100%;
6. **Monitoring**: Wearable devices track sleep-wake and light exposure; DLMO measurement (salivary melatonin) assesses rhythm phase;
7. **Synergy with NAD+ supplement**: Maintain light rhythm + NMN/NR supplement, two-pronged approach (see 7.3 for details).

## 9. Open issues and disputes

- **Real magnitude of blue light hazard**: Retinal phototoxicity mainly targets short-wave blue-violet light and requires extremely high illumination/long term; there is insufficient evidence of direct damage to the retina from daily screen blue light, but sufficient evidence of rhythm interference (melatonin suppression). It is necessary to distinguish two different endpoints: "retinal damage" and "rhythm disruption".
- **Does sunscreen block UVA-NO and reduce blood pressure**: The evidence is conflicting, and the UVA blocking spectrum of different sunscreen formulas varies greatly; it is recommended that the dosage of "sunscreen vs. sun exposure" be individualized.
- **Effectiveness of blue light filtering glasses**: Research results are inconsistent. Insufficient filtration of the rhythmic 480nm is a common design flaw; heavy screen users or those who have problems sleeping late may benefit, but the evidence for the general population is weak.
- **Is red light absolutely safe at night?**: Although red light does not suppress melatonin, excessive brightness can still affect sleep structure and pupils through rod/cone input; "total darkness at night" is still optimal, and red light is just a compromise.
- **Individualized dose of SAD phototherapy**: There is a lack of standards for the optimal illumination/duration/spectrum combination; there are large differences in seasonality, latitude, and individual sensitivity.
- **Long-term maintenance and relapse of phototherapy as an antidepressant**: The effect during the treatment period is clear, but there is insufficient evidence on the recurrence rate and the best maintenance plan after stopping the drug/light.
- **Cost-Benefit of Dynamic Lighting**: There is a lack of large-scale forward-looking data on whether the long-term benefits of smart lighting systems to the general healthy population outweigh the costs.
