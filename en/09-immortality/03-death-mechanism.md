## B. Background: A Typology of Death—Distinguish Three Kinds of “Death” Before Discussing “Death from Aging”

### 1. Three Kinds of Death: Accidental, Programmed, and Aging-Related

Before discussing “immortality,” we must recognize that death is not a single uniform event, but at least three forms of termination with very different mechanisms:

| Type | Driving factors | Relationship to age | Can it be avoided by an “immortality drug”? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Accidental death** | Trauma, infection, predation, accidents | Basically unrelated to age | No. Biological immortality ≠ invulnerability (see [01-immortality-framework](01-immortality-framework.md)) |
| **Programmed death** | Active cell death during development and homeostasis (apoptosis, pyroptosis) | Partly age-related | Partly: programmed cell death is itself a repair mechanism at the cellular level; the problem is collateral damage |
| **Death from aging** | Cascade of multisystem functional failure | Strongly associated with age (exponential rise) | This is the only kind an “immortality drug” truly aims to overcome |

The statistical mathematics of death from aging is expressed by the mortality law proposed by British actuary Benjamin Gompertz in 1825: **after adulthood, the risk of death per unit time rises exponentially with age**, or ln(mortality) ≈ α + β·age. β is the “rate of aging”—it means that mortality risk doubles about every eight years (in humans, β corresponds to a mortality-doubling time of about eight years). In 1860, William Makeham added an age-independent component (accidents and other external causes), producing the Gompertz–Makeham model. The law’s great contribution is that it makes “aging” quantifiable as a **measurable slope**. The essence of an immortality drug is not to reduce mortality to zero, but to **flatten the β slope** (see the definition “biological immortality = mortality does not increase with age” in [01-immortality-framework](01-immortality-framework.md)).

### 2. The Common Endpoint of Death from Aging: Multimorbidity and Organ Reserve

After modern medicine eliminated most acute causes of death, the character of death from aging changed fundamentally. WHO global cause-of-death statistics (2021) show that cardiovascular disease (about 32%), malignant tumors (about 13%), and chronic respiratory disease and diabetes together account for over 60% of deaths. In China, cardiovascular disease, malignant tumors, and chronic respiratory disease together account for over 80% of deaths among residents. More important, however, is a structural fact: **older adults rarely die from a single disease; they die with multimorbidity**. Atherosclerosis, cancer, Alzheimer’s disease, chronic kidney disease, and sarcopenia may progress simultaneously in the same 85-year-old, with the cascade of failure ultimately triggered by a “final precipitant” (one bout of pneumonia, one heart-failure episode, one fall).

This yields the first central judgment of this article: **death from aging is the cascade endpoint after “systemic redundancy is exhausted,” not a single failure in a single organ**. Organ reserve declines with age—by age 70, the reserve of most organs may be only 30–50% of that in youth. When multiple organs cross critical thresholds at once, any minor perturbation can trigger an irreversible cascade. Understanding this explains why “curing every disease of old age” is not equivalent to “curing aging,” and why an immortality drug must act upstream in the cascade.

## R. State of Research and Key Evidence

### 3. Evolutionary Theories: Why Natural Selection Does Not Maintain the Later Years

The first serious answer to “Why do we age?” came from evolutionary biology. Four theories form the central narrative, in chronological order:

1. **Weismann 1889 (programmed aging):** August Weismann proposed that aging was an active program selected “to make room for descendants”—individuals would age and die after reproduction, avoiding competition for resources with their offspring. This theory is now largely rejected: **aging individuals have almost always completed reproduction, and whether they die does not directly determine the number of their offspring**. Natural selection cannot provide direct selective pressure for “altruistic suicide.” Nevertheless, it opened the century-long question of whether aging is programmed.
2. **Medawar 1952 (mutation-accumulation theory):** Peter Medawar pointed out that the power of natural selection **declines exponentially with age**. Individuals may die at any time from accidents, so the probability of reaching old age is already very low; mutations expressed only late in life are therefore subject to little selection. Thus, **mutations harmful in later life accumulate in the gene pool**; aging is an inevitable by-product of the age-related decline in selective pressure.
3. **Williams 1957 (antagonistic-pleiotropy theory):** George Williams further argued that **if the same gene is beneficial in youth but harmful later in life, natural selection will retain it without hesitation** (“antagonistic pleiotropy”). Classic examples: p53 is a powerful tumor suppressor in youth, but later drives stem-cell exhaustion and cellular senescence; the mTOR pathway promotes growth and reproduction but later accelerates metabolic dysfunction; testosterone benefits men during their reproductive years but is associated with prostate cancer later in life.
4. **Kirkwood 1977 (disposable-soma theory):** Thomas Kirkwood unified the first two ideas through “resource allocation”: organisms optimally divide limited energy between **reproduction** and **somatic maintenance**. Since reproduction is the sole goal of evolution, maintenance investment is set at a level “just enough to last through the reproductive period.” **After reproduction, the maintenance debt comes due; damage accumulates, and aging becomes apparent.** This explains three observations: ① lifespan varies enormously across species because external mortality differs, changing the optimal maintenance investment; ② caloric restriction can extend lifespan because energy scarcity shifts investment from reproduction to maintenance; ③ aging is not “designed” but “allowed to happen.”

Together, these four papers established the modern consensus in aging biology: **aging is a by-product of evolution, not an evolutionary design**. Strong support comes from interspecies comparisons: species with high external mortality (such as mice, with many predators) have short lifespans, whereas species with extremely low external mortality (such as naked mole-rats and hydra) have evolved extremes close to “negligible senescence.” The naked mole-rat (*Heterocephalus glaber*) lives about 30 years, 5–10 times longer than rodents of similar size, and its mortality curve barely rises in adulthood (Buffenstein 2008). Under laboratory conditions, neither mortality nor fertility in hydra changes with age (Schaible 2015, *PNAS*). **Natural selection does not maintain old age—but when the risk of “accidental death” is reduced to a very low level, the return on maintenance investment rises, and longevity can be selected for.** This offers the first optimistic anchor for an “immortality drug”: long life is evolutionarily achievable, not prohibited by the laws of physics.

### 4. Cellular Mechanisms of Death: The Hayflick Limit, Telomeres, and Nondividing Cells

Evolutionary theory answers “Why do we age?” Cell biology answers “How does aging occur inside cells?” In 1961, Hayflick & Moorhead (*Exp Cell Res*) discovered that human embryonic fibroblasts in culture could divide only about **50±10 times** before entering irreversible growth arrest—the “Hayflick limit.” This brought aging, for the first time, from a macroscopic phenomenon to the cell-culture bench.

The **telomere hypothesis (Olovnikov 1973)** provided a molecular explanation: linear chromosome replication has an “end-replication problem.” DNA polymerase cannot copy the final segment at the end (after the RNA primer is removed, there is no template to fill the gap), resulting in the loss of 50–200 bp of telomeric sequence (TTAGGG repeats) with each division. When telomeres reach a critical length (about 4 kb), the shelterin complex (TRF2/POT1) can no longer mask the chromosome ends. The DNA-damage response system (ATM/ATR) mistakes them for breaks and activates the p53/p21 or p16 pathway, driving the cell into **replicative senescence**. Telomerase (TERT+TERC) is largely silenced in somatic cells, while 85–90% of cancer cells reactivate it. Telomerase is both the “immortality key” for cancer cells and the “division counter” for normal cells (see the complete mechanism in [01-foundations/05-telomeres.md](../01-foundations/05-telomeres.md)).

But the Hayflick limit is only half the story, and perhaps not even the more important half. **Most cells in the human body do not divide, or divide very rarely:** neurons are essentially established before birth; cardiomyocytes renew at only about 1% per year (falling to about 0.45% after age 25, Bergmann 2009, *Science*); skeletal muscle and liver cells renew slowly. These **nondividing cells do not undergo replicative senescence**, yet they still age. They reach functional failure through three other pathways:

- **Collapse of proteostasis:** accumulation of misfolded proteins (Aβ, α-synuclein, TDP-43) as autophagy and the ubiquitin–proteasome system become less efficient with age (see [01-foundations/06-proteostasis-autophagy.md](../01-foundations/06-proteostasis-autophagy.md));
- **Accumulation of mitochondrial damage:** mtDNA mutations, declining efficiency of the electron-transport chain, and ROS leakage (see [08-frontiers/05-mitochondria/01-mitochondrial-aging.md](../08-frontiers/05-mitochondria/01-mitochondrial-aging.md));
- **Epigenetic drift and lipofuscin deposition:** gene-expression programs diverge from the youthful state, while metabolic waste (lipofuscin) irreversibly accumulates in neurons and cardiomyocytes.

Conclusion: **cellular aging operates through two tracks: a “replication counter” (telomeres) and “damage accumulation” (proteins/mitochondria/epigenetics).** Merely enabling cells to “divide indefinitely” (by activating telomerase) cannot slow the aging of nondividing cells. This is the theoretical ceiling for telomere therapy as an immortality drug.

### 5. Systemic Mechanisms of Death: The Positive-Feedback Chain of the 12 Hallmarks and Loss of Epigenetic Information

Putting the cellular pieces together gives the framework of the **12 hallmarks of aging**, proposed by López-Otín’s team in 2013 and expanded in 2023 (*Cell*): primary damage (genomic instability, telomere attrition, epigenetic alterations, loss of proteostasis) → antagonistic responses (deregulated nutrient sensing, mitochondrial dysfunction, cellular senescence) → integrative consequences (stem-cell exhaustion, altered intercellular communication, macrophage-polarization imbalance, extracellular-matrix stiffening, chronic inflammation). The full chain is presented in [01-foundations/01-aging-hallmarks.md](../01-foundations/01-aging-hallmarks.md).

The 12 hallmarks are not a parallel checklist, but a **positive-feedback network in which the components cause one another**. A cycle along this knowledge base’s central pathways can be drawn as follows:

```
DNA/mitochondrial damage → cellular senescence (SASP secretion↑)
     ↑                                            ↓
epigenetic drift ←── SIRT1/NAD+ decline ←── chronic inflammation (inflammaging)
     ↑                                            ↓
accumulation of CD38+ immune cells ←── SASP recruits more inflammatory cells
```

CD38 is a key node: senescent and inflammatory cells express high levels of CD38. It is both an NAD+ hydrolase and an inflammation amplifier—CD38 depletes the NAD+ pool, causing SIRT1 activity to fall and coupling between epigenetics and metabolism to collapse (NAD+/SIRT1 network: [01-foundations/04-nad-sirtuin.md](../01-foundations/04-nad-sirtuin.md); inflammaging: [03-immunity/02-inflammaging.md](../03-immunity/02-inflammaging.md)). This positive-feedback chain means that **aging accelerates itself once it has begun; intervention is more effective the earlier it starts, and the network’s buffering capacity dilutes any single-point intervention**. This is the mechanistic basis for the conclusion in [02-breakthrough-candidates](02-breakthrough-candidates.md) that “one drug cannot be an immortality drug.”

**The most important theoretical upgrade of 2023–2024: the loss-of-epigenetic-information theory.** In 2023, Sinclair’s team published a review on the information theory of aging in *Nature Aging* (Lu, Tian & Sinclair, *Nat Aging* 2023;3:1486). Its core claim is that **the main driver of aging is not random damage to the DNA sequence (“hardware wear”), but deterministic loss of epigenetic information (DNA methylation, histone modifications, chromatin organization—“software failure”)**. The youthful “instruction manual” remains intact in the genome, but can no longer be read. Causal evidence comes from Yang et al.’s 2023 **ICE experiment** in *Cell*: using an inducible DNA double-strand-break system to locally rearrange the epigenome (disturbing epigenetic information without introducing sequence mutations), the researchers induced premature-aging phenotypes across multiple systems in mice. Partial reprogramming with the Yamanaka factors OSK restored epigenetic information and reversed these phenotypes. This elevated “reprogramming = reversal of aging” from a hypothesis to a program supported by genetic causal evidence (see [08-frontiers/01-reprogramming/01-concept.md](../08-frontiers/01-reprogramming/01-concept.md)).

Critics’ views must also be recorded honestly: in 2024, Timmons & Brenner argued in *Cell* that the information theory “has not been rigorously tested.” Double-strand breaks in the ICE system may themselves trigger damage responses unrelated to epigenetics, and “information loss” and “damage accumulation” are difficult to separate experimentally; Yang et al. subsequently responded. The current state of the debate is: **the loss-of-information theory is currently the framework best able to unify “damage” and “program” theories, but remains a strong hypothesis, not a settled conclusion.** System-level evidence also suggests that aging has a “central controller”: Zhang 2013 (*Nature*) showed that hypothalamic NF-κB activation can systemically accelerate aging, while inhibiting it can slow aging (see the hypothalamus–aging axis in [08-frontiers/02-light-brain/03-hypothalamus.md](../08-frontiers/02-light-brain/03-hypothalamus.md)). The heterochronic parabiosis experiments by Conboy 2005 (*Nature*) showed that a youthful blood environment can “restart” stem cells in older organs; breakdown of inter-organ communication is another dimension of aging. **Death from aging is therefore a “whole-network failure”: it involves both bottom-up damage accumulation and top-down failure of central regulation, as well as horizontal breakdown of inter-organ communication.**

### 6. The Human Lifespan Limit: Evidence Behind the Numbers

“How long can humans actually live?” is one of the most contentious empirical battlegrounds in research on mechanisms of death. Three bodies of data underpin the “lifespan wall” position:

- **Dong, Milholland & Vijg 2016 (*Nature* 538:257):** An analysis of the International Database on Longevity (IDL) mortality records since 1900 found that **the maximum age at death plateaued at about 115 years after 1980**, with model estimates of a human maximum lifespan around 125 years. Their conclusion: average lifespan (life expectancy) may continue to increase, but **maximum lifespan has reached a ceiling**.
- **Extreme-value analysis of Dutch mortality data (Einmahl et al. 2019):** Modeling 75,000 deaths among people over age 90 using extreme-value theory estimated maximum human lifespan at about 114 years (95% CI 113.4–114.8), supporting Dong 2016.
- **Olshansky et al. 2024 (*Nature Aging*, “Implausibility of radical life extension in humans in the twenty-first century”):** An analysis of mortality from 1990–2019 in 10 developed countries/regions found that life-expectancy gains had **slowed substantially** over nearly 30 years. For people born after 2010, the probability of reaching age 100 is under 2% for men and about 5% for women. This supports Olshansky’s famous 1990 prediction in *Science* that “human life expectancy will encounter biological limits.”

But the “lifespan wall” is not unchallenged; counterevidence is also real:

- **Gavrilova & Gavrilov (2020, *J Gerontol A*):** They noted severe selection biases in supercentenarian data (missing birth records, age exaggeration) and, using more reliable data from Italy and France, found that **mortality at the highest ages may plateau**—that is, Gompertz’s law may fail after age 106. If this mortality plateau is real, the idea of a “hard limit” may be a statistical artifact.
- **The longevity record itself:** Jeanne Calment’s 122 years and 164 days (died 1997) remains the only rigorously verified lifespan beyond 120. No one has broken the record in the 25 years since; the closest cases are Kane Tanaka (119, died 2022) and Sarah Knauss (119, died 1999). The fact that the “122-year record has stood unbroken for 25 years” is itself empirical support for the lifespan-wall position.
- **The century-long wager:** In 2000, Olshansky and optimist Steven Austad made a bet about whether a verified living person would reach age 150 by 2150; the principal and compounded interest amount to about $500 million. A 2025 *Nature* aging special issue reviewed the bet: 25 years of data favor Olshansky (slowing life-expectancy gains and no breach of the maximum-lifespan plateau), but both sides agree that “aging can be intervened upon”; they differ only on whether a hard ceiling can be broken.

**China’s longevity data offer a distinctive perspective:** The Seventh National Population Census (2020) counted about 119,000 centenarians nationwide. Chengmai in Hainan, Bama in Guangxi, and Rugao in Jiangsu are three well-known longevity regions, with centenarian density far above the national average. Research using the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS, covering tens of thousands of adults aged 85+ across 23 provinces) finds that centenarians commonly show “compression of morbidity” (a very short period of disability) and enrichment of longevity-associated genes such as FOXO3A (validated across multiple cohorts by Willcox 2008, *PNAS*). These data suggest that **there is still room to increase “average” lifespan, while whether the “maximum” dimension is truly locked at 115–125 years remains an open question in 2026**—because all “lifespan wall” statistics come from the era of disease treatment, and anti-aging medicine (treating aging itself) has not yet been incorporated into any lifespan statistics.

## I. Mechanistic Insights: A List of Immortality-Drug Targets from the Mechanisms of Death

### 7. Intervention Implications of Two Philosophies of Aging

Compress the mechanisms in Sections 3–5 into a map of targets: **which immortality drug you think is needed depends on which theory of the “mechanism of death” you hold.**

| Theory of aging | Core claim | Corresponding immortality-drug targets | Existing evidence | Main risks |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Damage-accumulation theory** (evolutionary theory + free-radical/DNA-damage tradition) | Aging = random damage exceeds repair capacity | Senolytics (clear senescent cells), enhanced DNA repair, mitophagy, proteostasis drugs (rapamycin/spermidine) | Strong animal evidence: clearing p16+ cells extends mouse lifespan (Baker 2016, *Nature*; Xu 2018, *Nat Med*); ITP rapamycin extends lifespan by 9–14% | Treats downstream, not upstream causes; clearance interventions risk tissue loss |
| **Loss-of-epigenetic-information theory** (Sinclair) | Aging = loss of the ability to read the instruction manual | Epigenetic reprogramming (partial OSK reprogramming, chemical reprogramming), regulation of epigenetic enzymes (TET/DNMT/HDAC) | Strong causal experiments: ICE experiment (Yang 2023, *Cell*) + reversal with OSK (Lu 2020, *Nature*; Browder 2022, *Nat Aging*); human Phase I initiated (ER-100, 2026) | Full reprogramming causes cancer; difficult to control the “identity-loss threshold” in partial reprogramming |
| **Program theory** (contemporary variants) | Aging = actively driven by master switches (hypothalamus/nutrient sensing) | Central regulation: mTOR/AMPK nutrient sensing, hypothalamic–neuroendocrine axis, SIRT1 network | Moderate: rapamycin/metformin extend lifespan across species (see [04-drugs/06-mtor-rapamycin.md](../04-drugs/06-mtor-rapamycin.md)); hypothalamic interventions work in animals (Zhang 2013, *Nature*) | The existence of a “master switch” is unproven; potent central inhibition has major side effects |

### 8. Three Core Insights

**Insight 1: Death from aging is a “cascade endpoint,” not a “single failure”; an immortality drug must interrupt the cascade, not repair its endpoint.** The facts of multimorbidity and organ-reserve failure mean that curing diseases of old age one by one (cardiovascular disease, cancer, dementia) merely shifts the trigger point in the cascade. The marginal returns of disease-specific medicine are declining (Olshansky 2024’s data illustrate this clinically). The proper engineering targets for an immortality drug are **shared upstream mechanisms in the cascade**—cellular senescence, chronic inflammation, NAD+ depletion, and epigenetic drift—which are common to almost all age-related diseases. This also explains why the “multi-target combination” scored highest in [02-breakthrough-candidates](02-breakthrough-candidates.md).

**Insight 2: Death from aging is both a “software failure” and “hardware wear”; an immortality drug must address both tracks.** The loss-of-information and damage-accumulation theories are not mutually exclusive, but describe different layers of the same cascade: DNA/mitochondrial damage is “hardware,” epigenetic drift is “software,” and SASP/inflammation is “error propagation at runtime.” The ICE experiment shows that software failure can independently cause aging, but does not negate an independent contribution from hardware wear (Timmons & Brenner’s 2024 criticism is valid on this point). The target list must therefore include both “repairing hardware” (senolytics, mitophagy) and “reinstalling software” (OSK reprogramming). A **“senolytics + reprogramming” combination**, “clean first, then reboot,” is currently the most logically complete immortality-drug loop, although its combined safety is entirely unknown.

**Insight 3: The lifespan limit is a “statistical phenomenon,” not a “law of physics”—but breaking it requires a paradigm shift, not drug stacking.** The 115-year plateau in Dong 2016 and the slowdown in Olshansky 2024 measure the exhaustion of gains from “disease control.” They do not—and cannot—include data on intervention in aging itself. On the other hand, the mortality-plateau debate raised by Gavrilova reminds us that even the statistical existence of a “hard ceiling” has not been finally decided. The real dividing line is whether **anti-aging interventions can rewrite the mortality curve from a “plateau” into a “slope,” as infection control did in the 20th century**. Animal experiments have already shown that such rewriting is feasible in principle (OSK increased remaining lifespan by 109% in 124-week-old mice, Macip 2024). Whether humans have similar potential is the only important lifespan question of the next 20 years.

## F. Frontiers and Open Questions

### 9. From “Treating Causes of Death” to “Treating Aging Itself”: At the Threshold of a Paradigm Shift

The true frontier in research on mechanisms of death consists of four unresolved questions. Together, they define the future boundaries of immortality drugs:

1. **Is the mortality plateau real or a data artifact?** If mortality among supercentenarians truly plateaus, as Gavrilova suggests, the concept of a “hard lifespan limit” itself must be rewritten. Answering this requires a more reliable registration system for the oldest-old; China’s centenarian database (119,000 people in the Seventh Census) could be the world’s largest natural experimental setting.
2. **Is the brain the last reversible frontier?** Neurons do not divide, and once neural circuits are lost, they are difficult to rebuild (see “tissue-architecture boundaries” in [01-immortality-framework](01-immortality-framework.md)). Even if OSK reprogramming succeeds throughout the body, whether synaptic and circuit loss caused by Alzheimer’s disease can be reversed will determine whether an “immortality drug” can preserve the “self.”
3. **Can chemical reprogramming become a systemic “software reinstall”?** In 2022, Hongkui Deng’s team showed in *Nature* that small-molecule combinations can chemically reprogram adult human cells into pluripotent stem cells (Guan 2022). If “partial chemical reprogramming” can be systemically delivered, the loss-of-information theory could advance from a “local victory using viral vectors” to “scalable whole-body repair”—the highest-scoring direction in [02-breakthrough-candidates](02-breakthrough-candidates.md).
4. **Will anyone surpass 120 years in humans?** This is not a scientific question so much as a question of medical paradigm: the lifespan wall can be pushed back only if anti-aging interventions enter clinical practice and prove that they reduce the rate of multisystem aging, rather than only the risk of a single disease. The wager will not be settled until 2150, but the first human data (the TAME metformin trial, ER-100 reprogramming Phase I, and D+Q senescence-marker trials) will provide intermediate answers within the next 5–10 years.

### 10. Series Summary: A Program for Immortality Drugs from the Mechanisms of Death

The complete chain of mechanisms of death (evolutionary neglect → two-track cellular mechanisms → systemic cascade → information loss) converges on an actionable answer:

- **Death is not a single event, but the collection of “maintenance debts” coming due**—the first task of an immortality drug is to **reduce that debt** (slow damage accumulation).
- **Death from aging involves failure of both “software and hardware”**—an immortality drug must both **repair hardware** (senolytics, mitophagy, DNA repair) and **reinstall software** (epigenetic reprogramming).
- **The lifespan limit is a statistical phenomenon that can be altered by engineering**—but rewriting it requires a paradigm shift from “treating disease” to “treating aging itself.” Animal evidence for this path is already substantial; human evidence is on its way.

The next article builds on this discussion to examine the “ultimate form of an immortality drug”: if epigenetic reprogramming is software repair and senolytics are hardware cleanup, what are the technological pathways and bottlenecks for engineering them into a scalable combined treatment?
