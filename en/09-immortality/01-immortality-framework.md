# Immortality Drug Series 01: The Scientific Definition of Immortality and a Feasibility Framework

> **Series positioning**: The first article in 09-immortality (the immortality drug series) / a foundational overview.

## B. Background and Problem Definition: First Answer “What Is Immortality?” Before Discussing “Drugs”

### 1. Core Concepts: Four Kinds of “Immortality” and One Key Distinction

The first thing to do before discussing immortality drugs is to strip the word “immortality” of its mythological connotations and break it down into four scientifically definable and testable meanings:

| Concept | Definition | Real-world counterpart | Scientific status |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Biological immortality (negligible senescence)** | After an individual reaches maturity, its mortality rate does not increase with age and its reproductive capacity does not decline with age | Hydra (*Hydra*), naked mole-rat, immortal jellyfish (*Turritopsis dohrnii*) | Truly exists; mechanisms partly elucidated |
| **Cultural/symbolic immortality** | “Existence” continues in the world through works, genes, or memories | Celebrity effect, descendants, digital twins | Not a biological issue; not discussed further here |
| **Lifespan extension** | Maximum/average lifespan increases | Doubling of life expectancy in the 20th century | Achieved; see the debate in §3 |
| **Healthspan extension** | Increase in the number of years lived without disability or major chronic disease | The goal of all 56 articles in this knowledge base | Partly achieved (exercise, blood sugar control, smoking cessation) |

**Key distinction**: Biological immortality ≠ being undying. Biological immortality requires only that “risk of death does not increase with age” (age-independent mortality); an individual can still die from accidents, predation, or infection. Hydra do indeed have an age-independent mortality rate under laboratory conditions, but they would still die if put in a pressure cooker. This distinction is extremely important—it reframes “immortality” from “never dying” to “**aging can be engineered around**,” making it a scientific question with testable hypotheses rather than a theological one.

**What three “immortal” model organisms teach us**:

1. **Hydra (*Hydra vulgaris*)**: Adult interstitial stem cells continuously self-renew, theoretically allowing each individual to reproduce indefinitely. Key molecular evidence: its Wnt signaling is coupled to stem-cell proliferation, while expression of “aging genes” is suppressed—a 2015 transcriptomic study in *Genome Biology* (Wenger et al.) showed that Hydra barely express classical aging programs (for example, high expression of FoxO downstream damage-response programs and low expression of aging-associated genes).
2. **Naked mole-rat (*Heterocephalus glaber*)**: Its lifespan is about 30 years, 5–10 times that of rodents of similar size. Its secret is not “being undying,” but **an extremely low age-related mortality rate** (the slope of its Gompertz mortality curve is close to zero). Candidate mechanisms include high expression of hyaluronan (HMW-HA) → anti-inflammatory effects, epigenetic suppression of p16 expression, and exceptionally high proteome stability (proteome-stability studies show its proteome to be an order of magnitude more stable than that of mice). It demonstrates that mammals, too, can evolve an extreme of “slowing rather than reversing aging.”
3. **Immortal jellyfish (*Turritopsis dohrnii*)**: The only known multicellular animal that can, after sexual maturity, **reverse back to the polyp stage** (transdifferentiation). In 2022, Pascual-Torner et al. (*PNAS*) sequenced its whole genome and compared it with that of the closely related, non-immortal species *Turritopsis rubra*. They found that the immortal species has a **significantly increased number of copies of genes related to replication and repair** (multiple copies of POLD1, PCNA, and others), and elevated expression of genes associated with epigenetic modification and pluripotency (Polycomb, EZH2 homologs). At the molecular level, “immortality” manifests as **redundancy in repair systems + retention of epigenetic plasticity**—two features strikingly analogous to the frontiers of human anti-aging research (enhanced DNA repair and epigenetic reprogramming).

**Implications**: Evolution has repeatedly demonstrated that aging is not an inviolable law of nature, but a **program that natural selection can switch off, or a constraint that can be engineered around**. But it is equally important that “immortality” in these model organisms occurs at the **population level**—no individual immortal jellyfish is truly immortal; the population persists through repeated cycles of “rejuvenation.” Translating “immortality” into an actionable human goal is one of this article’s central propositions: **an immortality drug ≠ making the present body run forever; rather, it means reducing the rate of aging sufficiently and strengthening repair capacity sufficiently so that “accidents,” not “aging,” become the primary cause of death**.

### 2. From “Long Life and Eternal Youth” to “The Modifiability of Aging”: A Paradigm Shift

In Chinese, “long life and eternal youth” implicitly combines two things: living a long time (lifespan) + not growing old (youth). Scientific discourse must separate these two and reorder them:

- **Delay of aging (delay)**: Shift both the starting point and slope of the aging curve to the right—equivalent to making the bodily state at age 60 resemble that at age 45. Most current interventions (exercise, caloric restriction, metformin, rapamycin) basically fall into this category.
- **Reversal of aging (reversal)**: Restore already aged tissues/cells to a younger state. Epigenetic reprogramming (OSK factors) is the only approach for which biological-age reversal has been measured in living animals (see [08-frontiers/01-reprogramming/02-animal-evidence.md](../08-frontiers/01-reprogramming/02-animal-evidence.md)).
- **Compression of morbidity**: Compress the period of disability into the final stage of life. This is an attainable real-world goal and the endpoint of all the documents in 07-interventions in this knowledge base.

**The key argument in this paradigm shift**: There is no “one-and-done immortality drug,” because aging is not a single disease but a **multi-pathway network of 12 hallmarks that are causally interconnected** (see [01-foundations/01-aging-hallmarks.md](../01-foundations/01-aging-hallmarks.md)). Understanding an immortality drug as “one pill” is a category error. The right framework is to understand an immortality drug as **a set of engineering tasks**. SENS (Strategies for Engineered Negligible Senescence), proposed by Aubrey de Grey in 2002, represents this way of thinking: break aging down into seven categories of “damage” (cell loss, mutations, mitochondrial mutations, waste accumulation, and so on), and repair each through engineering rather than trying to understand all mechanisms first and then “cure” aging. The value of this framework lies not in any particular solution, but in turning “immortality” from an untestable slogan into **an engineering checklist that can be decomposed, pursued in parallel, and independently validated**.

### 3. The Scientific Boundaries of an “Immortality Drug”: Three Limitations That Must Be Acknowledged

1. **Thermodynamic boundary**: Any repair mechanism requires energy and information, while the entropic trend of aging (the rate at which macromolecular damage accumulates) may exceed the limits of any known repair system. This is the basis of the argument made by Olshansky’s camp (discussed in §3).
2. **Tissue-architecture boundary**: Neurons cannot regenerate, cardiomyocytes renew extremely slowly, and telomeres are silenced in most somatic cells. Even if the system as a whole is “rejuvenated,” damage to certain structures (such as neural circuits in the brain) may be irreversible.
3. **Emergence boundary**: Even if all cells become young, can inter-organ coupling (the neural-endocrine-immune-vascular axes) still function in coordination? Heterochronic parabiosis experiments (Conboy 2005; regeneration of muscle/liver/nervous tissue recovered after old mice received young blood) suggest that circulating factors can partially “restart” aged organs, but also suggest that once inter-organ communication collapses, local rejuvenation may not rescue the whole. This points directly to the central thread of this knowledge base: **the NO/NAD⁺ network is a hub for inter-organ communication** (see [01-foundations/03-no-basics.md](../01-foundations/03-no-basics.md)).

## R. Research Status and Key Evidence

### 4. Why Do We Age? From “Why Do We Die?” to “Why Do We Grow Old?”

Human understanding of the mechanisms of aging has undergone three theoretical transitions:

**First transition: The cellular limit (1961)**. Hayflick & Moorhead (*Exp Cell Res* 1961;25:585) found that human embryonic fibroblasts could divide only about 50±10 times in culture (the “Hayflick limit”), and that this was correlated with donor age. This was the first time “aging” was brought from a macroscopic phenomenon into the cell-biology laboratory—but it also laid the groundwork for a misconception: many people then believed that aging = exhaustion of cell division. In fact, most human cells (neurons, cardiac muscle cells) barely divide at all; their aging has other causes.

**Second transition: Resource allocation (1977)**. Kirkwood’s **disposable soma theory** proposed that organisms optimally allocate limited resources between “reproduction” and “somatic maintenance.” Natural selection cares only that genes are passed on to the next generation, not how long an individual lives. Thus, repair systems are set at a level “sufficient to last through the reproductive period”; after reproduction, investment in maintenance declines and damage begins to accumulate. This was the first evolutionary framework able to explain “why lifespans differ so greatly among species, and why aging was not selected for but instead permitted.” It directly predicts that **aging is the accumulation of damage caused by inadequate maintenance, not programmed death**.

**Third transition: The modern synthesis of damage versus program (2013–2023)**. The López-Otín team proposed the **nine hallmarks** of aging in *Cell* in 2013 and expanded them to **12 hallmarks** in 2023 (adding imbalanced macrophage polarization, extracellular matrix stiffness, and chronic inflammation), organized into three layers: primary damage (genomic instability, telomere attrition, epigenetic alterations, loss of proteostasis) → antagonistic responses (deregulated nutrient sensing, mitochondrial dysfunction, cellular senescence) → integrative consequences (stem-cell exhaustion, altered intercellular communication, imbalanced macrophage polarization, ECM stiffness, chronic inflammation). [01-foundations/01-aging-hallmarks.md](../01-foundations/01-aging-hallmarks.md) in this knowledge base develops the full chain. Two key points:

- **The hallmarks are not a parallel list but an interlocking network**: mitochondrial ROS → DNA damage → cellular senescence → SASP → chronic inflammation → further suppression of mitochondrial/NAD⁺ metabolism, forming a positive-feedback loop. Any single-point intervention will be “buffered” by the network.
- **Epigenetic alterations have been elevated from a “hallmark” to a “driver”**: In 2024, Sinclair proposed the **information theory of aging**—aging is the gradual loss of epigenetic information (DNA methylation, histone modifications, chromatin organization), not merely an accumulation of DNA-sequence damage; the genome still contains the “young instruction manual,” but it can no longer be read. In a 2023 *Cell* study, his team (Yang et al.) used the ICE system (inducing DNA double-strand breaks to locally reorganize the epigenome without introducing mutations) to show that disrupting epigenetic information alone can drive multisystem aging in mice, while restoring it can reverse aging. This turned “epigenetic reprogramming = reversal of aging” from a hypothesis into a program supported by causal experiments (see [08-frontiers/01-reprogramming/01-concept.md](../08-frontiers/01-reprogramming/01-concept.md) for details).

**The contemporary answer to the debate over programmed versus damage-driven aging**: The two are not mutually exclusive. The contemporary synthesis is: **damage is the raw material, programs are the amplifiers, and information loss is the primary driver**. Aging involves both stochastic damage (mtDNA mutations, protein oxidation) and programmed components (the set point of the mTOR/IGF-1 nutrient-sensing axis, active secretion of SASP), while the deterministic drift of epigenetic information links the two into a directional river.

### 5. The Debate over Lifespan Limits: How Long Can Humans Live?

This is the most hotly contested empirical battleground in the “immortality drug” question, and both camps have real data:

**The statistical camp (lifespan has a limit, and we are close to it)**:
- **Dong, Milholland & Vijg 2016 (*Nature* 538:257)**: Analyzed death records since 1900 in the International Database on Longevity (IDL), finding a **plateau in the maximum age at death at about 115 years** after 1980. Their model estimated the human maximum lifespan at ~125 years. Conclusion: life expectancy (average) may continue to rise, but maximum lifespan has peaked.
- **Analysis of 75,000 Dutch deaths (Einmahl et al., *Extremes* 2019)**: Used extreme value theory (EVT) to model 75,000 deaths among Dutch people aged 90 and older through 2017, estimating a maximum human lifespan of about 114.1 years (95% confidence interval 113.4–114.8), corroborating Dong 2016.
- **Olshansky 2024 (*Nature Aging* 5:87)**: Analyzed mortality data from 10 developed countries/regions from 1990–2019. Life expectancy nearly doubled in the 20th century (the dividend from disease control), but the probability that children born after 2010 would live to age 100 was under 2% for males and about 5% for females; the rate of lifespan extension **slowed significantly** over the past 30 years. His 1990 *Science* prediction that “life expectancy gains will encounter biological limits” has been borne out by 30 years of data.

**The optimistic camp (aging is modifiable; limits can be broken)**: The argument has three layers—① the statistical camp analyzes diminishing returns from “old medicine” (treating disease), while anti-aging medicine (treating aging itself) has not yet been included in any lifespan statistics; ② in animal experiments, aging interventions have repeatedly exceeded “natural lifespan”: senolytic clearance of p16⁺ senescent cells extends median mouse lifespan by about 25–36% (Baker 2011/2016 *Nature*; Xu 2018 *Nat Med*), periodic OSKM expression extends the lifespan of progeroid mice by about 30% (Ocampo 2016 *Cell*), and AAV9-OSK extends the remaining median lifespan of 124-week-old mice by about 109% (Macip 2024); ③ turning back epigenetic clocks demonstrates that “biological age” is reversible (Browder 2022 *Nat Aging*, epigenetic age reversal across multiple tissues in naturally aged mice).

**The $500 million bet (2025)**: In 1990, Olshansky and the optimistic camp’s representative, gerontologist Steven Austad, made a bet: Austad wagered that someone would live to 150 by 2150 (later revised to “the oldest living, verified person reaching age 150”); Olshansky wagered that it was impossible. With principal and compound interest, the stake would be worth about $500 million by 2150, to be collected by the winner’s descendants. A 2025 *Nature Index* in-depth retrospective reported that **after 25 years, the data lean more toward Olshansky**—life-expectancy growth has slowed and the maximum-lifespan plateau has not been broken. But the article also noted that both sides agree that “aging is modifiable”; they differ only on “whether the hard ceiling can be broken.” This marks a shift in the debate from “Is anti-aging useful?” to “How far can anti-aging go?”

**This article’s assessment**: The statistical camp shows that “**under the disease-treatment paradigm**, lifespan is approaching a ceiling”; the optimistic camp’s evidence is currently all at the animal level. Therefore, “Can humans live past 125?” remains an **open question with no human data** in 2026, but there is already substantial human evidence that people can significantly extend healthspan and postpone disability (lifestyle interventions + clinical drugs). **The immortality-drug question should be reframed as: Can the human lifespan curve be changed from a “plateau” into a “slope”? This depends on whether aging interventions can become a public-health-level undertaking, like infectious-disease control in the 20th century.**

## I. Mechanistic Insights and Interpretation: The Immortality Drug = Multi-Target Engineering

### 6. Core Insight One: Why a Single Drug Cannot Become an Immortality Drug

If we view the 12 hallmarks as a network (see [08-frontiers/04-ai-drugs/03-multi-target.md](../08-frontiers/04-ai-drugs/03-multi-target.md)), single-target interventions have three structural shortcomings:

1. **Network buffering**: Inhibiting one pathway (such as mTOR) leaves other pathways (epigenetic drift, senescent-cell accumulation, stem-cell exhaustion) to continue progressing, ultimately still ending in age-related disease. Rapamycin extends mouse lifespan by 9–14%, far less than the benefit of eliminating all senescent cells, which illustrates this point.
2. **Dose-toxicity paradox**: Producing a significant lifespan-extending effect with a single target often requires high-intensity, long-duration exposure, bringing with it immunosuppression (rapamycin), cancer risk (the IGF-1 pathway), metabolic dysregulation, and more. Multi-target approaches can dilute toxicity by using “low doses at each target, with multiple targets acting synergistically.”
3. **Heterogeneity problem**: Different organs age at different rates (“organ clocks within the same body are not synchronized”), and a single target cannot address this spatiotemporal heterogeneity.

### 7. Core Insight Two: Assessing the “Distance from an Immortality Drug” for Five Candidate Approaches

Ranking all anti-aging approaches in this knowledge base along three dimensions—“strength of evidence × reversal capacity × systemic scope”—yields a five-tier ladder (for detailed mechanisms and evidence, see the corresponding entries):

| Tier | Approach | Reversal Capacity | Systemic Scope | Strength of Evidence | Knowledge Base Entry |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1 (closest)** | Epigenetic reprogramming (OSK) | **Reversible** (the only approach with a measured reversal of biological age) | Systemic (reversal across multiple tissues) | Conclusive animal evidence; first human trial initiated (2026) | [08-frontiers/01-reprogramming/](../08-frontiers/01-reprogramming/01-concept.md) |
| **2** | Senolytics/Senomorphics | Clears/suppresses senescent cells | Semi-systemic (targets senescent cells) | Conclusive animal evidence + exploratory human Phase I/II trials | [04-drugs/03-senolytics.md](../04-drugs/03-senolytics.md) |
| **3** | NAD⁺/SIRT1 network | Partially restores metabolic-epigenetic coupling | Systemic (network-wide) | Human RCTs show metabolic benefits; evidence for lifespan extension is weak | [01-foundations/04-nad-sirtuin.md](../01-foundations/04-nad-sirtuin.md) |
| **4** | Nutrient sensing (mTOR/AMPK/metformin) | Primarily delays aging | Systemic | Conclusive cross-species evidence + human metabolic evidence | [04-drugs/06-mtor-rapamycin.md](../04-drugs/06-mtor-rapamycin.md) |
| **5** | NO pathway/lifestyle | Primarily delays aging | Vascular-immune-metabolic hub | Strongest human evidence, but limited effect size | [01-foundations/03-no-basics.md](../01-foundations/03-no-basics.md) |

**Why epigenetic reprogramming ranks first**: Because it targets “information loss,” an upstream driver (§4, the third transition). Senolytics clear cells that have already become senescent (downstream cleanup), and NAD⁺ replenishment supplies fuel (supportive), whereas reprogramming directly rewrites how the “instruction manual is read”—in theory, it can correct multiple downstream hallmarks at once (epigenetic drift → genomic stability, SASP → chronic inflammation, stem-cell function → tissue regeneration). This is also why Sinclair calls it “currently the only technological approach that has directly measured reversal of biological age at the cellular level.” But it is also the riskiest: complete reprogramming causes dedifferentiation and cancer, while the “identity-loss threshold” for partial reprogramming is extremely difficult to control (sustained expression has been shown to be lethal in multiple laboratories: Abad 2013 *Nature*, Ohnishi 2014 *Cell*, Parras 2023 *Nat Aging*); see [08-frontiers/01-reprogramming/03-safety-risks.md](../08-frontiers/01-reprogramming/03-safety-risks.md).

### 8. Core Insight Three: A Genuine Immortality Drug Must Be a “Combination Punch”—Single-Target Initiation, Multi-Target Effects

The 2026 Rentosertib study (in this knowledge base: [04-drugs/01-rentosertib.md](../04-drugs/01-rentosertib.md)) offers an intriguing paradigm: the mechanism of a truly effective anti-aging drug is often “**inhibition of a main pathway (TNIK) → triggering of a downstream, multi-node cascade (SASP↓, NAMPT↑, M1→M2, antioxidant activity↑)**”—single-target initiation, multi-target effects. This indicates that a “multi-target” approach does not necessarily require a cocktail; it can also be a single molecule that leverages a network hub.

Candidate combination strategies (ranked by evidence maturity):
1. **Senolytics + NAD⁺ precursors**: First clear senescent cells (relieving their CD38-mediated consumption of NAD⁺), then supplement NMN/NR—a direct synergy in logic (clear CD38⁺ inflammatory cells → release the NAD⁺ pool), but joint experimental data are lacking;
2. **Metformin + rapamycin**: AMPK activation + mTORC1 inhibition, “rejuvenating” simultaneously at both ends of the energy-sensing switch. The ongoing human trial (the TAME metformin anti-aging trial) will provide the first data;
3. **Epigenetic reprogramming + targeted clearance**: “Restart” first, then “clean up”—theoretically the most complete loop approaching “rejuvenation,” but both approaches are at an early stage and the safety of the combination is entirely unknown;
4. **The NO pathway as a foundational layer**: Whatever the cutting-edge intervention, the status of the vascular-immune-metabolic hub determines how efficiently its effects are transmitted—declining NO bioavailability weakens the “infrastructure” for all other interventions (see [02-vascular/01-no-cardiovascular.md](../02-vascular/01-no-cardiovascular.md)).

### 10. Key Controversies and Unknowns (An Honest Inventory)

1. **Blind spots in the statistical camp**: All statistics on lifespan limits are based on historical data from a time when “medicine treated disease, not aging.” If anti-aging medicine can truly change the rate of aging, extrapolations from historical statistics will no longer hold. This is the strongest argument for the optimistic camp, and one that cannot currently be disproven.
2. **The animal-to-human gap**: The 36% lifespan extension from senolytics and the 109% extension from reprogramming were both observed in mice (lifespan ~2 years). Mice differ greatly from humans in the rate of aging, cancer burden, and metabolic characteristics; the lifespan-extension effects seen in mice will almost certainly be much smaller in primates.
3. **Epigenetic clock ≠ lifespan**: Turning back an epigenetic clock does not mean extending lifespan—the clock is a “reading” of aging, and whether it is also a “driver” has not been established in humans; existing studies suggest that transcriptomic rejuvenation through partial reprogramming does not always coincide with functional recovery.
4. **Side effects of the goal of “immortality”**: Even if the technology is feasible, cancer is a product of the balance between aging suppression (tumor-suppressive barriers) and cell renewal—excessive “rejuvenation” may increase cancer risk; excessive clearance of senescent cells may impair wound healing and tumor suppression (the moments when “senescent cells are needed”).

## F. Frontiers and Outlook

### 11. Three Watershed Experiments That Will Determine Whether an “Immortality Drug” Is Possible (2026–2035)

1. **TAME trial results (metformin, target around 2030)**: The first large anti-aging RCT with “delaying the onset of multiple aging-related diseases” as its endpoint. Whatever the result, it will establish whether “anti-aging” can become a clinically accepted endpoint for regulators.
2. **First human case of partial reprogramming by Life Biosciences (launched in June 2026)**: Delivering OSK factors into humans (indications: ophthalmic/neurodegenerative conditions). Safety data (carcinogenesis, dedifferentiation) and preliminary efficacy data will, for the first time, answer whether “reprogramming is safe and reversible in humans.”
3. **Hard-endpoint trials of next-generation senolytics**: At present, D+Q/Fisetin have only biomarker evidence, and UBX0101 failed in phase II. If future trials achieve positive results with “disability/lifespan” as endpoints, this will, for the first time, demonstrate in humans that “clearing senescent cells ≈ delaying aging.”

### 12. Open Questions and Previews of Upcoming Topics

- **Question 1**: How much of aging is a “program,” and how much is “damage”? This determines how to allocate emphasis between reprogramming (reading the table) and repair (clearance).
- **Question 2**: How can the “synchronization risk” of systemic reprogramming be managed when organ clocks are out of sync?
- **Question 3**: The sociological questions surrounding immortality drugs—if healthspan is extended by 20 years, how will pension systems, intergenerational equity, and population structure be reconfigured? This is not a biological question, but it will determine how the technology is implemented.
